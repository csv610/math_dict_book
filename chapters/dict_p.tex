\chapter*{P}
\label{chap:p}

\term{P vs NP}
The P versus NP problem asks whether every problem whose proposed solution can be checked in polynomial time can also be solved in polynomial time. Since $P\subseteq NP$, the question is whether this inclusion is strict. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} the problem affects optimization, scheduling, automated reasoning, cryptography, and the limits of efficient computation.

\term{P-adic Number}
The $p$-adic numbers are the completion of the rationals using the distance $|x|_p=p^{-v_p(x)}$, which measures divisibility by a prime $p$ rather than ordinary size. Numbers are close when their difference is highly divisible by $p$. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} $p$-adic numbers support Diophantine equations, arithmetic geometry, local-to-global methods, and cryptography.

\term{P-adic Valuation}
The $p$-adic valuation $v_p(n)$ is the exponent of the highest power of $p$ dividing an integer, extended to rationals and with $v_p(0)=\infty$. It satisfies $v_p(x+y)\geq\min(v_p(x),v_p(y))$. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} valuations measure divisibility and support congruences, factorization, and $p$-adic analysis.

\term{Painter's Paradox}
The observation connected with Gabriel's horn: the horn can be filled with a finite volume of paint, yet covering its surface seems to require an infinite amount. Under an idealized model with paint of no thickness the two statements conflict; in practice a finite film of paint on an infinite area still uses infinite volume, and the paradox highlights the difference between surface area and volume.

\term{Paley--Wiener}
The Paley--Wiener theorem characterizes entire functions of exponential type: a function $f$ is the restriction to $\mathbb{R}$ of an entire function of exponential type $\tau$ if and only if its Fourier transform has support in $[-\tau, \tau]$. Equivalently, the Fourier transform of a compactly supported function or distribution is an entire function of exponential type growing at most like $e^{\tau |z|}$. The theorem extends to tempered distributions and underlies the theory of Paley--Wiener spaces of bandlimited signals in sampling theory and harmonic analysis.

\term{Palindromic Sequence}
A palindromic sequence reads the same from left to right and right to left. Palindromes can be studied through recursive construction, symmetry, and word combinatorics. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} they occur in string algorithms, coding, molecular sequence analysis, and pattern detection.

\term{Parabolic PDE}
A parabolic PDE has a time-like direction and diffusion-like behavior, as in the heat equation. It generally smooths irregular initial data as time evolves. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} parabolic PDEs model heat flow, diffusion, pricing, population spread, and image denoising.

\term{Paracompact}
A topological space $X$ is paracompact if every open cover of $X$ admits a locally finite refinement, meaning an open refinement in which each point lies in only finitely many members. Paracompact Hausdorff spaces admit partitions of unity subordinate to any open cover, which is essential for defining smooth partitions of unity on manifolds and for gluing local constructions. Metric spaces and CW complexes are paracompact. Paracompactness plays a central role in sheaf theory, differential topology, and the construction of integrals on manifolds.

\term{Parallelepiped}
A parallelepiped is a solid generated by three vectors, with each pair of opposite faces parallel. Its volume is $V=|\mathbf a\cdot(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c)|$. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} parallelepipeds describe unit cells in crystals, coordinate changes, and volumes in vector calculus.

\term{Parallel Transport}
Parallel transport moves a vector along a curve while making its covariant derivative along the curve zero. On a curved manifold the result can depend on the path, revealing curvature. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} parallel transport is used in differential geometry, general relativity, robotics, and gauge theory.

\term{Parameter}
A parameter is a quantity that remains fixed during one calculation but can vary between models or cases. It can also label points on a parametric curve. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} parameters describe model uncertainty, geometric families, physical constants, and statistical coefficients.

\term{Parametric Equation}
A parametric equation describes coordinates as functions of a parameter, such as $x=\cos t$ and $y=\sin t$ for a circle. It can represent motion, orientation, and curves that are not graphs of a single-valued function. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} parametric equations are used in kinematics, computer graphics, geometry, and robotics.

\term{Parity}
The parity of an integer is whether it is even or odd. A function is even when $f(-x)=f(x)$ and odd when $f(-x)=-f(x)$. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} parity simplifies symmetry arguments, permutation signs, Fourier expansions, and selection rules in physics.

\term{Parrondo's Paradox}
Two games that are each losing can be combined, played alternately or by a random rule, into a winning strategy. It arises from capital-dependent or history-dependent strategies and connects to the theory of Brownian ratchets in physics; the winning arises from the nonlinearity of the combined process.

\term{Parseval's Identity}
Parseval's identity states that the squared norm of a function equals the sum of the squared magnitudes of its Fourier coefficients, with normalization depending on convention. It expresses conservation of energy under Fourier expansion. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} it supports signal power calculations, Fourier analysis, and error estimates for approximations.

\term{Partial Derivative}
A partial derivative differentiates a multivariable function with respect to one variable while holding the others fixed. It measures change along a coordinate direction. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} partial derivatives form gradients and Jacobians and are used in optimization, sensitivity analysis, thermodynamics, and PDEs.

\term{Partial Differential Equation}
A partial differential equation (PDE) is an equation relating an unknown function $u$ of several variables to its partial derivatives, of the form $F(x, u, \partial u/\partial x_i, \partial^2 u/\partial x_i \partial x_j, \ldots) = 0$. Second-order linear equations are classified by the sign of their principal symbol as elliptic, parabolic, or hyperbolic, each with its own existence, uniqueness, and regularity theory. A problem is well-posed in the sense of Hadamard when a solution exists, is unique, and depends continuously on the data. Weak solutions and Sobolev spaces are central tools in the analysis of PDEs.

\term{Partial Fraction}
A partial-fraction decomposition writes a rational function as a sum of simpler fractions associated with the factors of its denominator. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} it simplifies integration, inverse Laplace transforms, recurrence solutions, and symbolic algebra.

\term{Partial Sum}
A partial sum is $S_n=a_1+\cdots+a_n$, the sum of the first $n$ terms of a series. A series converges when its partial sums approach a finite limit. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} partial sums provide finite approximations and are used to study convergence and numerical error.

\term{Partial Order}
A partial order is a relation that is reflexive, antisymmetric, and transitive. It allows some elements to be incomparable, unlike a total order. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} partial orders represent dependencies, task schedules, lattices, version histories, and optimization constraints.

\term{Partition (Integer)}
A way of writing a positive integer $n$ as a sum of positive integers, where the order of the summands does not matter. The partition function $p(n)$ grows rapidly and is studied using generating functions and Hardy-Ramanujan asymptotics.

\term{Path-Connected Space}
A space is path-connected if every two points can be joined by a continuous path. Path-connectedness implies connectedness, although the converse can fail. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} it describes reachable configurations, connected regions, and continuous motions in topology and robotics.

\term{Path Integral}
A formulation of quantum mechanics developed by Richard Feynman, where the probability amplitude for a particle to travel from $a$ to $b$ is the sum over all possible paths, weighted by $e^{iS/\hbar}$ where $S$ is the action.

\term{Pattern}
A regular, repeating or predictable structure, studied formally in combinatorics and formal language theory.

\term{Pearson Correlation Coefficient}
The Pearson correlation is $\rho_{XY}=\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)/(\sigma_X\sigma_Y)$ and lies between $-1$ and $1$. It measures linear association, not causation or general dependence. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} correlation summarizes data relationships and is used in regression, screening, and exploratory statistics.

\term{Perfect Number}
A positive integer that is equal to the sum of its proper positive divisors. Examples include 6, 28, and 496. All known perfect numbers are even and given by the formula $2^{p-1}(2^p - 1)$ where $2^p-1$ is a Mersenne prime.

\term{Perfect Field}
A field $K$ such that every irreducible polynomial over $K$ has distinct roots in its splitting field. All fields of characteristic 0 and all finite fields are perfect.

\term{Perfect Matching}
A perfect matching is a set of edges in which every vertex is incident to exactly one selected edge. It is also called a 1-factor. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} perfect matchings model pairings, assignment, scheduling, molecular bonding, and resource allocation.

\term{Permutation}
A permutation is a bijection from a set to itself. A finite set of $n$ elements has $n!$ permutations. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} permutations model rearrangements, schedules, symmetries, randomized algorithms, and group actions.

\term{Permutation Group}
A group whose elements are permutations of a given set and whose operation is the composition of permutations. The symmetric group $S_n$ is the group of all permutations of $n$ elements.

\term{Permutation Matrix}
A permutation matrix has exactly one $1$ in each row and column and zeros elsewhere. Multiplication by it reorders rows or columns without changing their values. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} permutation matrices represent reindexing, pivoting, graph relabeling, and sparse numerical algorithms.

\term{Perron--Frobenius Theorem}
A non-negative irreducible square matrix has a positive real eigenvalue equal to its spectral radius, with a strictly positive eigenvector; this eigenvalue is simple and dominates all others in modulus. It is the foundation for the convergence of Markov chains and for stable states in population dynamics, such as those modeled by Leslie matrices.

\term{Phase Space}
Phase space is the set of all possible states of a system. In classical mechanics, a state usually includes position and momentum, and evolution traces a path through phase space. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} phase space supports the study of planetary motion, gases, control systems, and statistical mechanics.

\term{Phi ($\phi$) (Golden Ratio)}
The irrational number $\phi = \frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2} \approx 1.618$, which is the positive solution to $x^2 - x - 1 = 0$. It appears in the geometry of pentagons, the Fibonacci sequence, and phyllotaxis.

\term{Picard Theorem}
Picard's theorems concern the range of holomorphic functions. The little Picard theorem states that a nonconstant entire function omits at most one complex value, so every other value is attained. The great Picard theorem states that a holomorphic function in a punctured neighborhood of an essential singularity assumes every complex value infinitely often in every neighborhood of the singularity, with at most one exception. These results vastly strengthen Casorati--Weierstrass and are deep consequences of the theory of normal families; Nevanlinna theory refines them in the study of meromorphic functions.

\term{Picard--Lindelöf Theorem}
For the initial value problem $y' = f(x,y)$, $y(x_0) = y_0$, if $f$ is continuous in $x$ and Lipschitz continuous in $y$, then a unique solution exists on some interval about $x_0$. The proof typically proceeds via the contraction mapping principle applied to a space of continuous functions.

\term{Pigeonhole Principle}
The pigeonhole principle states that placing more than $m$ objects into $m$ containers forces some container to contain at least two objects. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} it proves existence results in combinatorics, number theory, hashing, scheduling, and collision analysis.

\term{Planar Graph}
A graph that can be drawn in the plane so that its edges meet only at common endpoints. Kuratowski's theorem characterizes planar graphs as those containing neither $K_5$ nor $K_{3,3}$ as a minor (equivalently, as a topological minor). A connected planar graph satisfies Euler's formula $V - E + F = 2$ relating vertices, edges, and faces. Every planar graph is 4-colorable, the four-color theorem. Planarity testing, dual graphs, and planar embeddings matter in graph drawing, network design, and algorithmic graph theory.

\term{Pointwise Convergence}
The sequence $f_n$ converges pointwise to $f$ on $S$ if $f_n(x)\to f(x)$ for each fixed $x\in S$. The rate may depend on $x$, so pointwise convergence is weaker than uniform convergence. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} it appears in approximation, probability, integration, and the study of function limits.

\term{Poisson Distribution}
The Poisson distribution models the number of independent events in a fixed interval with mean rate $\lambda$: $P(X=k)=e^{-\lambda}\lambda^k/k!$. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} it models arrivals, defects, mutations, calls, and other count data.

\term{Poisson Equation}
A partial differential equation of the form $\Delta u = f$, where $\Delta$ is the Laplacian. It describes the potential $u$ generated by a source density $f$, appearing in electrostatics and gravity.

\term{Poisson Process}
A Poisson process $N(t)$ counts events whose independent increments have Poisson distributions proportional to interval length. Its waiting times are independent exponential variables. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} it models queues, radioactive decay, insurance claims, network traffic, and random arrivals.

\term{Poisson Summation Formula}
A relationship between the sum of values of a function at integers and the sum of values of its Fourier transform at integers: $\sum_{n=-\infty}^\infty f(n) = \sum_{k=-\infty}^\infty \hat{f}(k)$.

\term{Polytope}
A polytope is a finite-dimensional generalization of a polygon and polyhedron. A convex polytope is the convex hull of finitely many points, or equivalently a bounded intersection of finitely many half-spaces. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} polytopes describe feasible regions in linear programming, geometry, combinatorics, and computer graphics.

\term{Polyhedron}
In Euclidean geometry, a three-dimensional solid bounded by flat polygonal faces, the higher-dimensional analogue of a polygon. More generally a polyhedron is a union of finitely many convex polytopes in $\mathbb{R}^n$. Convex polyhedra are studied through the theory of polytopes, with Euler's formula $V - E + F = 2$ for convex solids relating vertices, edges, and faces. Regular and vertex-transitive polyhedra include the five Platonic solids, the classification of which dates to antiquity and reappears in symmetry and group theory.

\term{Polynomial}
A polynomial is a finite sum of terms involving coefficients, variables, and nonnegative integer powers. Its degree controls growth and algebraic complexity. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} polynomials model data, approximate functions, define algebraic varieties, and support symbolic and numerical computation.

\term{Polynomial Ring}
The ring $R[x]$ consisting of all polynomials with coefficients in a ring $R$. If $R$ is a field, $R[x]$ is a Euclidean domain.

\term{Polar Coordinates}
Polar coordinates represent a planar point by radius $r$ and angle $\theta$, with $x=r\cos\theta$ and $y=r\sin\theta$. The area element is $r\,dr\,d\theta$. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} polar coordinates simplify circular geometry, wave equations, orbital motion, and double integrals.

\term{Polar Decomposition}
The factorization of a complex square matrix $A$ as $A = UP$, where $U$ is a unitary matrix and $P$ is a positive semi-definite Hermitian matrix.

\term{Pontryagin Duality}
A duality between a locally compact abelian (LCA) group $G$ and its dual group $\widehat{G}$, which consists of all continuous characters of $G$. The double dual is canonically isomorphic to $G$.

\term{Predicate Logic}
Also called first-order logic or first-order predicate calculus. It extends propositional logic by introducing quantifiers ($\forall$, $\exists$) and predicates applied to variables. Predicate logic is the standard formal system for expressing mathematical statements and proofs.

\term{Poincaré Disk Model}
A model of hyperbolic geometry where the hyperbolic plane is represented by the interior of the unit disk in $\mathbb{R}^2$. Geodesics are arcs of circles orthogonal to the boundary.

\term{Poincaré Recurrence Theorem}
A theorem in dynamical systems stating that for a measure-preserving transformation on a finite-measure space, almost every point in any set $A$ will eventually return to $A$ infinitely often.

\term{Poincaré Conjecture}
A famous theorem stating that every simply connected, closed 3-manifold is homeomorphic to the 3-sphere. It was proven by Grigori Perelman (2003) using Ricci flow.

\term{Poincaré Duality}
A fundamental result in manifold topology: for a compact oriented $n$-manifold $M$, there is an isomorphism $H^k(M; \mathbb{Z}) \cong H_{n-k}(M; \mathbb{Z})$.

\term{Poincaré--Hopf Theorem}
For a smooth vector field $V$ on a compact manifold $M$ with isolated zeros, the sum of the indices of the zeros equals the Euler characteristic $\chi(M)$.

\term{Population}
The entire set of objects of interest in a statistical study, from which samples are drawn.

\term{Power Set}
The set of all subsets of a set $S$, denoted $\mathcal{P}(S)$ or $2^S$. By Cantor's theorem, the cardinality of the power set is strictly greater than the cardinality of $S$.

\term{Power Series}
An infinite series of the form $\sum_{n=0}^\infty a_n (x-c)^n$. Each power series has a radius of convergence $R$, within which the series converges absolutely.

\term{Preconditioner}
A matrix or operator that transforms a linear system into one with better conditioning for iterative solvers.

\term{Preconditioning}
The process of transforming a linear system $Ax = b$ into an equivalent system $M^{-1}Ax = M^{-1}b$ to reduce its condition number. A good preconditioner $M$ approximates $A$ but is easy to invert, thereby accelerating the convergence of iterative solvers like the conjugate gradient method.

\term{Preimage}
For a function $f: X \to Y$ and a subset $B \subseteq Y$, the preimage $f^{-1}(B)$ is the set of all $x \in X$ such that $f(x) \in B$.

\term{Pre-order}
A binary relation that is reflexive and transitive, but not necessarily antisymmetric. A partial order is a pre-order that is also antisymmetric.

\term{Presheaf}
An assignment of data to open sets together with restriction maps, compatible with inclusions of open sets.

\term{Primal Problem}
The original optimization problem, to which a dual problem is associated.

\term{Primality Test}
An algorithm deciding whether a given integer $n$ is prime. Trial division is simple but exponential in the number of digits; probabilistic tests such as Miller--Rabin and Solovay--Strassen determine primality quickly with small error probability. The AKS algorithm, announced in 2002, gives the first deterministic polynomial-time primality test, unconditionally proving primality in time polynomial in $\log n$. Primality testing is distinct from integer factorization and is central to public-key cryptography, where large primes are generated for RSA and related schemes.

\term{Prime Ideal}
An ideal $P$ such that $ab \in P$ implies $a \in P$ or $b \in P$; the quotient ring is an integral domain.

\term{Prime Number Theorem}
The number $\pi(x)$ of primes at most $x$ is asymptotically $\pi(x) \sim x / \ln x$. An equivalent statement is that $\pi(x)$ is asymptotic to the logarithmic integral $\operatorname{li}(x)$, which approximates $\pi(x)$ even more closely. The size of the error term depends on the zeros of the Riemann zeta function, and the Riemann hypothesis would imply a sharp square-root error bound.

\term{Principal Ideal}
An ideal $I$ in a ring $R$ that is generated by a single element $a$: $I = (a) = \{ra : r \in R\}$.

\term{Principal Component Analysis (PCA)}
A statistical procedure that uses an orthogonal transformation to convert a set of observations of possibly correlated variables into a set of values of linearly uncorrelated variables called principal components.

\term{Principal Fiber Bundle}
A fiber bundle $\pi: P \to B$ together with a Lie group $G$ that acts freely and transitively on each fiber. These are used to define gauge theories in physics.

\term{Principal Ideal Domain (PID)}
An integral domain in which every ideal is a principal ideal. Examples include the ring of integers $\mathbb{Z}$ and the ring of polynomials $F[x]$ over a field $F$.

\term{Principal Value (Cauchy)}al
The Cauchy principal value is a method for assigning a value to certain divergent integrals, such as $\int_{-a}^a \frac{1}{x}\,dx$, by taking a symmetric limit around the singularity.

\term{Probability Density Function}
The function $f$ such that $P(a \leq X \leq b) = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx$ for a continuous random variable $X$.

\term{Probability Space}
A mathematical triple $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)$ consisting of a sample space $\Omega$, a $\sigma$-algebra $\mathcal{F}$ of events, and a probability measure $P$.

\term{Product Measure}
Given measure spaces $(X, \mathcal{A}, \mu)$ and $(Y, \mathcal{B}, \nu)$, the product measure $\mu \times \nu$ on $X \times Y$ is constructed from the outer measure generated by the measurable rectangles $A \times B$. Fubini and Tonelli theorems justify interchanging integrals over the factors. In probability, product measures model independent random variables, and the Kolmogorov extension theorem constructs infinite product measures for arbitrary collections of random variables, underlying stochastic processes.

\term{Product Space}
The Cartesian product of two or more sets, spaces, or groups, equipped with the product structure (e.g., the product topology or product measure).

\term{Product Topology}
The coarsest topology on a product $\prod X_i$ such that all projection maps $\pi_i$ are continuous. A basis for this topology consists of products of open sets, where all but finitely many are the whole space $X_i$.

\term{Projection Operator}
A linear operator $P: V \to V$ such that $P^2 = P$. It decomposes the space $V$ into the direct sum of the image of $P$ and the kernel of $P$.

\term{Projective Geometry}
The study of geometric properties that are invariant under projective transformations. It extends Euclidean geometry by adding "points at infinity."

\term{Projective Module}
An $R$-module $P$ is projective if the functor $\operatorname{Hom}_R(P, -)$ is exact, meaning every epimorphism $M \to N$ induces a surjection $\operatorname{Hom}_R(P, M) \to \operatorname{Hom}_R(P, N)$. Equivalently, $P$ is a direct summand of a free module, or the image of an idempotent endomorphism of a free module. Projective modules give projective resolutions and the derived functor Ext; over a commutative ring, Bass's theorem characterizes the projective generation of modules, and the Serre--Swan correspondence relates finitely generated projective modules over the ring of smooth functions on a manifold to vector bundles.

\term{Projective Scheme}
A scheme of the form $\operatorname{Proj}(S)$ for a graded ring $S$, equivalently a scheme admitting an ample line bundle, which yields a closed immersion into projective space $\mathbb{P}^n$. Finitely generated graded algebras over a field give projective varieties. Cohomology of coherent sheaves on projective schemes is finite-dimensional, and Serre vanishing and the GAGA correspondence are central results.

\term{Projective Space}
The set of all lines passing through the origin in a vector space $\mathbb{R}^{n+1}$. It is denoted $\mathbb{P}^n(\mathbb{R})$ and can be viewed as the quotient of $\mathbb{R}^{n+1} \setminus \{0\}$ by the equivalence $v \sim \lambda v$.

\term{Projective Variety}
The set of common zeros of a collection of homogeneous polynomials in a projective space. Projective varieties are the central objects of study in classical algebraic geometry.

\term{Proper Map}
A continuous map $f: X \to Y$ between topological spaces is proper if the preimage of every compact set in $Y$ is compact in $X$.

\term{Primitive Root}
An integer $g$ is a primitive root modulo $n$ if every integer $a$ coprime to $n$ is congruent to a power of $g$ modulo $n$. This means $g$ generates the multiplicative group $(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^\times$.

\term{Primitive Element Theorem}
A theorem in field theory stating that every finite separable extension $L/K$ is a simple extension, meaning $L = K(\alpha)$ for some $\alpha \in L$.

\term{Proportionality}
A relationship between two quantities where their ratio remains constant. If $y$ is proportional to $x$, then $y = kx$ for some constant $k$.

\term{Proximal Operator}
The map $\operatorname{prox}_f(x) = \arg\min_y (f(y) + \frac{1}{2}\|x-y\|^2)$, central to proximal methods in optimization.

\term{Pullback}
The map $f^*$ induced by a function $f$, sending objects on the codomain to objects on the domain, such as differential forms or bundles.

\term{Purely Imaginary}
A complex number of the form $bi$ with real part zero.

\term{Pushforward}
The map $f_*$ induced by a function $f$, pushing vectors from the tangent space of the domain to that of the codomain.

\term{Pushout}
The colimit of a diagram $B \leftarrow A \rightarrow C$, the result of gluing $B$ and $C$ along the maps from $A$, equivalently the coproduct of $B$ and $C$ over $A$. For topological spaces, pushouts are quotients of disjoint unions, as in adjunction spaces. Pushout squares and homotopy pushouts are central in homotopy theory, and pushouts are dual to pullbacks.
\term{Poisson Bracket}
A bilinear operation on functions on a symplectic manifold, $\{f, g\} = \sum_i \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial q_i}\frac{\partial g}{\partial p_i} - \frac{\partial f}{\partial p_i}\frac{\partial g}{\partial q_i}\right)$, satisfying skew-symmetry, the Jacobi identity, and acting as a derivation. In Hamiltonian mechanics, the time evolution of any observable $f$ is $\dot{f} = \{f, H\}$, and canonical transformations are exactly those preserving the Poisson bracket.

\term{Paradox}
A statement or argument that leads to a contradiction or a situation that defies intuition. Mathematical paradoxes often reveal limitations in naive reasoning or exposed flaws in foundational assumptions. Famous examples include Russell's paradox, the Banach--Tarski paradox, and Zeno's paradoxes.

\term{Positive-Definite Matrix}
A symmetric (or Hermitian) matrix $A$ is positive definite if $x^T A x > 0$ for all non-zero vectors $x$ (or $x^* A x > 0$ in the complex case). Equivalent conditions include: all eigenvalues are strictly positive, all leading principal minors are positive (Sylvester's criterion), and $A$ can be factored as $A = L L^T$ with $L$ lower triangular with positive diagonal entries. Positive-definite matrices arise in optimization (strict convexity), statistics (covariance matrices), and numerical analysis (preconditioners).
