\chapter*{G}
\label{chap:g}

\term{Articulation Point}
See Cut Vertex. An articulation point or cut vertex is a vertex whose removal increases the number of connected components. \par\noindent Applications: it identifies single points of failure in communication, transport, and dependency networks.

\term{Canonical Transformation}
A change of coordinates in phase space that preserves the form of Hamilton's equations. In symplectic geometry, it is a diffeomorphism $\phi$ satisfying $\phi^*\omega=\omega$, where $\omega=\sum dq_i\wedge dp_i$. \par\noindent Applications: canonical transformations simplify mechanics problems, identify conserved quantities, and connect different Hamiltonian descriptions.

\term{Gabriel's Horn}
Gabriel's horn is a trumpet-shaped surface with finite volume but infinite surface area. It can hold a finite amount of paint, yet covering its entire outside would require infinitely much paint. \par\noindent Applications: it illustrates surprising behavior of infinite processes, improper integrals, and the difference between volume and area.

\term{GAGA}
GAGA (``Géométrie Algébrique et Géométrie Analytique'') compares algebraic geometry over the complex numbers with complex analytic geometry. For a complex projective variety, many algebraic and analytic objects correspond, and their cohomology agrees. \par\noindent Applications: this bridge lets methods from complex analysis and topology be used to study polynomial equations and algebraic varieties.

\term{Galois Extension}
A field extension $L/K$ is Galois when it is algebraic, normal, and separable. Informally, every polynomial over $K$ that has one root in $L$ has all of its roots in $L$, and the roots are related by automorphisms that fix $K$. \par\noindent Applications: Galois extensions turn questions about polynomial equations into questions about symmetry groups.

\term{Galois Group}
The Galois group is the collection of all ways to rearrange the roots of a polynomial while preserving every arithmetic rule. These rearrangements form a group and reveal the hidden symmetries of the equation. \par\noindent Applications: Galois groups explain when polynomial equations can be solved using ordinary roots and connect equations with symmetry.

\term{Galois Representation}
A Galois representation records the symmetries of algebraic numbers as matrices acting on a vector space. It translates complicated rearrangements of roots into calculations with linear transformations. \par\noindent Applications: Galois representations connect polynomial equations, elliptic curves, number theory, and the Langlands program.

\term{Galois Theory}
Galois theory studies polynomial equations by studying the symmetries of their roots. It creates a dictionary between intermediate number systems and groups of root rearrangements. \par\noindent Applications: it determines whether equations can be solved by radicals and links algebra, number theory, and geometry.

\term{Game Theory}
Game theory studies situations in which each person's best choice depends on what others choose. It describes players, possible actions, and the rewards or costs that result. \par\noindent Applications: it models markets, auctions, voting, animal behavior, negotiation, cybersecurity, and competing algorithms.

\term{Gamma Function}
For $\operatorname{Re}(s)>0$, the Gamma function is
\[
\Gamma(s)=\int_0^\infty t^{s-1}e^{-t}\,dt.
\]
It extends the factorial to non-integer values because $\Gamma(n+1)=n!$ for non-negative integers. \par\noindent Applications: it appears in probability distributions, statistics, differential equations, and mathematical physics.

\term{Gâteaux Derivative}
The Gâteaux derivative of $F$ at $x$ in the direction $h$ is
\[
\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{F(x+th)-F(x)}{t},
\]
when the limit exists. It tests change along one direction and is weaker than the Fréchet derivative, which requires one approximation to work uniformly in all directions. \par\noindent Applications: it is used in variational calculus, optimization, and partial differential equations.

\term{Gauge Theory}
Gauge theory studies physical fields whose descriptions can change without changing the underlying physical state. The freedom to choose a description creates equations that capture forces and interactions. \par\noindent Applications: gauge theory describes electromagnetism, the strong and weak forces, particle physics, and important ideas in geometry.

\term{Gauss--Bonnet Theorem}
The Gauss--Bonnet theorem connects the total bending of a closed surface with its overall pattern of holes. For a sphere the total curvature is positive; for a flat surface it is zero, and the exact total is determined by the Euler characteristic. \par\noindent Applications: it links geometry and topology and helps analyze surfaces, maps, shells, and curved spaces.

\term{Gaussian Curvature}
Gaussian curvature measures how a surface bends in two perpendicular directions at once. A sphere has positive curvature, a flat sheet has zero curvature, and a saddle has negative curvature. \par\noindent Applications: it describes surfaces, maps, shells, general relativity, computer graphics, and shape analysis.

\term{Gaussian Distribution (Normal Distribution)}
The Gaussian or normal distribution is the familiar bell-shaped probability curve. Values near the average are common, while values far from it are progressively rarer. \par\noindent Applications: it models measurement errors, noise, heights, test scores, regression errors, and averages of many small effects.

\term{Gaussian Elimination}
Gaussian elimination solves a system of linear equations by changing it step by step into a simpler equivalent system. Once the system is triangular, the unknowns can be found starting with the last one. \par\noindent Applications: it is used in engineering, simulations, statistics, computer graphics, and scientific computing.

\term{Gaussian Integers}
Gaussian integers are complex numbers whose real and imaginary parts are integers, such as $3+2i$. They allow ordinary ideas about divisibility and primes to be studied in the complex plane. \par\noindent Applications: they help solve sums-of-squares problems, study lattice points, and develop number-theoretic algorithms.

\term{Gaussian Process}
A Gaussian process is a model for an unknown function in which any finite collection of values follows a bell-shaped joint distribution. Instead of choosing one fixed formula, it assigns likely shapes and updates them as observations arrive. \par\noindent Applications: it supports prediction, interpolation, optimization, time-series modeling, and machine learning.

\term{Gaussian Quadrature}
A numerical integration method that chooses sample points (nodes) and weights so that polynomials up to a specified degree are integrated exactly. For example, Gauss--Legendre quadrature uses roots of Legendre polynomials on a finite interval. \par\noindent Applications: it estimates difficult integrals in engineering, physics, probability, and finite-element computations with relatively few function evaluations.

\term{Gauss's Law (Electrostatics)}
Gauss's law says that the total electric field flowing through a closed surface depends only on the charge inside it. Charges outside the surface send as much field in as out. \par\noindent Applications: it calculates electric fields around spheres, wires, plates, and other symmetric charge arrangements.

\term{Gauss's Theorem (Divergence Theorem)}
Gauss's theorem says that the total outward flow through a closed boundary equals the total amount created inside. It converts a calculation over a volume into one over its surface. \par\noindent Applications: it models fluid flow, heat, electric fields, and conservation laws.

\term{Gelfand Triple}
A Gelfand triple, or rigged Hilbert space, is a chain $\Phi\subset H\subset\Phi^*$ in which test functions in $\Phi$ sit densely inside a Hilbert space $H$, while $\Phi^*$ contains generalized functions such as distributions. It extends ordinary inner-product methods to situations with continuous spectra and generalized eigenvectors. \par\noindent Applications: it is used in partial differential equations, quantum mechanics, and the mathematical treatment of Fourier transforms and distributions.

\term{Generalized Eigenspace}
For an eigenvalue $\lambda$, the generalized eigenspace is $\ker(A-\lambda I)^m$ for sufficiently large $m$. It contains the ordinary eigenvectors and the additional vectors needed to describe Jordan blocks when an operator is not diagonalizable. \par\noindent Applications: it describes repeated eigenvalues and helps solve systems of linear differential equations.

\term{Generator}
A generator is an element or set of elements from which all elements of a structure are produced using its allowed operations. For example, $1$ generates the additive group of integers, while one element can generate every member of a cyclic group. \par\noindent Applications: generators give compact descriptions of groups, symmetries, error-correcting codes, and algorithms.

\term{Genus}
The genus of an orientable surface is its number of handles: a sphere has genus $0$ and a torus has genus $1$. For a compact Riemann surface, it also equals the complex dimension of its space of holomorphic $1$-forms. \par\noindent Applications: genus classifies surfaces and appears in topology, algebraic curves, and mesh or shape analysis.

\term{Geodesic}
A geodesic is a curve $\gamma(t)$ whose tangent vector is parallel transported along itself, so $\nabla_{\dot\gamma}\dot\gamma=0$. Geodesics are locally shortest paths, although they need not be globally shortest. Straight lines in a plane and great circles on a sphere are examples. \par\noindent Applications: geodesics describe travel on curved surfaces, shortest paths in robotics, and the motion of freely falling objects in general relativity.

\term{Geometric Distribution}
If independent Bernoulli trials have success probability $p$, the number $X$ of trials until the first success has
\[
P(X=k)=(1-p)^{k-1}p,\qquad k=1,2,\ldots.
\]
It is a discrete model for a waiting time and has the memoryless property. \par\noindent Applications: it models repeated tests, failures before success, and the number of attempts required by a simple service or communication system.

\term{Geometric Mean}
For positive values $a_1,\ldots,a_n$, the geometric mean is
\[
\left(\prod_{i=1}^n a_i\right)^{1/n}.
\]
It is appropriate when values combine multiplicatively, such as percentage changes or ratios, rather than additively. \par\noindent Applications: it summarizes investment returns, growth rates, index numbers, and relative performance.

\term{Geometric Measure Theory}
Geometric measure theory studies geometric properties of sets via measure theory. Key objects: rectifiable sets, currents (generalised surfaces), varifolds (unoriented surfaces). Federer--Fleming deformation theorem: any integral current can be deformed into a polyhedral current. The coarea formula relates integrals over level sets to integrals over the domain. \par\noindent Applications: minimal surfaces, Plateau's problem, image processing, calculus of variations. The isoperimetric inequality: among sets of given volume, the ball minimises surface area.

\term{Geometric Series}
The geometric series $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} ar^n$ converges to $a/(1-r)$ when $|r|<1$ and diverges when $|r|\geq 1$ unless a trivial case applies. \par\noindent Applications: it models repeated discounts, compound growth, probabilities, and the accumulation of successive error terms.

\term{Geometry of Numbers}
Geometry of numbers, initiated by Minkowski, studies lattice points in convex bodies. Minkowski's theorem: a centrally symmetric convex body in $\mathbb{R}^n$ with volume $> 2^n \det(\Lambda)$ contains a non-zero lattice point of $\Lambda$. \par\noindent Applications: Minkowski's bound for class numbers, Dirichlet's unit theorem, lattice basis reduction (LLL algorithm). The successive minima $\lambda_i$ are the radii of the smallest balls containing $i$ linearly independent lattice points.

\term{Gergonne Point}
The Gergonne point is the concurrency point of the three lines joining each vertex of a triangle to the point where the incircle touches the opposite side. It is a notable triangle center obtained from tangency. \par\noindent Applications: it is used in triangle geometry and illustrates how metric information can produce important incidence points.

\term{Gibbs Measure}
The Gibbs measure gives each possible state of a physical system a probability based on its energy and temperature. Low-energy states are more likely, especially at low temperature. \par\noindent Applications: it models equilibrium in gases, magnets, materials, phase transitions, and statistical physics.

\term{Girard's Theorem}
Girard's theorem says that the area of a triangle drawn on a sphere is proportional to how much its three angles exceed $180$ degrees. On a flat surface the angles add to $180$ degrees, but on a sphere they add to more. \par\noindent Applications: it supports navigation, surveying, astronomy, and spherical geometry.

\term{Global Minimum}
A global minimum is the smallest value a function attains on its entire domain: $x^*$ is a global minimizer if $f(x^*)\leq f(x)$ for every allowed $x$. A local minimum only compares nearby points, so it need not be global. \par\noindent Applications: global minima represent best feasible designs, lowest-energy states, and optimal parameter choices.

\term{Gödel Numbering}
Gödel numbering encodes each symbol, formula, and proof as an ordinary whole number. It lets arithmetic talk about statements and proofs as objects that can themselves be counted and manipulated. \par\noindent Applications: it supports results about formal proof, self-reference, computability, and the limits of algorithms.

\term{Gödel's Completeness Theorem}
Gödel's completeness theorem says that a statement in first-order logic is provable from given rules exactly when it is true in every interpretation that satisfies those rules. Thus logical truth and formal proof agree for this system. \par\noindent Applications: it explains the reliability of formal logic and supports model theory, automated reasoning, and verification.

\term{Gödel's Incompleteness Theorems}
Gödel's incompleteness theorems show that any consistent formal system powerful enough to describe ordinary arithmetic has limits. Some true arithmetic statements cannot be proved inside the system, and the system cannot prove its own consistency. \par\noindent Applications: they clarify the limits of automated proof, axioms, computer verification, and formal foundations.

\term{Goldbach's Conjecture}
Goldbach's conjecture says that every even whole number greater than $2$ can be written as the sum of two prime numbers. It has been checked for enormous ranges, but a general proof is still unknown. \par\noindent Applications: it guides research on primes, additive number theory, computation, and patterns in integers.

\term{Golden Ratio}
The golden ratio is $\varphi=(1+\sqrt5)/2\approx1.618$, satisfying $\varphi=1+1/\varphi$. Ratios of consecutive Fibonacci numbers approach $\varphi$, and it arises naturally in pentagonal geometry. \par\noindent Applications: it is useful in recurrence analysis, geometric constructions, and examples of limiting growth ratios.

\term{Gradient}
The gradient points in the direction in which a function increases fastest. Its length tells how quickly the function rises in that direction. \par\noindent Applications: gradients guide optimization, describe slopes and forces, and train machine-learning models.

\term{Gradient Descent}
Gradient descent uses
\[
x_{k+1}=x_k-\alpha_k\nabla f(x_k)
\]
to move toward lower values of $f$. With suitable step sizes and convexity or smoothness assumptions it can converge to a minimizer; otherwise it may stop at only a local or stationary point. \par\noindent Applications: it is central to machine learning, regression, inverse problems, and engineering design.

\term{Gradient Vector}
The gradient vector is $\nabla f=(\partial f/\partial x_1,\ldots,\partial f/\partial x_n)$. It points in the direction of steepest local increase and is perpendicular to a level set of $f$. \par\noindent Applications: it measures local sensitivity and supplies the direction used by optimization methods.

\term{Graph}
A graph consists of vertices and edges that represent pairwise relationships. Edges may be directed or undirected and may carry weights such as distance, cost, or capacity. \par\noindent Applications: graphs model roads, social networks, circuits, dependencies, communication systems, and recommendation systems.

\term{Graph Coloring}
Graph coloring assigns colors to connected objects so that neighboring objects receive different colors. The goal is to use as few colors as possible. \par\noindent Applications: it schedules jobs, assigns radio frequencies, labels maps, allocates computer registers, and separates conflicting tasks.

\term{Graph Laplacian}
For a graph, the Laplacian matrix is $L=D-A$, where $D$ is the degree matrix and $A$ is the adjacency matrix. The vector $Lx$ records differences between a value at a vertex and values at its neighbors; its zero eigenvalues reflect connected components. \par\noindent Applications: graph Laplacians support clustering, network diffusion, image segmentation, and semi-supervised learning.

\term{Graph Theory}
Graph theory studies vertices and edges and the connections between them. A graph can represent a road map, a social network, a circuit, or dependencies between tasks. \par\noindent Applications: graph algorithms solve routing, scheduling, matching, network design, recommendation, and molecular-structure problems.

\term{Grassmannian}
The Grassmannian is the space of all $k$-dimensional flat directions inside an $n$-dimensional space. For example, it can be the collection of all planes through the origin in three-dimensional space. \par\noindent Applications: Grassmannians organize subspaces in geometry, linear algebra, data analysis, and the study of vector bundles.

\term{Gray Code}
A Gray code lists binary numbers so that consecutive entries differ in only one digit. This prevents several switches from changing at once when a number is increased or decreased. \par\noindent Applications: Gray codes reduce errors in sensors, analog-to-digital conversion, rotary encoders, and digital communication.

\term{Green Function}
A Green function records how a differential equation responds at one location to a point source at another. Adding these responses gives the solution for a spread-out source. \par\noindent Applications: Green functions solve heat, wave, electrostatic, diffusion, and boundary-value problems.

\term{Green's Function}
Green's function is another name for the point-source response used to solve a linear differential equation. It shows how an effect at one point reaches every other point. \par\noindent Applications: it supports heat flow, electrostatics, waves, fluid models, and quantum theory.

\term{Green's Identity}
Green's identity relates what happens inside a region to what happens on its boundary. It changes an integral involving second derivatives into simpler interior and boundary terms. \par\noindent Applications: it proves uniqueness and solves boundary-value problems for heat, potential, and wave equations.

\term{Green's Theorem}
Green's theorem says that the circulation around a closed curve equals the total turning or rotation inside the region it encloses. It converts a line calculation into an area calculation. \par\noindent Applications: it measures fluid circulation, work, flow, and rotation in the plane.

\term{Gromov--Hausdorff Distance}
The Gromov--Hausdorff distance measures how close two compact metric spaces are after allowing them to be embedded into a common metric space. Distance zero means that the spaces are isometric. \par\noindent Applications: it compares shapes and geometric data when the spaces do not come with a fixed coordinate system.

\term{Grothendieck Duality}
Grothendieck duality generalizes Serre duality and integration by parts to maps between algebraic or geometric spaces. It uses a dualizing complex to relate pushforwards, cohomology, and homomorphisms of sheaves. \par\noindent Applications: it computes cohomology and extension groups in algebraic geometry, especially for proper maps.

\term{Grothendieck Group}
The Grothendieck group turns a system in which objects can be added and split into a group where subtraction is also possible. It records an object as the formal difference of simpler pieces. \par\noindent Applications: it creates algebraic versions of size, rank, Euler characteristic, and vector-bundle information.

\term{Grothendieck--Riemann--Roch}
The Grothendieck--Riemann--Roch theorem relates information measured on a geometric space to the information measured after mapping it to another space. A correction for curvature makes the two calculations agree. \par\noindent Applications: it computes Euler characteristics and links geometry, algebra, and topology.

\term{Grothendieck's Six Operations}
Grothendieck's six operations are standard ways to move mathematical information along a map: carry it forward, bring it back, combine it, or compare maps between spaces. Together they organize many duality and integration-like rules. \par\noindent Applications: they provide a common language for cohomology, algebraic geometry, topology, and sheaf theory.

\term{Grothendieck Topology}
A Grothendieck topology generalizes the idea of covering a space with open sets. It specifies which collections of maps count as enough local pieces to describe an object. \par\noindent Applications: it lets algebraic geometry use local descriptions even when there is no ordinary space of points.

\term{Group Action}
A group action of $G$ on a set $X$ assigns to each $g\in G$ a transformation of $X$ so that identity does nothing and products act in sequence: $e\cdot x=x$ and $(gh)\cdot x=g\cdot(h\cdot x)$. The orbit of $x$ is $G\cdot x=\{g\cdot x:g\in G\}$, while the stabilizer is $G_x=\{g:g\cdot x=x\}$. For finite groups, the orbit--stabilizer theorem gives $|G\cdot x|=[G:G_x]$, and Burnside's lemma counts orbits by averaging fixed points. \par\noindent Applications: group actions organize symmetries of objects, count distinct configurations, and describe permutations in geometry, chemistry, and combinatorics.

\term{Group (Algebra)}
A group is a collection of actions that can be combined, undone, and associated in any order. It is the basic mathematical language for symmetry. \par\noindent Applications: groups describe rotations, reflections, permutations, solutions of equations, patterns, and physical symmetries.

\term{Group Cohomology}
Group cohomology measures the ways a group's actions can have hidden twists or obstructions. Its first levels describe changes and extensions that cannot be removed by a simple change of description. \par\noindent Applications: it studies symmetry, group extensions, topology, number theory, and physical models.

\term{Group Homomorphism}
A map $f:G\to H$ between groups is a homomorphism if $f(ab)=f(a)f(b)$ for all $a,b\in G$. It preserves the identity and inverses automatically. \par\noindent Applications: homomorphisms transfer symmetry information, define quotient groups, and provide structure-preserving transformations and invariants.

\term{Group Representation}
A group representation turns abstract symmetries into concrete matrices acting on vectors. It lets us study a rotation or permutation using linear algebra. \par\noindent Applications: representations classify symmetries and are used in quantum mechanics, chemistry, signal analysis, and geometry.

\term{Group Theory}
Group theory studies groups, their subgroups, actions, and representations. It provides a common language for symmetry and composition of operations. \par\noindent Applications: it models molecular and crystal symmetry, permutations, coding, cryptography, and particle physics.

\term{Grunsky Coefficients}
Grunsky coefficients are numbers extracted from a complex function that does not overlap itself. They record how the function distorts pairs of points and provide tests for whether that non-overlap property holds. \par\noindent Applications: they support conformal mapping, complex analysis, and the study of shapes in the plane.

\term{Gurtler's Theorem}
Gürtler's theorem says that a complete curved space with nonpositive curvature and no repeating geodesic directions has the same broad shape as ordinary Euclidean space. Curvature conditions can therefore control the global shape. \par\noindent Applications: it supports comparison geometry, the study of spaces with nonpositive curvature, and geometric group theory.

\term{Gyrovector Space}
A gyrovector space is a vector-like system designed for hyperbolic geometry, where the rules for adding directions are altered by curvature. It gives a way to do familiar vector calculations in a curved setting. \par\noindent Applications: gyrovector spaces model hyperbolic geometry, relativistic velocity addition, navigation, and curved-space computation.

\term{Gysin Sequence}
The Gysin sequence is a chain of connected algebraic steps that relates the holes in a bundle to the holes in its base and fiber. For a sphere bundle, it includes maps joined by cup product with the Euler class. \par\noindent Applications: it computes cohomology of sphere bundles, projective bundles, links, and geometric spaces.
