\chapter*{A}
\label{chap:a}

\term{Abelian Category}
An abelian category is a mathematical setting in which maps between objects can be studied using familiar ideas such as kernels, images, and quotients. It is a generalization of the category of vector spaces: for a map $f$, one can form both its kernel and cokernel, and exact sequences record when information is neither lost nor added. Abelian groups, modules, and sheaves of abelian groups are examples. \par\noindent Applications: abelian categories provide the language of homological algebra, which studies algebraic structures arranged in chains and complexes.

\term{Abelian Group}
An abelian group is a group in which the order of combination does not matter. Its operation satisfies $ab=ba$ for all elements $a$ and $b$; for addition this reads $a+b=b+a$. The integers under addition and vectors under addition are examples. \par\noindent Applications: abelian groups model quantities that can be combined in either order, including charges, displacements, and modular arithmetic.

\term{Abel's Theorem}
Abel's theorem describes what happens to a power series near the edge of its region of convergence. If $\sum a_nz^n$ converges at a boundary point $z=r$, then its values approach the boundary sum as $z$ approaches $r$ from inside the circle of convergence. A separate theorem of Abel shows that the general fifth-degree equation cannot be solved by radicals. \par\noindent Applications: these results guide complex analysis, Fourier methods, and the study of the limits of formula-based equation solving.

\term{Abilene Paradox}
The Abilene paradox occurs when a group agrees to do something that most members privately dislike. Each person assumes that everyone else wants it, so no one expresses a different preference. It is a social paradox rather than a mathematical contradiction. \par\noindent Applications: it explains poor group decisions and motivates anonymous polling and explicit discussion of individual preferences.

\term{Absolute Continuity}
Absolute continuity is a strong form of continuity: changes over intervals with very small total length must produce a very small total change in the function. On $[a,b]$, an absolutely continuous function can be written as $f(x)=f(a)+\int_a^x g(t)\,dt$ for some integrable $g$, and then $f'=g$ almost everywhere. For measures, $\mu\ll\nu$ means every $\nu$-null set is also $\mu$-null. \par\noindent Applications: absolute continuity justifies differentiation under integration and expresses the existence of probability densities.

\term{Absolute Convergence}
Absolute convergence means that a series still converges after replacing every term by its magnitude. Thus $\sum a_n$ is absolutely convergent when $\sum|a_n|<\infty$. For example, $\sum(-1)^n/n^2$ is absolutely convergent, while $\sum(-1)^n/n$ converges but is not. Absolute convergence allows rearrangement without changing the sum. \par\noindent Applications: it gives reliable convergence tests in numerical computation, power series, and Fourier analysis.

\term{Absolutely Continuous}
Absolutely continuous means that a function or measure changes only in a controlled way, as described by \termref{Absolute Continuity}. For functions, absolute continuity is stronger than ordinary continuity and implies differentiability almost everywhere. For example, an integral $F(x)=\int_a^x g(t)\,dt$ is absolutely continuous when $g$ is integrable. \par\noindent Applications: it is used in probability densities, signal reconstruction, and the fundamental theorem of calculus.

\term{Absorbing Element}
An absorbing element is a value that takes over an operation: combining it with any other value leaves it unchanged. For multiplication, $0$ is absorbing because $0x=x0=0$ for every $x$. In a computation, an absorbing state similarly cannot be changed by later steps. \par\noindent Applications: absorbing elements model failure states, terminal states in Markov chains, and irreversible outcomes in algorithms.

\term{Abstract Algebra}
Abstract algebra studies systems by the rules governing their operations rather than by the nature of their elements. A group, ring, field, or module can therefore describe numbers, matrices, symmetries, or transformations whenever the same rules apply. \par\noindent Applications: abstract algebra underlies cryptography, coding theory, error correction, and symmetry analysis in physics.

\term{Accumulation Point}
An accumulation point is a point that can be approached by points of a set, even if it is not itself in the set. Formally, every neighborhood of $p$ contains a point of $S$ different from $p$. Thus $0$ is an accumulation point of $(0,1)$, while $2$ is not. \par\noindent Applications: accumulation points describe limits, continuity, convergence, and boundaries of geometric or data sets.

\term{Adaptive Mesh Refinement}
Adaptive mesh refinement improves a numerical grid by using small cells where the solution changes rapidly and larger cells where it is smooth. A solver estimates the local error, then refines only the regions that need more detail. \par\noindent Applications: it saves computation in simulations of fluid flow, weather, structural stress, and electromagnetic fields while preserving accuracy near sharp features.

\term{Additive Inverse}
The additive inverse of a number or vector is the value that cancels it under addition. It is written $-a$ and satisfies $a+(-a)=0$; for example, the additive inverse of $7$ is $-7$. Subtraction is addition of an additive inverse: $a-b=a+(-b)$. \par\noindent Applications: additive inverses solve equations, balance physical quantities, and compute differences in data.

\term{Adjacency Matrix}
A square matrix $A=(a_{ij})$ represents a graph when $a_{ij}$ records whether vertices $i$ and $j$ are connected, or records an edge weight. Its eigenvalues form the graph spectrum. \par\noindent Applications: adjacency matrices support network ranking, community detection, graph clustering, and connectivity analysis.

\term{Adjoint}
The adjoint of a linear map is the map that moves the action from one side of an inner product to the other. It is defined by $\langle Ax,y\rangle=\langle x,A^*y\rangle$. For a real matrix $A$, $A^*$ is $A^{\mathsf T}$; for a complex matrix, it is the conjugate transpose. \par\noindent Applications: adjoints simplify least-squares fitting, optimization, quantum mechanics, and automatic differentiation.

\term{Adjunction}
An adjunction is a precise relationship between two constructions that work in opposite directions. Written $F\dashv G$, it gives a natural one-to-one correspondence between maps $F(X)\to Y$ and maps $X\to G(Y)$. For example, forming a free group and forgetting a group structure are adjoint operations. \par\noindent Applications: adjunctions organize free constructions, optimization duality, logic, and transformations in algebra and computer science.

\term{Affine Connection}
An affine connection tells us how to compare or differentiate vectors at different points of a curved space. It defines parallel transport and geodesics, which are the curved-space versions of moving a vector unchanged and following a straight line. In coordinates, it is described by Christoffel symbols $\Gamma^k_{ij}$. \par\noindent Applications: affine connections describe motion and curvature in differential geometry and general relativity.

\term{Affine Variety}
An affine variety is the set of points satisfying one or more polynomial equations. For example, $x^2+y^2=1$ defines a circle in the plane, and $xy=0$ defines the two coordinate axes. Algebraic geometry studies these solution sets together with the polynomial functions on them. \par\noindent Applications: affine varieties model constraints in algebraic geometry, robotics, control theory, and algebraic statistics.

\term{Albanese}
The Albanese variety is a geometric object that summarizes how an algebraic variety maps into abelian varieties. For a smooth projective curve, it is the same as the curve's Jacobian, which records integrals of differential forms along paths. \par\noindent Applications: Albanese varieties connect algebraic geometry with topology, integrals, and the study of rational points.

\term{Algebra}
An algebra is a vector space whose elements can also be multiplied in a way compatible with addition and scalar multiplication. Bilinearity means $(au+bv)w=a(uw)+b(vw)$ and similarly in the other input. Matrices with ordinary addition and multiplication form an important example. \par\noindent Applications: algebras describe matrices, polynomial systems, symmetries, and models in physics and engineering.

\term{Algebraic Closure}
An algebraic closure of a field $k$ is a larger field in which every nonconstant polynomial with coefficients in $k$ has all its roots. For example, the algebraic closure of $\mathbb R$ is $\mathbb C$, because every real polynomial factors completely over the complex numbers. It is unique up to an isomorphism that preserves $k$. \par\noindent Applications: algebraic closures provide a common setting for polynomial equations, Galois theory, and algebraic geometry.

\term{Algebraic Extension}
An algebraic extension enlarges a field only by adding solutions of polynomial equations. Thus $K/F$ is algebraic when every element of $K$ satisfies a nonzero polynomial with coefficients in $F$. The extension $\mathbb Q(\sqrt2)/\mathbb Q$ is algebraic because $\sqrt2$ satisfies $x^2-2=0$. \par\noindent Applications: algebraic extensions describe how number systems grow when equations acquire new solutions.

\term{Algebraic Number}
An algebraic number is a complex number that solves a nonzero polynomial equation with rational coefficients. For example, $\sqrt2$ is algebraic because it satisfies $x^2-2=0$, whereas $\pi$ and $e$ are transcendental. Algebraic numbers include all rational numbers and are closed under addition and multiplication. \par\noindent Applications: algebraic numbers support exact computation, number theory, and the study of polynomial equations.

\term{Algebraic Structure}
An algebraic structure is a collection of objects together with specified operations and rules. For example, a group has one operation with an identity and inverses, while a ring has addition and multiplication. The rules determine which calculations are valid. \par\noindent Applications: algebraic structures provide reusable models for arithmetic, symmetry, logic, coding, and computation.

\term{Algorithm}
An algorithm is a finite list of precise steps for solving a problem or transforming input into output. For example, Euclid's algorithm repeatedly replaces $(a,b)$ by $(b,a\bmod b)$ to find a greatest common divisor. An algorithm is judged by correctness, running time, and memory use. \par\noindent Applications: algorithms power search engines, encryption, numerical simulation, data analysis, and software.

\term{Allais Paradox}
The Allais paradox is a choice pattern in which people prefer a guaranteed prize in one pair of gambles but prefer a risky option in another pair that differs only slightly. These choices violate the independence principle of expected-utility theory, which says that adding the same third outcome should not reverse a preference. \par\noindent Applications: the paradox influences models of decision-making, economics, finance, and behavioral data analysis.

\term{Almost Everywhere}
Almost everywhere means “except on a set too small to have positive measure.” A property holds almost everywhere if the points where it fails form a measure-zero set. For example, changing a function at one point does not change its Lebesgue integral, so the two functions are equal almost everywhere. \par\noindent Applications: this idea is fundamental in probability, statistics, signal processing, and differential equations.

\term{Almost Sure Convergence}
Almost sure convergence means that random variables eventually approach their limit for every outcome except possibly a probability-zero set. We write $X_n\to X$ almost surely when $P(\{\omega:X_n(\omega)\to X(\omega)\})=1$. It is stronger than convergence in probability. \par\noindent Applications: it describes the long-run behavior of estimators, averages, simulations, and stochastic processes.

\term{Alternating Group}
The alternating group $A_n$ is the group of permutations of $n$ objects that can be made using an even number of swaps. It contains half the elements of the symmetric group $S_n$, so $|A_n|=n!/2$. For $n\ge5$, $A_n$ is simple, meaning it has no nontrivial normal subgroups. \par\noindent Applications: alternating groups describe symmetry and explain limits on solving polynomial equations by radicals.

\term{Analysis}
Analysis studies quantities that vary continuously or involve limiting processes. Its basic ideas include limits, continuity, derivatives, integrals, and infinite series; its main branches include real, complex, and functional analysis. \par\noindent Applications: analysis supplies the mathematical foundations of physics, differential equations, numerical methods, probability, and signal processing.

\term{Analytic Continuation}
Analytic continuation extends an analytic function beyond the region where its original formula converges. The new function must agree with the old one wherever both are defined; when the extension exists on a connected domain, it is unique. For example, a power series for a function may continue to points outside its disk of convergence. \par\noindent Applications: analytic continuation extends the zeta function and many complex models in number theory and physics.

\term{Analytic Function}
An analytic function is a function that can be represented near every point by a convergent power series. For example, $e^x=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}x^n/n!$ is analytic everywhere. In complex analysis, analytic and holomorphic functions are equivalent notions. \par\noindent Applications: analytic functions support contour integration, conformal mapping, fluid flow, and differential equations.

\term{Annihilator}
The annihilator of a subspace is the collection of linear measurements that give zero on every vector in that subspace. For $S\subseteq V$, it is $S^\circ=\{\varphi\in V^*: \varphi(s)=0\text{ for all }s\in S\}$. Thus it records which measurements cannot detect directions in $S$. \par\noindent Applications: annihilators are used in duality, constrained optimization, control theory, and linear equations.

\term{Annulus}
An annulus is the region between two concentric circles. In the complex plane it is $\{z:r<|z|<R\}$, where $0<r<R$. Unlike a disk, an annulus has a hole, and functions on it may need Laurent series with both positive and negative powers. \par\noindent Applications: annuli model regions around holes and occur in complex analysis, potential flow, and two-dimensional field problems.

\term{Antiderivative}
An antiderivative reverses differentiation. A function $F$ is an antiderivative of $f$ when $F'=f$, so $\int f(x)\,dx=F(x)+C$. For example, an antiderivative of $2x$ is $x^2$, and the constant $C$ represents the different functions with the same derivative. \par\noindent Applications: antiderivatives calculate distance from velocity, work from force, and probability from a density.

\term{Antisymmetric Relation}
An antisymmetric relation does not allow two distinct elements to be related in both directions. Formally, $xRy$ and $yRx$ together imply $x=y$. The relation $\le$ is antisymmetric: if $a\le b$ and $b\le a$, then $a=b$. \par\noindent Applications: antisymmetry is a defining property of partial orders used in scheduling, optimization, databases, and dependency analysis.

\term{Approximation Error}
Approximation error measures how far a computed or simplified value is from the exact value. If $x$ is exact and $\hat x$ is approximate, the absolute error is $|x-\hat x|$ and the relative error is $|x-\hat x|/|x|$ when $x\ne0$. Truncation error comes from stopping a process; round-off error comes from finite precision. \par\noindent Applications: error estimates are essential in numerical simulation, engineering design, scientific computing, and reliable data analysis.

\term{Archimedean Property}
The Archimedean property says that natural numbers eventually exceed any fixed real scale. Equivalently, for positive $a$ and $b$, some $n\in\mathbb N$ satisfies $na>b$. The real numbers have this property, while non-Archimedean systems can contain infinitesimal or infinitely large elements. \par\noindent Applications: it supports the usual scale of measurement and the foundations of limits and real-number computation.

\term{Argument Principle}
The argument principle counts how many times a complex function winds around zero. If $f$ is meromorphic and nonzero on a closed curve $\gamma$, then $\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_\gamma f'(z)/f(z)\,dz=N-P$, where $N$ and $P$ count zeros and poles inside $\gamma$. \par\noindent Applications: it counts complex roots, analyzes stability of control systems, and supports the Nyquist criterion.

\term{Arrow's Impossibility Theorem}
Arrow's impossibility theorem shows that no ranking rule for three or more choices can satisfy several reasonable fairness requirements simultaneously. In particular, unrestricted preferences, unanimity, independence of irrelevant alternatives, and non-dictatorship cannot all hold at once. The theorem identifies a trade-off rather than saying that voting is useless. \par\noindent Applications: it guides the design and interpretation of elections, ranking systems, and group decisions.

\term{Articulation Point}
See Cut Vertex. An articulation point or cut vertex is a vertex whose removal increases the number of connected components. \par\noindent Applications: it identifies single points of failure in communication, transport, and dependency networks.

\term{Artinian Ring}
An Artinian ring has a finiteness property: descending chains of ideals must eventually stop. For a left Artinian ring, every chain $I_1\supseteq I_2\supseteq\cdots$ becomes constant; such a ring is also left Noetherian, and its Jacobson radical is nilpotent. \par\noindent Applications: Artinian rings make classification problems manageable and appear in representation theory and finite-dimensional algebra.

\term{Arzelà--Ascoli Theorem}
The Arzelà--Ascoli theorem explains when a family of continuous functions contains a well-behaved convergent subsequence. On a compact metric space, an equicontinuous and uniformly bounded family has a subsequence that converges uniformly to a continuous function. \par\noindent Applications: it proves that approximate solutions of differential equations converge to an actual solution and supports compactness arguments in analysis.

\term{Asymptote}
An asymptote is a line or curve that a graph approaches without necessarily reaching it. For example, $y=0$ is a horizontal asymptote of $y=1/x$ because $1/x\to0$ as $|x|\to\infty$, and $x=0$ is a vertical asymptote. \par\noindent Applications: asymptotes describe limiting behavior in calculus, model long-term trends, and help analyze rational functions and physical laws.

\term{Automorphic Form}
An automorphic form is a function that respects a discrete symmetry group. Under a transformation $\gamma$, it typically satisfies a rule such as $f(\gamma z)=(cz+d)^k f(z)$, rather than simply staying unchanged. Modular forms are an important special case. \par\noindent Applications: automorphic forms connect symmetry with number theory, Fourier expansions, cryptographic ideas, and elliptic curves.

\term{Automorphism}
An automorphism is a symmetry that maps a mathematical object to itself while preserving its structure. For a graph, it permutes the vertices without changing which pairs are connected. All automorphisms of one object form a group under composition. \par\noindent Applications: automorphisms describe symmetries of graphs, equations, geometric objects, codes, and physical systems.

\term{Axiom}
An axiom is a starting assumption of a mathematical system. It is not proved inside that system; instead, theorems are derived from it using logic. For example, Euclidean geometry assumes a parallel postulate, while non-Euclidean geometries use a different choice. \par\noindent Applications: explicit axioms clarify the assumptions behind geometry, set theory, probability, and formal verification.

\term{Axiom of Choice}
The axiom of choice says that for any collection of nonempty sets, one can select one element from each set, even without an explicit selection rule. It is independent of the other usual Zermelo--Fraenkel axioms. For example, it guarantees a basis for every vector space, including spaces with no easily describable basis. \par\noindent Applications: it supports results about bases, compactness, and infinite products, but also permits non-measurable sets.

\term{Axiom Schema}
An axiom schema is a template that produces a separate axiom for every allowed substitution into a pattern. For example, the separation schema in set theory gives an axiom for each formula describing a property used to select elements from a set. \par\noindent Applications: axiom schemas express broad principles in first-order systems while keeping each individual axiom finite.
