\chapter*{R}
\label{chap:r}

\term{Radius of Convergence}
The distance $R$ from the center of a power series within which the series converges absolutely. For $z$ such that $|z-c| < R$, the series converges; for $|z-c| > R$, it diverges. It is often computed using the Cauchy-Hadamard formula.

\term{Radon--Nikodym Theorem}
A fundamental result in measure theory stating that for two $\sigma$-finite measures $\mu$ and $\nu$ on the same measurable space, if $\nu$ is absolutely continuous with respect to $\mu$, there exists a unique (up to $\mu$-almost everywhere) non-negative measurable function $f$ such that $\nu(A) = \int_A f \, d\mu$.

\term{Radon Transform}
An integral transform that maps a function on $\mathbb{R}^n$ to the set of its integrals over all hyperplanes. It is the mathematical basis for Computerized Tomography (CT) scans, allowing the reconstruction of an object from its projections.

\term{Radical (Ring Theory)}
The intersection of all maximal ideals of a ring, known as the Jacobson radical. More generally, the nilradical is the intersection of all prime ideals and consists of all nilpotent elements.

\term{Radical (Number Theory)}
For a positive integer $n$, the radical $\operatorname{rad}(n)$ is the product of the distinct prime numbers dividing $n$. The $abc$ conjecture relates the sum of three coprime integers to the radical of their product.

\term{Radon Measure}
A Borel measure that is finite on compact sets and regular in the sense of inner and outer approximation by compact and open sets.

\term{Ramsey's Theorem}
Any sufficiently large complete graph whose edges are colored with finitely many colors contains a monochromatic clique of any prescribed size $k$. The infinite version states that every edge-coloring of the complete graph on $\mathbb{N}$ with finitely many colors has an infinite monochromatic clique. The Ramsey numbers $R(k,l)$ are the smallest $n$ such that every red-blue coloring of $K_n$ contains a red $K_k$ or a blue $K_l$.

\term{Random}
Governed by chance rather than deterministic rules, described by probability distributions.

\term{Random Walk}
A stochastic process that describes a path consisting of a succession of random steps on some mathematical structure (such as the integer lattice $\mathbb{Z}^d$). The behavior of random walks is central to the study of diffusion and Brownian motion.

\term{Random Variable}
A measurable function $X: \Omega \to \mathbb{R}$ from a probability space to the real numbers. It assigns a numerical value to each outcome of a random experiment.

\term{Rank}
The dimension of the image of a linear map, or the size of the largest non-vanishing minor of a matrix.

\term{Rank (Linear Algebra)}
The dimension of the column space (or row space) of a matrix. It represents the maximum number of linearly independent columns or rows. The rank-nullity theorem relates the rank to the dimension of the kernel.

\term{Rank (Group Theory)}
For a free group, the rank is the cardinality of its generating set. For an abelian group, the rank is the size of the largest linearly independent subset over $\mathbb{Z}$.

\term{Rank--Nullity Theorem}
For a linear map $T: V \to W$ between finite-dimensional vector spaces, $\dim V = \operatorname{rank}(T) + \operatorname{nullity}(T)$, where the rank is the dimension of the image of $T$ and the nullity is the dimension of its kernel. As a consequence, a square matrix is invertible if and only if its rank equals its size, equivalently if and only if its kernel is trivial. The theorem follows from the first isomorphism theorem.

\term{Rational Function}
A ratio of two polynomials, $f(x) = P(x)/Q(x)$, defined where $Q(x) \neq 0$.

\term{Rational Root Theorem}
A theorem stating that if a polynomial with integer coefficients has a rational root $p/q$ (in lowest terms), then $p$ must be a divisor of the constant term and $q$ must be a divisor of the leading coefficient.

\term{Rayleigh Quotient}
For a Hermitian matrix $A$ and a non-zero vector $x$, the quotient $R(A,x) = \frac{x^* A x}{x^* x}$. The maximum and minimum values of the Rayleigh quotient are the largest and smallest eigenvalues of $A$.

\term{Ricci Curvature}
The contraction of the Riemann curvature tensor, $\operatorname{Ric}_{ab} = R^c_{\ acb}$, central to the Einstein field equations.

\term{Rice's Theorem}
Any non-trivial semantic property of partial recursive functions is undecidable, meaning the set of indices of the functions possessing the property cannot be decided by an algorithm. It generalizes the undecidability of the halting problem, which concerns the specific property of halting. It shows that essentially every interesting question about the behavior of programs is undecidable.

\term{Richard's Paradox}
A number defined as differing from the $n$-th number definable by a finite English phrase in its $n$-th decimal place. Such a number both is and is not definable by a finite phrase, a contradiction. It shows the notions of definability and enumeration are subtle and relates to diagonalization and the arithmetization of syntax in computability theory.

\term{Real Analysis}
The branch of analysis dealing with real-valued functions, sequences, limits, and measure on the real line.

\term{Real Line}
The set $\mathbb{R}$ of real numbers viewed as a one-dimensional continuum.

\term{Reciprocal}
The multiplicative inverse of a number $x$, denoted $1/x$ or $x^{-1}$, such that $x \cdot (1/x) = 1$. In $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$, an element has a reciprocal iff it is coprime to $n$.

\term{Recurrence Relation}
An equation that defines a sequence where each term is a function of the preceding terms. Examples include the Fibonacci sequence $F_n = F_{n-1} + F_{n-2}$ and the factorial recurrence $n! = n \cdot (n-1)!$.

\term{Recursive}
A definition or algorithm that refers to itself with smaller inputs, terminating at a base case.

\term{Recursive Function}
A function computable by an algorithm: it can be defined from the basic functions (zero, successor, projections) by composition, primitive recursion, and minimization. By the Church--Turing thesis this class coincides with the Turing-computable and lambda-definable functions. A set is recursive if its characteristic function is recursive, and recursively enumerable if it is the domain of a partial recursive function. The notion underlies Gödel's incompleteness theorems and the undecidability results of computability theory.

\term{Reducible Polynomial}
A polynomial that can be factored into the product of two or more non-constant polynomials over the same field. A polynomial that is not reducible is called irreducible.

\term{Reduction (Algebra)}
The process of simplifying a mathematical object or mapping it to a simpler structure, such as reducing a polynomial modulo another polynomial or reducing a ring modulo an ideal.

\term{Reflection (Geometry)}
An isometry that maps every point $P$ to a point $P'$ such that the line segment $PP'$ is perpendicularly bisected by a fixed hyperplane (the mirror).

\term{Reflexive Space}
A Banach space $X$ for which the canonical evaluation map $X \to X^{**}$, $x \mapsto \operatorname{ev}_x$, is surjective, so that $X$ is canonically isomorphic to its double dual. By the Eberlein--Šmulian theorem, $X$ is reflexive if and only if its closed unit ball is weakly compact. Hilbert spaces and $L^p$ for $1 < p < \infty$ are reflexive, whereas $L^1$, $L^\infty$, and $c_0$ are not. Reflexivity is central to weak compactness, variational methods, and the geometry of Banach spaces.

\term{Regular Expression}
A sequence of characters that defines a search pattern, primarily used in formal language theory to describe regular languages. They are equivalent to finite automata.

\term{Regular Graph}
A graph where each vertex has the same number of neighbors; that is, every vertex has the same degree. A $k$-regular graph is one where every vertex has degree $k$.

\term{Regular Language}
A language that can be recognized by a finite automaton or described by a regular expression. They form the lowest level of the Chomsky hierarchy.

\term{Regular Sequence}
In commutative algebra, a sequence of elements $a_1, \ldots, a_n$ in a ring $R$ such that $a_1$ is not a zero-divisor in $R$, and each $a_i$ is not a zero-divisor in $R/(a_1, \ldots, a_{i-1})$.

\term{Regular Space}
A topological space where for every closed set $F$ and point $x \notin F$, there exist disjoint open sets $U$ and $V$ such that $x \in U$ and $F \subseteq V$.

\term{Relation (Set Theory)}
A subset of the Cartesian product $X \times Y$ of two sets. A relation is a function if every element of $X$ is related to exactly one element of $Y$.

\term{Relatively Prime}
Two integers whose greatest common divisor is 1, also called coprime.

\term{Remainder Theorem}
A result stating that the remainder of the division of a polynomial $f(x)$ by $(x-a)$ is equal to $f(a)$.

\term{Renormalisation}
A technique for controlling infinities in quantum field theory by redefining parameters.

\term{Representation (Group Theory)}
A homomorphism $\rho: G \to \operatorname{GL}(V)$ from a group $G$ to the general linear group of a vector space $V$. It allows the study of groups via the linear algebra of matrices.

\term{Representation Theory}
The study of homomorphisms from groups, algebras, or rings into endomorphism rings, and of their modules.

\term{Residue}
The coefficient $a_{-1}$ in the Laurent expansion of a meromorphic function at a pole.

\term{Residue (Complex Analysis)}
The coefficient $a_{-1}$ of the $(z-z_0)^{-1}$ term in the Laurent series expansion of a function $f$ around a singularity $z_0$. The Residue Theorem allows for the evaluation of contour integrals via residues.

\term{Residue Theorem}
For a meromorphic function $f$ on a domain $D$ and a closed curve $\gamma$ in $D$ that does not pass through any pole of $f$, the contour integral equals $\int_\gamma f(z) \, dz = 2\pi i \sum \operatorname{Res}(f, z_k)$, summed over the poles $z_k$ inside $\gamma$. The identity assumes $\gamma$ is traversed counterclockwise; traversing it with the opposite orientation changes the sign. It is frequently used to evaluate real integrals by closing the contour in the complex plane.

\term{Resolution of Singularities}
The problem of eliminating the singular locus of an algebraic variety by a birational morphism from a smooth variety. Hironaka's theorem shows that every algebraic variety over a field of characteristic zero admits a resolution by repeated blow-ups along smooth subvarieties, with strong local and global control. In positive characteristic, existence remains open in general, and the analytic analogue for complex-analytic spaces was settled by Hironaka. Resolutions are essential to intersection theory, mixed Hodge theory, and the study of cohomology.

\term{Resultant}
A polynomial expression involving the coefficients of two polynomials that vanishes if and only if the polynomials share a common root.

\term{Retraction (Topology)}
A continuous map $r: X \to A$ from a space $X$ to a subspace $A$ such that $r(a) = a$ for all $a \in A$. A subspace $A$ is a retract of $X$ if such a map exists.

\term{Reverse Polish Notation}
A postfix notation for writing mathematical expressions where operators follow their operands. It eliminates the need for parentheses in evaluation.

\term{Riemann Mapping Theorem}
Every simply connected, proper open subset of the complex plane is conformally equivalent to the unit disk. Since conformal equivalence is transitive, any two such domains are conformally equivalent to each other, so there are no conformal invariants distinguishing them. The boundary behavior of the mapping is subtle, and extending it to the boundary requires assumptions on the regularity of the domain's boundary.

\term{Riemann Sum}
A method for approximating the definite integral of a function by summing the areas of thin rectangles. As the width of the rectangles tends to zero, the sum converges to the integral.

\term{Riemann Surface}
A one-dimensional complex manifold. They provide a natural setting for studying multi-valued functions (like $\sqrt{z}$ or $\log z$) by treating them as single-valued functions on a multi-sheeted surface.

\term{Riemann Zeta Function}
The function $\zeta(s) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty n^{-s}$, defined for $\operatorname{Re}(s) > 1$ and analytically continued to the whole complex plane. The Riemann Hypothesis conjectures that all non-trivial zeros lie on the line $\operatorname{Re}(s) = 1/2$.

\term{Riemann--Hilbert Problem}
The problem of finding a holomorphic function with a prescribed jump across a given contour. It is central to the study of integrable systems and orthogonal polynomials.

\term{Riemannian Metric}
A smooth positive definite inner product on each tangent space of a manifold, enabling the measurement of lengths, distances, and angles.

\term{Ring (Algebra)}
A set equipped with two binary operations (addition and multiplication) such that it is an abelian group under addition and a semigroup under multiplication, with multiplication distributing over addition.

\term{Ring of Integers}
The integral closure of $\mathbb{Z}$ in a number field $K$, denoted $\mathcal{O}_K$; it consists of the algebraic integers of $K$. It is a Dedekind domain, so ideals factor uniquely into products of prime ideals even though elements need not factor uniquely. Its unit group, discriminant, and ideal class group govern much of algebraic number theory, with the class group measuring the failure of unique factorization. The Gaussian integers $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ and the Eisenstein integers $\mathbb{Z}[\omega]$ are the basic examples.

\term{Riesz Representation Theorem}
A theorem stating that every continuous linear functional on a Hilbert space $H$ can be represented as an inner product with a unique vector $y \in H$: $f(x) = \langle x, y \rangle$.

\term{Riesz Space}
A vector lattice that is also a vector space over $\mathbb{R}$, where the order structure is compatible with the linear structure.

\term{Root (Polynomial)}
A value $r$ such that $f(r) = 0$ for a polynomial $f(x)$. The Fundamental Theorem of Algebra states that every non-constant complex polynomial has at least one root.

\term{Root Mean Square}
The quantity $\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum x_i^2}$, the quadratic mean of a set of numbers.

\term{Ross--Littlewood Paradox}
A supertask in which at step $n$ ten balls (numbered $10n-9$ through $10n$) are added and ball $n$ is removed. Although the urn grows by nine balls at each finite step, after infinitely many steps every ball has been removed, so the urn is empty. The resolution turns on the limit of the indicator functions being pointwise zero.

\term{Rotational Symmetry}
A property of a geometric figure that remains unchanged after a rotation by a certain angle about a fixed point.

\term{Rotation Matrix}
A square matrix $R$ that preserves lengths and orientation in $\mathbb{R}^n$. In 2D, it is given by $\begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{pmatrix}$.

\term{Rouché's Theorem}
The theorem that if $|f| < |g|$ on a closed curve, then $f$ and $f + g$ have the same number of zeros inside the curve.

\term{Rough Path Theory}
A generalization of stochastic calculus that allows for the integration of functions against paths that are too irregular for classical Ito or Stratonovich calculus.

\term{Round-off Error}
The error introduced by representing real numbers with finite precision in floating-point arithmetic.

\term{Row Echelon Form}
A matrix representation where all non-zero rows are below any rows of all zeros, and the leading coefficient of a non-zero row is to the right of the leading coefficient of the row above it.

\term{Rudin--Shapiro Sequence}
A binary sequence with a low autocorrelation, constructed recursively. It is used in the study of the distribution of polynomials with coefficients in $\{-1, 1\}$.

\term{Ruler}
A straightedge used in classical constructions; with compass, the tools of Euclidean construction.

\term{Runge--Kutta Method}
A family of iterative methods used for the numerical solution of ordinary differential equations. They provide a higher-order approximation of the solution than the simple Euler method.

\term{Runge's Theorem}
A result in complex analysis stating that any holomorphic function on a compact set $K$ can be uniformly approximated by rational functions with poles outside $K$.

\term{Russell's Paradox}
The set $R = \{x : x \notin x\}$ of sets that do not contain themselves cannot consistently be asked whether $R \in R$: each answer implies the other. Discovered by Russell in 1901, it undermined naive set theory and motivated the axioms of ZFC and Russell's theory of types. The "barber paradox" is its everyday analogue.

\term{R-module}
A module over a ring $R$. If $R$ is a field, an $R$-module is simply a vector space.

\term{R-linear map}
A map between two $R$-modules that preserves the $R$-action: $f(ax+by) = af(x) + bf(y)$ for $a, b \in R$.
