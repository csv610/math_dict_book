\label{chap:s}

\term{S-matrix (Scattering Matrix)}
In quantum mechanics, the S-matrix relates the initial and final states of a scattering process. Its entries are transition amplitudes whose squared magnitudes give probabilities. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} it describes particle collisions and scattering in quantum physics.

\term{S-adic Space}
An S-adic space is built as a limit of symbolic or digit expansions using a sequence of bases. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} S-adic spaces model tilings, symbolic dynamics, and nonperiodic sequences.

\term{Sample}
A sample is a subset of a population selected for study. Random selection helps reduce systematic bias, although sample size and design still affect accuracy. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} samples support surveys, experiments, quality control, and clinical studies.

\term{Sample Space}
The sample space $\Omega$ is the set of all possible outcomes of a random experiment. Events are subsets of $\Omega$ to which probabilities are assigned. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} sample spaces organize probability models for games, experiments, reliability, and risk.

\term{Scalar}
A scalar is a single number used to scale or measure a quantity, in contrast with a vector that has direction. In a vector space, scalars come from the underlying field. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} scalars represent mass, temperature, gains, and coefficients in scientific models.

\term{Scalar Curvature}
Scalar curvature is the trace of the Ricci curvature, $R=g^{ab}\operatorname{Ric}_{ab}$. It summarizes averaged curvature at a point. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} scalar curvature appears in general relativity, geometric flows, and curvature-based optimization.

\term{Sectional Curvature}
Sectional curvature assigns a real number to each two-dimensional tangent plane of a Riemannian manifold. It equals the Gaussian curvature of the corresponding geodesic surface. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} sectional curvature controls geodesics, comparison theorems, and geometric structure.

\term{Scalar Product}
The scalar product, or inner product, of $\mathbf u$ and $\mathbf v$ is $\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v=\sum_i u_iv_i$. It determines lengths and angles, and orthogonal vectors have product zero. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} scalar products support projections, mechanics, geometry, and machine learning.

\term{Scale Factor}
A scale factor is the number by which all lengths are multiplied in a similarity transformation. Areas then scale by its square and volumes by its cube. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} scale factors support maps, models, engineering drawings, and dimensional analysis.

\term{Scale Space}
Scale space represents an image or signal at several resolutions by progressively smoothing it, often with Gaussian filters. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} scale space helps computer vision detect edges, corners, and objects at different sizes.

\term{Schauder Basis}
A Schauder basis is a sequence $(e_n)$ in a Banach space such that every vector has a unique convergent expansion $x=\sum a_ne_n$. Unlike a Hamel basis, the expansion may be infinite. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} Schauder bases support approximation and the study of function spaces.

\term{Scheme}
A scheme is a locally ringed space locally modeled on the spectrum of a commutative ring. It generalizes algebraic varieties and retains information about nilpotent and arithmetic structure. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} schemes organize modern algebraic geometry, number theory, and moduli problems.

\term{Schröder--Bernstein Theorem}
The Schröder--Bernstein theorem states that injections $A\to B$ and $B\to A$ imply a bijection $A\to B$. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} it compares infinite sizes and supports proofs about cardinality without constructing the bijection directly.

\term{Schur Complement}
For a block matrix with invertible block $D$, the Schur complement of $D$ is $A-BD^{-1}C$. It reduces block equations to a smaller system. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} Schur complements support matrix inversion, optimization, covariance analysis, and numerical solvers.

\term{Schur's Lemma}
Schur's lemma states that an endomorphism of an irreducible representation over an algebraically closed field is a scalar multiple of the identity. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} it classifies symmetry-preserving operators in representation theory and quantum mechanics.

\term{Schur Decomposition}
The Schur decomposition writes a complex square matrix as $A=QUQ^*$, where $Q$ is unitary and $U$ is upper triangular. The diagonal of $U$ contains the eigenvalues. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} it provides a stable basis for eigenvalue computation.

\term{Schwarz Lemma}
Schwarz's lemma states that a holomorphic map from the unit disk to itself fixing zero satisfies $|f(z)|\leq|z|$ and $|f'(0)|\leq1$. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} it controls analytic maps and leads to rigidity results in complex analysis.

\term{Schwarz Space}
See \term{Schwartz Space}. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} Schwartz space supports Fourier analysis and distribution theory.

\term{Schwartz Space}
The Schwartz space $\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^n)$ consists of smooth functions whose derivatives decrease faster than every power of $1/\lVert x\rVert$ as $\lVert x\rVert\to\infty$. The Fourier transform maps this space to itself. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} it is the natural setting for Fourier analysis, signal processing, partial differential equations, and distributions.

\term{S-curve}
An S-curve is a sigmoid-shaped graph showing slow initial growth, rapid middle growth, and eventual leveling. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} S-curves model adoption, population growth, learning, and technology diffusion.

\term{Second Isomorphism Theorem}
The second isomorphism theorem states that if $H$ is a subgroup and $N$ is normal in $G$, then $HN/N\cong H/(H\cap N)$. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} it compares subgroup quotients and simplifies calculations in groups, rings, and modules.

\term{Second-order PDE}
A second-order PDE is a partial differential equation whose highest derivatives have order two. Its type is commonly elliptic, parabolic, or hyperbolic. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} second-order PDEs model diffusion, waves, elasticity, and steady-state fields.

\term{Section (Fiber Bundle)}
A section of a fiber bundle is a map $s:B\to E$ satisfying $\pi\circ s=\operatorname{id}_B$. It selects one point continuously from each fiber. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} sections represent fields, coordinates, and solutions of geometric constraints.

\term{Sector (Geometry)}
A sector is the part of a disk bounded by two radii and an arc. For angle $\theta$ in radians, its area is $\frac12r^2\theta$. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} sectors are used in geometry, surveying, mechanics, and circular design.

\term{Seifert--van Kampen Theorem}
The Seifert--van Kampen theorem computes the fundamental group of a union from the groups of two overlapping, path-connected subspaces and their intersection. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} it reduces loop calculations in complicated spaces to calculations on simpler pieces.

\term{Self-adjoint Operator}
A self-adjoint operator satisfies $T=T^*$ on a Hilbert space. Its spectrum is real, and under suitable conditions it has a spectral decomposition. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} self-adjoint operators represent observables in quantum mechanics and energy operators in PDE.

\term{Semi-algebraic Set}
A semi-algebraic set is defined by finitely many polynomial equalities and inequalities. Such sets are closed under Boolean operations and projections. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} they describe feasible regions in optimization, geometry, and real algebraic computation.

\term{Semi-continuous Function}
A function is lower semi-continuous when its value is no greater than the limiting lower values nearby; upper semi-continuity is the reverse inequality for upper limits. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} semi-continuity ensures existence of minimizers or maximizers in optimization.

\term{Semi-group}
A semigroup is a set with an associative binary operation. It need not have an identity or inverses. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} semigroups model sequential processes, formal languages, automata, and time evolution.

\term{Semi-norm}
A seminorm satisfies homogeneity and the triangle inequality but may assign zero to nonzero vectors. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} seminorms define topologies on function spaces and measure smoothness or distributions.

\term{Semidirect Product}
A semidirect product $N\rtimes H$ combines a normal group $N$ with an acting group $H$. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} semidirect products describe affine transformations, dihedral symmetries, and group extensions.

\term{Separable Space}
A separable space has a countable dense subset, such as $\mathbb Q$ in $\mathbb R$. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} separability makes approximation and analysis manageable in topology and functional analysis.

\term{Separation Axioms}
Separation axioms specify how points and closed sets can be distinguished by open sets. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} they control uniqueness, convergence, and function construction in topology.

\term{Separable Extension}
A separable extension is an algebraic extension in which every element has a minimal polynomial with distinct roots. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} separable extensions are central to Galois theory and algebraic number theory.

\term{Separating}
In topology, a family is separating when it distinguishes distinct points or sets. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} separating families embed spaces into simpler products and support approximation results.

\term{Sequence}
A sequence is an ordered list $(a_n)$ indexed by natural numbers. It converges to $L$ when its terms eventually lie arbitrarily close to $L$. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} sequences define limits, series, numerical algorithms, and discrete data.

\term{Series}
A series is the sum of the terms of a sequence, interpreted through its partial sums. It converges when those partial sums approach a finite limit. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} series represent functions, signals, solutions, and numerical approximations.

\term{Set Theory}
Set theory studies sets, membership, operations, relations, and cardinality. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} it provides foundations for mathematics and formal models of computation and structures.

\term{Sheaf}
A sheaf assigns local data to open sets and provides restriction and gluing rules that assemble compatible local data into global sections. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} sheaves organize functions, solutions, geometry, and local-to-global arguments.

\term{Sheaf Cohomology}
Sheaf cohomology measures the failure of local sections of a sheaf to assemble globally. Its groups are derived from the global-sections functor. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} it detects obstructions and supports topology, algebraic geometry, and differential geometry.

\term{Sieve Method}
A sieve method removes integers divisible by selected primes to estimate the remaining set. The sieve of Eratosthenes is the elementary example. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} sieve methods count primes and almost-primes in number theory.

\term{Sigma-Algebra}
A sigma-algebra is a collection of subsets closed under complements and countable unions. Its members are the measurable sets on which a measure is defined. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} sigma-algebras provide the foundation for probability and integration.

\term{Sigmoid Function}
A sigmoid function has an S-shaped graph, often approaching two limiting values. The logistic function is a common example. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} sigmoids model saturation and are used in population models, classification, and neural networks.

\term{Signed Measure}
A signed measure is a countably additive set function that may assign positive or negative values. It can be decomposed into positive and negative measures. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} signed measures model charge, differences of probabilities, and linear functionals in analysis.

\term{Simplex}
A simplex is the convex hull of affinely independent points; an $n$-simplex has $n+1$ vertices. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} simplices build meshes, define barycentric coordinates, and support optimization and topology.

\term{Simple Group}
A simple group has no normal subgroups other than the identity and itself. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} simple groups are the basic factors in finite-group classification.

\term{Simple Loop}
A simple loop is a closed path that does not intersect itself except at its starting and ending point. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} simple loops describe boundaries, cycles, and separation phenomena in topology.

\term{Simple Root}
A simple root is a positive root that cannot be decomposed into a sum of two positive roots. Simple roots form a basis for a root system. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} they describe Lie algebras, reflection groups, and representation theory.

\term{Simplicial Set}
A simplicial set is a combinatorial object built from abstract simplices with face and degeneracy maps satisfying compatibility rules. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} simplicial sets model spaces and homotopy types in algebraic topology and higher category theory.

\term{Simpson's Paradox}
Simpson's paradox occurs when a trend in each subgroup reverses after the data are combined. A confounding variable or unequal group sizes can cause the reversal. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} it warns against careless aggregation in statistics, medicine, and policy analysis.

\term{Singular Integral}
A singular integral is an integral operator whose kernel is singular at the integration point and is interpreted using a principal value when needed. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} singular integrals are central to harmonic analysis and regularity theory for PDEs.

\term{Singular Matrix}
A singular matrix is a square matrix that is not invertible, equivalently one with determinant zero and a nontrivial kernel. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} singularity identifies dependent equations, ill-posed systems, and loss of information.

\term{Singular Value Decomposition (SVD)}
The singular value decomposition writes $A=U\Sigma V^T$ or $U\Sigma V^*$, with orthogonal or unitary factors and nonnegative diagonal singular values. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} SVD supports least squares, compression, denoising, and principal-component analysis.

\term{Singular Point}
A singular point is a point where a geometric object fails to be smooth, often because derivatives vanish or are undefined. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} singular points identify cusps, intersections, and exceptional behavior in geometry and modeling.

\term{Singular Variety}
A singular variety is an algebraic variety containing at least one point where its tangent space is too large. The collection of such points is the singular locus. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} singular varieties motivate resolution methods and appear in algebraic geometry and physical models.

\term{Singular Value}
The singular values of $A$ are the nonnegative square roots of the eigenvalues of $A^*A$. They measure stretching along orthogonal input directions. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} singular values quantify conditioning, rank, compression, and amplification.

\term{Sine (Trigonometric)}
The sine of an angle is the opposite-to-hypotenuse ratio in a right triangle and the corresponding unit-circle $y$-coordinate. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} sine models periodic motion, waves, rotations, and signal components.

\term{Skew Lines}
Skew lines are lines in three-dimensional space that are neither parallel nor intersecting because they lie in different planes. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} skew lines occur in spatial geometry, robotics, and 3D design.

\term{Skolem's Paradox}
Skolem's paradox is the apparent tension between a countable model of set theory and that model's internal claim that uncountable sets exist. The resolution is that countability is evaluated inside the model. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} it clarifies model theory, formal semantics, and the relativity of mathematical properties.

\term{Sleeping Beauty Problem}
The Sleeping Beauty problem is a probability puzzle about updating beliefs when an experimenter creates repeated, indistinguishable awakenings. Different answers depend on how one models self-location. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} it clarifies conditional probability, sampling, and Bayesian reasoning.

\term{Simply Connected Space}
A path-connected space is simply connected when every loop can be continuously contracted to a point, equivalently when its fundamental group is trivial. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} simple connectivity supports covering-space theory, complex analysis, and classification of surfaces.

\term{Smooth Manifold}
A smooth manifold is a topological manifold with coordinate charts whose transition maps are infinitely differentiable. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} smooth manifolds provide the setting for differential geometry, mechanics, relativity, and dynamical systems.

\term{Smooth Variety}
A smooth variety is an algebraic variety whose tangent space has the expected dimension at every point. It has no algebraic singularities. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} smooth varieties support algebraic geometry, geometry of solutions, and mathematical physics.

\term{Sobolev Embedding Theorem}
The Sobolev embedding theorem gives conditions under which a Sobolev space embeds continuously into an $L^q$ space or a space of continuous functions. It converts weak derivative bounds into regularity. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} it is fundamental for existence and regularity results in PDEs.

\term{Sobolev Solution}
A Sobolev solution is a PDE solution whose function and weak derivatives belong to the required Sobolev spaces. The equation is interpreted through integrals against test functions. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} Sobolev solutions allow PDEs with rough data or nonsmooth classical solutions.

\term{Sobolev Space}
A Sobolev space $W^{k,p}$ consists of functions whose weak derivatives through order $k$ lie in $L^p$. It measures both size and smoothness. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} Sobolev spaces are the standard setting for weak PDE solutions and finite-element methods.

\term{Soluble Group}
A soluble group has a finite normal series whose successive quotient groups are abelian. Equivalently, its derived series eventually becomes trivial. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} soluble groups describe solvable symmetries and determine when polynomial equations can be solved by radicals.

\term{Solvable by Radicals}
A polynomial is solvable by radicals when its roots can be obtained from the coefficients using arithmetic operations and extraction of roots. Galois theory characterizes this by the solubility of its Galois group. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} the concept explains why quadratic, cubic, and quartic formulas exist but no general quintic formula does.

\term{Sorgenfrey Line}
The Sorgenfrey line is the real line with basis intervals $[a,b)$. It has properties different from the usual real topology, including being separable but not second-countable. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} it provides counterexamples in general topology and illustrates the role of basis choice.

\term{Span (Linear Algebra)}
The span of vectors is the set of all their finite linear combinations. A set spans a vector space when every vector can be expressed in this way. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} spans describe feasible directions, generated subspaces, interpolation, and linear models.

\term{Spectral Method}
A spectral method approximates a solution by a finite expansion in global basis functions, such as Fourier modes or Chebyshev polynomials. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} spectral methods solve smooth PDEs with high accuracy in fluid dynamics and wave propagation.

\term{Spectral Radius}
The spectral radius of a matrix is the largest absolute value of its eigenvalues. It controls powers of the matrix and convergence of series such as $\sum_{k\geq0}A^k$. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} spectral radius tests stability and convergence in iterative methods and Markov models.

\term{Spectral Sequence}
A spectral sequence is a sequence of successive algebraic approximations whose pages are linked by differentials and eventually approach a target invariant. It organizes complicated homology or cohomology calculations. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} spectral sequences are used in algebraic topology, homological algebra, and algebraic geometry.

\term{Spectral Theorem}
The spectral theorem states that a real symmetric or complex normal matrix can be diagonalized using an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors. Related operator versions use spectral measures. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} it simplifies quadratic forms, differential operators, quantum observables, and data analysis.

\term{Spectral Theory}
Spectral theory studies the spectrum and eigenstructure of linear operators, including operators on infinite-dimensional spaces. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} it analyzes stability, PDEs, vibrations, quantum mechanics, and signal frequencies.

\term{Spectrum (of an Operator)}
The spectrum of a bounded operator $T$ is the set of scalars $\lambda$ for which $T-\lambda I$ is not invertible. It extends the idea of eigenvalues to infinite-dimensional spaces. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} operator spectra describe stability, frequencies, and solvability of differential equations.

\term{Sphere}
A sphere is the set of points at a fixed distance from a center. In topology, $S^n$ is the boundary of an $(n+1)$-dimensional ball. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} spheres model particles, wave fronts, surfaces, and configuration spaces.

\term{Spherical Harmonic}
Spherical harmonics are orthogonal functions on a sphere that describe angular variation. They arise when separating variables in Laplace's equation. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} they model gravitational fields, antenna patterns, quantum orbitals, and 3D data.

\term{Spherical Geometry}
Spherical geometry studies points and great circles on a sphere. A spherical triangle has angle sum greater than $180$ degrees. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} it supports navigation, astronomy, geodesy, and global mapping.

\term{Splitting Field}
A splitting field of a polynomial over $K$ is the smallest field extension containing all its roots. It is unique up to an isomorphism that fixes $K$. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} splitting fields provide the setting for Galois groups and the study of polynomial solvability.

\term{Spanning Tree}
A spanning tree is a cycle-free subgraph containing every vertex of a connected graph. It connects all vertices with the fewest possible edges. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} spanning trees support network design, routing, clustering, and minimum-cost infrastructure.

\term{Special Linear Group (SLn)}
The special linear group $\operatorname{SL}_n$ consists of $n\times n$ matrices with determinant $1$. Its transformations preserve orientation and volume. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} it describes symmetry in geometry, differential equations, and representation theory.

\term{Special Orthogonal Group (SOn)}
The special orthogonal group $\operatorname{SO}_n$ consists of orthogonal matrices with determinant $1$, representing rotations of $\mathbb R^n$. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} it models rigid-body orientation, robotics, computer graphics, and physics.

\term{Spec (Spectrum of a Ring)}
For a commutative ring $R$, $\operatorname{Spec}(R)$ is the set of prime ideals with the Zariski topology and its natural structure sheaf. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} spectra are the local building blocks of schemes and connect algebra with geometry.

\term{Spectrum (Topology)}
A spectrum in topology is a sequence of pointed spaces linked by suspension-compatible structure maps. Spectra provide a stable setting in which generalized homology and cohomology theories can be represented. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} spectra organize stable homotopy theory, K-theory, and bordism.

\term{Sperner's Theorem}
Sperner's theorem states that the largest family of pairwise incomparable subsets of an $n$-element set has size $\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}$. The largest families are the middle-sized subsets. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} it bounds independent collections in combinatorics and information theory.

\term{Sphere Packing Problem}
The sphere-packing problem asks how densely non-overlapping spheres can fill space. In three dimensions the optimal density is $\pi/\sqrt{18}$. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} packing theory informs coding, materials science, storage, and discrete geometry.

\term{St. Petersburg Paradox}
The St. Petersburg paradox is a lottery with infinite expected monetary payoff but a small practical willingness to pay. It shows that expected value alone may not describe decisions. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} it motivates expected utility, risk modeling, and economic choice theory.

\term{Stability}
Stability means that small changes in data, initial conditions, or numerical errors produce only controlled changes in the result. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} stability is essential in dynamical systems, control, numerical computation, and physical modeling.

\term{Stable Homotopy}
Stable homotopy studies homotopy information that becomes unchanged after repeated suspension. It is naturally organized by spectra and stable homotopy groups. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} stable homotopy supports generalized cohomology theories, algebraic topology, and geometric classification.

\term{Stable Marriage Problem}
The stable marriage problem asks for a matching between two sets in which no unmatched pair would prefer each other to their assigned partners. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} stable matching algorithms allocate students, residents, jobs, and school placements.

\term{Stable Range}
The stable range is a dimension range in which algebraic structures stop changing essentially as the dimension increases. In K-theory it describes when maps between general linear groups stabilize. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} stable ranges simplify calculations in algebra, topology, and matrix groups.

\term{Standard Form}
Standard form is a conventional representation chosen to make an expression easy to read, compare, or manipulate. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} standard forms support algebraic simplification, equation solving, communication, and data normalization.

\term{State Space}
The state space is the set of all variables needed to describe a system at a given time. A point in it represents one complete state. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} state spaces support control design, simulation, filtering, and dynamical-system analysis.

\term{Stationary Process}
A stationary process has probability laws, or selected statistics, that are unchanged by shifts in time. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} stationarity underlies time-series modeling, signal processing, forecasting, and spectral analysis.

\term{Stationary Point}
A stationary point is a point where the derivative or gradient is zero. It may be a maximum, minimum, or saddle point. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} stationary points are central to optimization, equilibrium analysis, and bifurcation theory.

\term{Statistical Inference}
Statistical inference uses sample data to draw conclusions about a population or an unknown parameter. It includes estimation, confidence intervals, hypothesis tests, and Bayesian updating. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} inference supports experiments, medical studies, quality control, policy, and scientific decision-making.

\term{Statistics}
Statistics is the science of collecting, analyzing, interpreting, and presenting data. It distinguishes variation in observations from evidence for a broader pattern. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} statistics supports research, forecasting, risk assessment, public health, and business decisions.

\term{Steady State}
A steady state is a state or solution that remains constant in time. In an iterative model it is a fixed point. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} steady states describe equilibria in heat flow, chemical reactions, population models, and economics.

\term{Stochastic Calculus}
Stochastic calculus defines differentiation and integration for random processes, especially processes with irregular paths. It includes the Itô integral and Itô formula. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} it models noise-driven systems in finance, physics, biology, and control.

\term{Stochastic Differential Equation}
A stochastic differential equation includes random forcing, commonly written $dX_t=b(X_t)\,dt+\sigma(X_t)\,dW_t$. The coefficient $b$ gives drift and $\sigma$ gives noise intensity. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} SDEs model asset prices, diffusion, population dynamics, and noisy physical systems.

\term{Stochastic Integral}
A stochastic integral integrates a random process with respect to a stochastic process such as Brownian motion, using a definition such as Itô or Stratonovich integration. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} it defines stochastic differential equations and models accumulated random effects.

\term{Stochastic Process}
A stochastic process is a collection of random variables indexed by time, space, or another parameter. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} stochastic processes model queues, stock prices, weather, signals, epidemics, and particle motion.

\term{Stokes' Theorem}
Stokes' theorem states that for an oriented manifold with boundary, $\int_Md\omega=\int_{\partial M}\omega$. It relates an integral over a region to one over its boundary. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} it unifies the fundamental theorem of calculus, Green's theorem, and the divergence theorem.

\term{Stone--Cech Compactification}
The Stone--Čech compactification is the largest compact Hausdorff space containing a completely regular space densely, with every bounded continuous function extending to it. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} it studies compactification, ultrafilters, and functional analysis.

\term{Stone--Weierstrass Theorem}
The Stone--Weierstrass theorem states that a suitable subalgebra of continuous functions on a compact space is uniformly dense in all continuous functions. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} it justifies approximation by polynomials, trigonometric functions, and simpler models.

\term{Stopping Time}
A stopping time is a random time whose occurrence can be decided using only information available up to that time. It cannot depend on future observations. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} stopping times model decisions in sequential experiments, finance, queueing, and optimal stopping.

\term{Stratification}
Stratification decomposes a space into disjoint smooth pieces, called strata, that fit together in a controlled way. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} stratifications organize singular spaces in geometry, topology, and data analysis.

\term{Strong Induction}
Strong induction proves a statement for $n$ by assuming it holds for every smaller index and using that assumption to prove the case $n$. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} it is useful for recursive algorithms, factorizations, and statements depending on several earlier cases.

\term{Strong Topology}
The strong topology on a dual space is the topology of uniform convergence on bounded subsets of the original space. It is generally finer than the weak-* topology. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} strong topologies distinguish convergence modes in functional analysis and operator theory.

\term{Sturm--Liouville Problem}
A Sturm--Liouville problem is a second-order eigenvalue problem such as $-(py')'+qy=\lambda wy$ with boundary conditions. Its eigenfunctions are often orthogonal. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} it separates variables in heat, wave, and quantum equations.

\term{Subalgebra}
A subalgebra is a subset closed under the algebra's addition, multiplication, scalar operations, and any required identity. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} subalgebras provide smaller models and invariant structures in algebra, analysis, and operator theory.

\term{Subbasis}
A subbasis is a collection of sets whose finite intersections form a basis for a topology. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} subbases define product and weak topologies and simplify topological constructions.

\term{Subgradient}
A subgradient of a convex function at $x$ is a vector $g$ satisfying $f(y)\geq f(x)+g\cdot(y-x)$ for every $y$. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} subgradients enable optimization of nonsmooth objectives such as absolute-value and sparsity penalties.

\term{Subgroup}
A subgroup is a subset of a group that contains the identity and is closed under products and inverses. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} subgroups describe symmetries, constraints, stabilizers, and solution structure.

\term{Submanifold}
A submanifold is a subset of a manifold that inherits a compatible smooth manifold structure. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} submanifolds represent constraints, trajectories, surfaces, and configuration spaces.

\term{Subspace}
A subspace is a subset of a vector space closed under addition and scalar multiplication. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} subspaces represent solution sets, feasible directions, features, and invariant states.

\term{Sub-additive Function}
A sub-additive function satisfies $f(x+y)\leq f(x)+f(y)$. Combining inputs therefore costs no more than handling them separately. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} sub-additivity appears in norms, coding lengths, complexity, and resource allocation.

\term{Super-additive Function}
A super-additive function satisfies $f(x+y)\geq f(x)+f(y)$. Combining inputs gives at least the separate total. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} super-additivity models increasing returns, cooperative games, and growth processes.

\term{Super-symmetry}
Supersymmetry is a proposed physical symmetry relating bosons and fermions. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} it organizes particle models and motivates research in quantum field theory and mathematical physics.

\term{Support (of a function)}
The support of a function is the closure of the set where it is nonzero: $\operatorname{supp}(f)=\overline{\{x:f(x)\ne0\}}$. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} support identifies where signals, densities, and distributions are active.

\term{Supremum}
The supremum of a set is its least upper bound. It may belong to the set or may only be approached by elements of the set. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} suprema define limits, norms, extrema, and completeness in analysis.

\term{Surjection}
A surjection is a function whose image equals its codomain, so every target value has at least one preimage. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} surjections describe quotient maps, solutions with existence, and counting arguments.

\term{Suspension}
The suspension of a space joins two cones on it, equivalently collapsing the two ends of $X\times I$ to points. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} suspension relates homotopy groups in successive dimensions and is central to stable homotopy theory.

\term{Symmetric}
An object is symmetric when exchanging specified parts leaves its defining relation unchanged. For a matrix, symmetry means $A^T=A$. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} symmetry reduces calculations and identifies invariants in geometry, algebra, and physics.

\term{Symmetric Group (Sn)}
The symmetric group $S_n$ is the group of all permutations of $n$ elements, with order $n!$. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} it describes relabeling symmetries and underlies permutation methods, Galois theory, and combinatorics.

\term{Symmetric Matrix}
A symmetric matrix satisfies $A=A^T$. Every real symmetric matrix has real eigenvalues and an orthonormal eigenbasis. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} symmetric matrices represent quadratic forms, covariance matrices, energy models, and stable numerical problems.

\term{Symmetric Space}
A symmetric space is a Riemannian manifold with an isometry reversing geodesics through every point. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} symmetric spaces provide standard geometric models in Lie theory, relativity, and differential geometry.

\term{Symmetric Polynomial}
A symmetric polynomial is unchanged when its variables are permuted. It can be expressed using elementary symmetric polynomials. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} symmetric polynomials simplify equations, eigenvalue formulas, and invariant theory.

\term{Symmetrization}
Symmetrization replaces an object by an average over permutations or a group action so that the result has the desired symmetry. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} it constructs symmetric tensors, invariant functions, and unbiased estimators.

\term{Symplectic Geometry}
Symplectic geometry studies manifolds with a closed, nondegenerate two-form. This form encodes position-momentum structure in phase space. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} it describes Hamiltonian mechanics, dynamical systems, and geometric quantization.

\term{Symplectic Form}
A symplectic form is a closed, nondegenerate differential two-form $\omega$. It pairs tangent directions and defines Hamiltonian vector fields. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} symplectic forms model phase space and conserve geometric structure in mechanics.

\term{Symplectic Manifold}
A symplectic manifold is a smooth manifold equipped with a symplectic form, and therefore has even dimension. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} symplectic manifolds are the natural state spaces of Hamiltonian systems.

\term{Synge's Equation}
Synge's equation is a differential equation used to study the behavior and stability of geodesics. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} it supports the analysis of geodesic deviation and gravitational effects in relativity.

\term{Syntactic Category}
A syntactic category encodes a logical theory as a category whose objects represent contexts or types and whose morphisms represent provable maps. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} syntactic categories connect logic, type theory, and categorical semantics.

\term{S-system}
An S-system is a power-law differential-equation model for interacting biochemical components. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} S-systems represent metabolic networks and support parameter estimation and systems biology.

\term{Sylow Theorems}
The Sylow theorems describe subgroups whose order is the highest power of a prime $p$ dividing a finite group's order. Such subgroups exist, are conjugate, and have constrained counts. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} they restrict finite-group structure and support classification proofs.
\term{Inclusion Exclusion Principle}
The inclusion-exclusion principle counts a union by adding individual sizes, subtracting pairwise overlaps, adding triple overlaps, and continuing alternately. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} it counts arrangements, probabilities, derangements, and numbers avoiding specified divisors.
\term{Simplex Method}
The simplex method solves linear programs by moving between vertices of the feasible polytope while improving the objective. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} it supports resource allocation, scheduling, transportation, and production planning.
\term{Sieve}
A sieve method estimates or counts integers that avoid divisibility by selected primes or other forbidden patterns. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} sieve methods study primes, almost-primes, and additive number theory.
\term{Sobolev Theorem}
The Sobolev theorem gives continuous or compact embeddings of $W^{k,p}$ into suitable $L^q$ or continuous-function spaces, depending on dimension and indices. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} it converts weak derivative estimates into regularity for elliptic PDEs.
