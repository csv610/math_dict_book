\label{chap:c}

\term{Calculus}
Calculus studies change and accumulation using limits, derivatives, integrals, and infinite series. Differential calculus describes instantaneous change, while integral calculus describes accumulation and area. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} calculus models motion, growth, optimization, fluid flow, finance, and the behavior of physical systems.

\term{Calculus of Variations}
The calculus of variations finds functions that minimize or maximize quantities such as $J(y)=\int_a^bF(x,y,y')\,dx$. Critical functions satisfy the Euler--Lagrange equation $\partial F/\partial y-d(\partial F/\partial y')/dx=0$. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} it models shortest paths, elastic shapes, optimal control, fluid flow, and variational forms of partial differential equations.

\term{Coordinate Ring}
The ring of polynomial functions on an algebraic variety $V$, defined as the quotient $k[x_1, \ldots, x_n] / I(V)$, where $I(V)$ is the ideal of polynomials vanishing on $V$. The variety is recovered as $\operatorname{Spec} A$ for the coordinate ring $A$. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} coordinate rings translate geometric questions into algebraic calculations and are used in algebraic geometry, robotics, and solving polynomial systems.

\term{C*-algebra}
A C*-algebra is a complete algebra of complex-valued objects with an operation $*$ satisfying $\|a^*a\|=\|a\|^2$. For matrices, $*$ is the conjugate transpose. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} C*-algebras provide the mathematical language for observables in quantum mechanics and for operators in signal and harmonic analysis.

\term{Cantor--Bernstein Theorem}
If there is an injection from $A$ to $B$ and an injection from $B$ to $A$, then there is a bijection between $A$ and $B$. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} the theorem compares infinite sets and is useful in proofs about cardinality, data encodings, and the size of function spaces.

\term{Cantor's Paradox}
Assuming a set $V$ containing every set leads to a contradiction, because its power set would have larger cardinality while still supposedly belonging to $V$. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} the paradox motivates axioms that control set formation and underpin rigorous foundations for mathematics and computer science.

\term{Cantor's Theorem}
For every set $X$, the power set $\mathcal{P}(X)$ has strictly greater cardinality than $X$: there is no surjection $X \to \mathcal{P}(X)$. The proof is Cantor's diagonal argument, which shows that no list $x_1, x_2, \ldots$ of elements can cover $\mathcal{P}(X)$. Since $\mathbb{R}$ has the same cardinality as $\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N})$, the uncountability of the reals follows as a corollary. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} Cantor's theorem establishes limits on enumeration and is central to computability, set theory, and the analysis of infinite data.

\term{Canonical Bundle}
The canonical bundle of a smooth complex manifold $X$ of dimension $n$ is the determinant line bundle $K_X = \det T^*X = \bigwedge^n T^*X$, whose sections are holomorphic $n$-forms. Positivity or negativity of $K_X$ governs Kodaira vanishing and the minimal model program: if $K_X$ is ample, the canonical ring is finitely generated and $X$ admits a canonical model. $K_X$ is holomorphically trivial precisely when $X$ is Calabi--Yau, as for complex tori, and the Kodaira dimension measures the growth of $H^0(X, K_X^{\otimes m})$.

\term{Cardinality}
The size of a set, measured by the number of its elements; two sets have equal cardinality when a bijection exists between them. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} cardinality compares finite and infinite data structures and underlies counting, probability, and the theory of computability.

\term{Cartesian Product}
The set $A \times B = \{(a,b) : a \in A, b \in B\}$ of all ordered pairs with first coordinate in $A$ and second in $B$. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} Cartesian products build coordinate spaces, parameter spaces, databases, sample spaces, and the state spaces of dynamical systems.

\term{Casorati--Weierstrass Theorem}
If $f$ has an isolated essential singularity at $z_0$, then the image of every punctured neighbourhood of $z_0$ is dense in $\mathbb{C}$: near an essential singularity the function comes arbitrarily close to every complex value. This contrasts sharply with removable singularities, where $f$ has a finite limit, and with poles, where $|f(z)| \to \infty$. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} the theorem helps classify complex singularities and analyze complex models near exceptional points.

\term{Category}
A category consists of objects, arrows between objects, a way to compose arrows, and an identity arrow for every object. Composition must be associative. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} categories organize relationships between sets, spaces, algebraic systems, programs, and transformations without depending on the internal details of each object.

\term{Category Theory}
Category theory studies objects through the maps between them. Its basic tools are categories, functors that translate between categories, and natural transformations between functors. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} it unifies constructions across algebra, topology, geometry, logic, and computer science.

\term{Cauchy Problem}
A Cauchy problem asks for a solution of a differential equation from data prescribed on an initial curve, surface, or time slice. For a wave equation, the initial data may be the starting displacement and velocity. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} Cauchy problems model time evolution in waves, heat flow, fluid motion, and electromagnetic fields.

\term{Cauchy--Kovalevskaya Theorem}
For a first-order PDE system with analytic coefficients and analytic initial data, written as $\partial_tu_i=F_i(x,t,u,\nabla u)$, there is a unique analytic solution near the initial surface under the theorem's noncharacteristic hypotheses. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} it provides a local existence benchmark for PDE models, even though it does not guarantee global solutions or regularity for non-analytic data.

\term{Cauchy--Schwarz Inequality}
In an inner-product space, $|\langle u,v\rangle|^2\le\langle u,u\rangle\langle v,v\rangle$, with equality exactly when $u$ and $v$ are linearly dependent. It includes the familiar vector and integral inequalities. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} it bounds correlations, proves the triangle inequality, and supports least squares, statistics, and signal analysis.

\term{Cauchy Integral Theorem}
If $f$ is holomorphic on a simply connected domain $D$ and $\gamma$ is any closed curve in $D$, then $\oint_\gamma f(z)\,dz=0$. More generally, the integral is zero when $\gamma$ can be continuously shrunk to a point without leaving the domain. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} the theorem gives path independence for complex integrals and leads to the integral formula and residue theorem.

\term{Cauchy Sequence}
A sequence $(x_n)$ is Cauchy if its terms eventually become arbitrarily close to one another: for every $\epsilon>0$, there is $N$ such that $d(x_n,x_m)<\epsilon$ whenever $m,n\ge N$. A metric space is complete when every Cauchy sequence has a limit in the space; $\mathbb Q$ is not complete, but $\mathbb R$ is. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} completeness guarantees that iterative numerical approximations converge to an available answer.

\term{Cauchy's Integral Formula}
If $f$ is holomorphic on a simply connected domain $D$ and $\gamma$ is a positively oriented simple closed curve in $D$, then for any $a$ inside $\gamma$: $f(a) = \frac{1}{2\pi i} \oint_\gamma \frac{f(z)}{z-a}\,dz$. This formula shows that holomorphic functions are infinitely differentiable and completely determined by their values on any closed curve. All higher derivatives follow: $f^{(n)}(a) = \frac{n!}{2\pi i} \oint_\gamma \frac{f(z)}{(z-a)^{n+1}}\,dz$. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} the formula evaluates derivatives from boundary data and is used in contour integration, residue calculations, and complex potential theory.

\term{Cayley's Theorem}
Every group $G$ is isomorphic to a subgroup of the permutation group of its underlying set, acting by left multiplication. If $G$ has $n$ elements, it therefore embeds in $S_n$. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} the theorem turns abstract group calculations into concrete permutations, which helps with symmetry, computation, and the construction of finite examples.

\term{Cayley--Hamilton Theorem}
Every square matrix satisfies its own characteristic equation: if $p(\lambda)=\det(\lambda I-A)$, then $p(A)=0$. For a $2\times2$ matrix this reduces every higher power to a combination of $I$ and $A$. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} it computes matrix functions and helps solve systems of linear differential equations.

\term{Center (Group Theory)}
The center of a group $G$ is $Z(G) = \{g \in G : gh = hg \text{ for all } h \in G\}$. It is a normal subgroup of $G$. For a non-abelian group, the center may be trivial: $Z(S_n) = \{e\}$ for $n \geq 3$. The center measures how far a group is from being abelian. If $G/Z(G)$ is cyclic, then $G$ is abelian. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} centers help measure symmetry and classify groups, matrix algebras, and transformation systems.

\term{Central Limit Theorem}
If $X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_n$ are independent identically distributed random variables with mean $\mu$ and finite nonzero variance $\sigma^2$, then $\frac{\bar{X}_n - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}}$ converges in distribution to $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ as $n\to\infty$. This explains why normal approximations arise for sums and averages. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} the theorem supports polling, measurement error, quality control, and statistical inference.

\term{Centralizer}
The centralizer of $a$ in a group $G$ is $C_G(a)=\{g\in G:ga=ag\}$, the set of symmetries that commute with $a$. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} centralizers reveal hidden symmetry and are used to classify groups, matrices, and transformations.

\term{Chain Complex}
A sequence of abelian groups or modules $A_* = \cdots \to A_{n+1} \xrightarrow{d_{n+1}} A_n \xrightarrow{d_n} A_{n-1} \to \cdots$ with $d_n \circ d_{n+1} = 0$, so that $\operatorname{im} d_{n+1} \subseteq \ker d_n$. The homology $H_n(A_*) = \ker d_n / \operatorname{im} d_{n+1}$ measures how far the sequence is from exact. Exact sequences have vanishing homology; quasi-isomorphisms are chain maps inducing isomorphisms on homology, and the mapping cone encodes the homological features of a map. Chain complexes are the central objects of homological algebra, with complexes of sheaves arising in algebraic geometry.

\term{Chain Rule}
If $f$ and $g$ are differentiable, then $D(g\circ f)(x)=Dg(f(x))Df(x)$; in one variable, $(g\circ f)'=g'(f)f'$. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} the chain rule drives multivariable sensitivity analysis, backpropagation in machine learning, mechanics, and ordinary calculus.

\term{Chaos}
Chaos is complicated, apparently random behavior produced by a deterministic system. A key feature is sensitive dependence on initial conditions: tiny starting differences can grow rapidly. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} chaotic models describe weather, turbulence, population dynamics, encryption, and the limits of long-term prediction.

\term{Characteristic Class}
A characteristic class assigns a cohomology class to a vector bundle in a way preserved by pullbacks. Chern, Pontryagin, Stiefel--Whitney, and Euler classes are standard examples. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} characteristic classes detect twisting, obstruct global sections, and connect geometry with topology and index theory.

\term{Characteristic Function}
For a random variable $X$, its characteristic function is $\varphi_X(t)=\mathbb E[e^{itX}]$. It uniquely determines the distribution of $X$, and independent sums correspond to products of characteristic functions. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} characteristic functions simplify probability calculations, limit theorems, and the analysis of sums of random variables.

\term{Characteristic Polynomial}
The characteristic polynomial of a square matrix $A$ is $p(\lambda)=\det(\lambda I-A)$. Its roots are the eigenvalues of $A$, and the Cayley--Hamilton theorem says that $p(A)=0$. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} it helps analyze stability, matrix powers, differential equations, and vibration modes.

\term{Characteristic Variety}
For a linear partial differential operator $P$ of order $m$ on a manifold $X$ with principal symbol $p_m(x, \xi)$, the characteristic variety is the zero set $\{p_m(x, \xi) = 0\}$ in the cotangent bundle $T^*X$. Solvability and uniqueness hold away from it: Holmgren's theorem gives unique continuation of analytic Cauchy problems across non-characteristic hypersurfaces, while microlocal analysis shows that singularities of solutions propagate along the bicharacteristic flow within the characteristic variety. For a coherent $D_X$-module, the characteristic variety is the support of the associated graded module, and its involutivity (Cauchy--Kovalevskaya--Kashiwara theorem) forces dimension at least $\dim X$.

\term{Chebotarev Density Theorem}
For a finite Galois extension $L/K$ of number fields with Galois group $G$, the Chebotarev density theorem asserts that the primes of $K$ unramified in $L$ whose Frobenius automorphism lies in a fixed conjugacy class $C \subseteq G$ have natural density $|C|/|G|$. It generalises Dirichlet's theorem on primes in arithmetic progressions, which is recovered for cyclotomic extensions of $\mathbb{Q}$, and determines the distribution of primes that split completely in $L$, which have density $1/|G|$. The theorem is foundational in algebraic number theory and arithmetic geometry, underlying the Brauer--Siegel theorem and results on Galois representations.

\term{Chebyshev Polynomials}
The Chebyshev polynomials satisfy $T_n(\cos\theta)=\cos(n\theta)$ and $T_{n+1}=2xT_n-T_{n-1}$. Their special spacing of extrema makes them excellent for approximation. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} they reduce interpolation error and are used in spectral numerical methods and differential equations.

\term{Chebyshev's Inequality}
For a random variable $X$ with mean $\mu$ and finite variance $\sigma^2$, $P(|X - \mu| \geq k\sigma) \leq \frac{1}{k^2}$ for any $k>0$. This gives a distribution-free bound on tail probabilities. For any distribution, at least 75\% of values lie within two standard deviations and at least $8/9$ within three. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} it provides error and risk bounds when the full probability model is unknown.

\term{Chern Classes}
The Chern classes $c_i(E)$ are cohomology classes that measure how a complex vector bundle twists. The total class is $c(E)=1+c_1(E)+c_2(E)+\cdots$. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} Chern classes classify bundles, detect topological twisting, and appear in geometry, topology, and mathematical physics.

\term{Chern--Weil Theory}
A method for constructing characteristic classes (Chern, Pontryagin, Euler) of vector bundles using curvature forms. Given a connection $\nabla$ on a bundle $E$ with curvature $\Omega$, one forms invariant polynomials in $\Omega$ whose cohomology classes are independent of the connection. This bridges differential geometry and topology, realising topological invariants via geometric data. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} Chern--Weil theory connects local curvature measurements with global features of bundles in geometry and mathematical physics.

\term{Chi-squared (\ensuremath{\chi^2}) Distribution}
A continuous probability distribution that arises when summing the squares of $k$ independent standard normal random variables. It is widely used in hypothesis testing to determine "goodness of fit" between observed and expected data.

\term{Chi-squared (\ensuremath{\chi^2}) Test}
A statistical test used to determine if there is a significant difference between the expected frequencies and the observed frequencies in one or more categories of data.

\term{Chinese Remainder Theorem}
If the moduli $m_1,\ldots,m_k$ are pairwise coprime, the congruences $x\equiv a_i\pmod{m_i}$ have one solution modulo $m_1\cdots m_k$. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} it makes modular arithmetic faster by splitting a large computation into smaller ones and is used in number theory, cryptography, and computer arithmetic.

\term{Christoffel Symbols}
Coefficients $\Gamma^k_{ij}$ describing the Levi--Civita connection on a Riemannian manifold with metric $g_{ij}$ are $\Gamma^k_{ij}=\frac12g^{kl}(\partial_i g_{jl}+\partial_jg_{il}-\partial_lg_{ij})$. They appear in the geodesic equation $\ddot{x}^k+\Gamma^k_{ij}\dot{x}^i\dot{x}^j=0$ and in covariant differentiation. They are not tensors but transform in a controlled way under coordinate changes. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} Christoffel symbols calculate geodesics, acceleration in curved coordinates, and gravitational motion in general relativity.

\term{Church--Turing Thesis}
The thesis that every function computable by an algorithm is computable by a Turing machine, and vice versa; equivalently, that effective computability coincides with computability by the lambda calculus or by general recursive functions. It pins down the informal notion of computability in a precise mathematical form. Since it identifies an intuitive concept with a formal one, it is a thesis rather than a proved theorem, although the equivalence among the various formal models is a theorem.

\term{Class Field Theory}
The classification of abelian extensions of number fields and local fields: for a number field $K$, finite abelian extensions $L/K$ correspond to open subgroups of the idele class group, and the Galois group $G(L/K)$ is isomorphic, via the reciprocity map, to the corresponding quotient. Artin reciprocity generalizes quadratic and higher reciprocity laws; the Hilbert class field, the maximal abelian unramified extension of $K$, has Galois group isomorphic to the ideal class group. The Kronecker--Weber theorem, that every abelian extension of $\mathbb{Q}$ is cyclotomic, is the base case.

\term{Classical Solution}
A classical solution of a differential equation has the ordinary derivatives required by the equation and satisfies it point by point. This contrasts with a weak or distributional solution, which may have fewer derivatives. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} the distinction is essential when modeling physical systems and proving that approximate PDE solutions converge.

\term{Classification of Finite Simple Groups}
The complete classification, assembled over decades in tens of thousands of published pages, asserts that every finite simple group is one of: a cyclic group $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ of prime order, an alternating group $A_n$ ($n \geq 5$), a member of one of the infinite families of groups of Lie type, or one of the 26 sporadic groups culminating in the Monster. Its consequences are far-reaching: structural theorems for all finite groups, such as the Odd Order Theorem, follow from it, and the classification underpins much of modern group theory.

\term{Classification of Surfaces}
Every closed connected surface is homeomorphic to a sphere with $g$ handles (orientable case) or with $k$ crosscaps (non-orientable case). Thus a closed surface is determined up to homeomorphism by orientability and one integer, its genus. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} classification reduces complicated two-dimensional shapes to standard models in topology, geometry, and mesh analysis.

\term{Clebsch--Gordan Coefficients}
Coefficients expressing the coupling of two angular momenta in quantum mechanics. The product of two irreducible representations of $SU(2)$ with spins $j_1$ and $j_2$ decomposes as $\bigoplus_{j=|j_1-j_2|}^{j_1+j_2}j$. The coefficients $\langle j_1m_1j_2m_2\mid jm\rangle$ convert between uncoupled and coupled basis states. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} they predict how angular momenta combine in atomic, nuclear, and particle physics.

\term{Closed Graph Theorem}
The closed graph theorem says that a linear map between Banach spaces is continuous if its graph is closed. The graph is the set of pairs $(x,Tx)$. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} the theorem proves continuity of operators defined indirectly, especially in functional analysis and differential equations.

\term{Closed Map}
A function $f:X\to Y$ is closed if the image of every closed set in $X$ is closed in $Y$. A continuous bijection from a compact space to a Hausdorff space is a homeomorphism because it is closed. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} closed maps help show that feasible sets, limits, and solution sets remain well behaved under transformations.

\term{Closed Set}
A subset $C$ is closed if its complement is open; in a metric space this is equivalent to containing all its limit points. In $\mathbb R$, intervals $[a,b]$, finite sets, and the Cantor set are closed. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} closed sets describe feasible regions, solution sets of equations, and limiting configurations in analysis and geometry.

\term{Closure}
The closure of a set $S$ is the smallest closed set containing $S$; in familiar spaces it consists of $S$ together with all of its limit points. For example, the closure of $(0,1)$ is $[0,1]$. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} closure describes boundaries, limits, feasible regions, and the completion of algebraic or geometric constructions.

\term{Closure Operator}
A closure operator on a set $X$ is a function $\operatorname{cl}: \mathcal{P}(X) \to \mathcal{P}(X)$ satisfying: $A \subseteq \operatorname{cl}(A)$, $A \subseteq B \implies \operatorname{cl}(A) \subseteq \operatorname{cl}(B)$, and $\operatorname{cl}(\operatorname{cl}(A)) = \operatorname{cl}(A)$. The topological closure, the algebraic closure in field theory, and the subgroup generated by a set are all closure operators.

\term{Cluster Point (Accumulation Point)}
A point $x$ is a cluster point of a sequence if every neighborhood of $x$ contains infinitely many terms. For a set, every neighborhood of a cluster point contains a different point of the set. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} cluster points describe accumulation in data, guarantee convergent subsequences, and support compactness arguments.

\term{Coarea Formula}
A generalisation of Fubini's theorem for integrals over level sets. If $f: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ is smooth with $n \geq m$ and $g: \mathbb{R}^n \to [0,\infty)$ is integrable, then $\int_{\mathbb{R}^n} g(x) J_m f(x)\,dx = \int_{\mathbb{R}^m} \left(\int_{f^{-1}(y)} g\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-m}\right) dy$, where $J_m f$ is the Jacobian determinant of $f$. Used in geometric measure theory and the study of Sobolev functions.

\term{Coastline Paradox}
The measured length of a coastline or other irregular boundary can increase as the measuring unit becomes smaller, because new detail appears at each scale. The measured length therefore depends on resolution. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} the paradox motivates fractal models of coastlines and explains scale-dependent measurements in geography and image analysis.

\term{Cobordism}
Two closed oriented manifolds $M$ and $N$ of dimension $n$ are cobordant if there exists a compact oriented manifold $W$ of dimension $n+1$ whose boundary is $M \sqcup (-N)$. Cobordism is an equivalence relation, and the cobordism classes form the cobordism ring $\Omega_n^{SO}$. Thom computed this ring, showing $\Omega_*^{SO} \otimes \mathbb{Q}$ is a polynomial algebra generated by complex projective spaces $\mathbb{CP}^{2k}$.

\term{Code (Error-Correcting)}
A code is a set of allowed words $C\subseteq\Sigma^n$. The Hamming distance counts differing positions; if the minimum distance is $2t+1$, the code can correct up to $t$ errors. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} error-correcting codes protect data stored or transmitted in satellites, computers, mobile networks, and digital storage.

\term{Coding Theory}
The study of error-detecting and error-correcting codes, subsets $C$ of $\Sigma^n$ chosen so that distinct codewords stay far apart in Hamming distance. Linear codes over finite fields are vector subspaces with parameters $[n,k,d]$, and their minimum distance determines error-correcting capability. Bounds such as the Hamming, Singleton, and Gilbert--Varshamov bounds constrain the achievable parameters. Constructions over finite fields, including Reed--Solomon codes, connect coding theory to cryptography, design theory, and algebraic geometry.

\term{Codomain}
The codomain of a function $f:A\to B$ is the declared target set $B$; the range is the subset of $B$ actually reached. Distinguishing them makes it possible to ask whether a function is surjective. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} codomains are important in modeling, type checking, inverse problems, and the precise composition of functions.

\term{Coefficient of Variation}
The coefficient of variation is $CV = \sigma/\mu$, the ratio of standard deviation to mean. It is a dimensionless measure of dispersion, useful for comparing variability across datasets with different units or scales. A $CV > 1$ indicates high relative variability; a $CV < 0.1$ indicates low relative variability.

\term{Combination}
A combination is a selection in which order does not matter. The number of ways to choose $k$ objects from $n$ is $\binom nk=n!/[k!(n-k)!]$. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} combinations count samples, possible teams, subsets in algorithms, and outcomes in probability.

\term{Cofibration}
A cofibration is a map $i:A\to X$ with the homotopy extension property: a deformation already defined on $A$ can be extended to all of $X$. In model categories, cofibrations are one of the three distinguished classes of maps and are dual to fibrations. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} cofibrations provide well-behaved inclusions for building spaces in homotopy theory and algebraic topology.

\term{Coherence (Category Theory)}
Coherence theorems in category theory state that all diagrams built from the structural natural isomorphisms (associator, unitors, braiding) commute. Mac Lane's coherence theorem for monoidal categories asserts that any two parallel morphisms built from associators and unitors are equal. This justifies suppressing parentheses in monoidal categories without ambiguity.

\term{Coherent Sheaf}
A coherent sheaf is a sheaf of functions or modules that is locally described by finitely many generators and relations. On a proper space, its cohomology groups are finite-dimensional; for a sufficiently positive line bundle, higher cohomology eventually vanishes. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} coherent sheaves encode algebraic families, constraints, and sections in algebraic geometry.

\term{Cohomology}
Cohomology is the contravariant functor $H^n(X; G)$ associated to a topological space $X$ and coefficient group $G$, computed from the dual of the singular chain complex. Unlike homology, cohomology carries a natural ring structure (the cup product), making $H^*(X) = \bigoplus H^n(X)$ a graded ring. Cohomology is more computable than homology in many cases and carries richer structure.

\term{Colimit}
A categorical construction dual to a limit: the colimit of a diagram $D: J \to \mathcal{C}$ is the universal cocone, an object $\operatorname{colim} D$ with maps $D_j \to \operatorname{colim} D$ through which every other cocone factors uniquely. Direct limits, pushouts, and coproducts are special cases. Colimits of sets and modules exist and are built from generators and relations. The construction is a left adjoint to the diagonal functor, and filtered colimits commute with finite limits in categories such as modules.

\term{Commutative Algebra}
The study of commutative rings, their ideals, and modules, forming the algebraic foundation of algebraic geometry and number theory.

\term{Compact Operator}
A bounded linear operator $T: X \to Y$ between Banach spaces is compact if the image of the unit ball is relatively compact (its closure is compact). Equivalently, for every bounded sequence $(x_n)$, the sequence $(Tx_n)$ has a convergent subsequence. Compact operators generalise matrices to infinite dimensions; the spectral theory of compact operators closely mirrors finite-dimensional linear algebra.

\term{Compact Space}
A topological space is compact if every collection of open sets that covers it has a finite subcollection that still covers it. In $\mathbb{R}^n$, the Heine--Borel theorem says that compactness is equivalent to being closed and bounded. A continuous real-valued function on a compact space attains both a maximum and a minimum. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} compactness helps prove that optimization problems have solutions and that limits or subsequences exist.

\term{Compactness Theorem}
In first-order logic, a set of sentences has a model if and only if every finite subset of it has a model; the substantive direction is that finite satisfiability forces satisfiability of the whole set. It follows from Gödel's completeness theorem and implies the Löwenheim--Skolem theorems and the existence of nonstandard models of arithmetic. Compactness is widely used to transfer finite results to infinite settings, as when the four-colour theorem for finite planar graphs is extended to infinite graphs.

\term{Completeness (Metric Space)}
A metric space $(X,d)$ is complete if every Cauchy sequence in $X$ converges to a point of $X$. The real numbers are complete, while the rationals are not; completing a space adds the missing limits. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} completeness ensures that limiting numerical approximations remain meaningful in analysis, differential equations, and computation.

\term{Complexity Class}
A set of decision problems solvable within prescribed resource bounds, such as time or space as functions of input length. Major classes include $\mathsf{P}$, $\mathsf{NP}$, $\mathsf{PSPACE}$, and $\mathsf{EXP}$. Reductions order problems by difficulty; a problem is complete for a class if it lies in the class and every member reduces to it, as in NP-completeness. Hierarchy theorems show the corresponding time and space classes are strictly increasing. Whether $\mathsf{P} = \mathsf{NP}$ remains open.

\term{Complex Manifold}
A real manifold of even dimension with an atlas whose transition functions are biholomorphic, modeled on open subsets of $\mathbb{C}^n$ with holomorphic coordinate changes. Complex manifolds carry a holomorphic tangent bundle and Dolbeault cohomology groups $H^{p,q}$. Riemann surfaces, the complex dimension-one case, and complex projective space $\mathbb{CP}^n$ are the primary examples. An almost-complex structure is a pointwise condition that is integrable exactly when the Nijenhuis tensor vanishes, by the Newlander--Nirenberg theorem.

\term{Complex Number}
A complex number is $z = a + bi$ where $a, b \in \mathbb{R}$ and $i^2 = -1$. The complex plane $\mathbb{C}$ is identified with $\mathbb{R}^2$. The modulus is $|z| = \sqrt{a^2+b^2}$ and the argument is $\arg(z) = \arctan(b/a)$. Complex numbers form an algebraically closed field: every non-constant polynomial has a root in $\mathbb{C}$ (Fundamental Theorem of Algebra).

\term{Composition Series}
A composition series of a group $G$ is a finite chain $G = G_0 \triangleright G_1 \triangleright \cdots \triangleright G_n = \{e\}$ where each $G_{i+1}$ is a maximal normal subgroup of $G_i$, so each factor $G_i/G_{i+1}$ is simple. The Jordan--Hölder theorem states that any two composition series have the same length and the same multiset of simple factors (up to isomorphism).

\term{Concave Function}
A function $f$ is concave if $f(tx+(1-t)y)\ge tf(x)+(1-t)f(y)$ for $0\le t\le1$; its graph lies above its chords. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} concavity models diminishing returns and helps analyze utility, entropy, risk, and optimization problems.

\term{Concentration of Measure}
In high-dimensional spaces, most of the volume (or measure) concentrates near the boundary or equator. For the unit sphere $S^{n-1} \subset \mathbb{R}^n$, the measure of an $\epsilon$-neighbourhood of the equator $\{x : |x_1| \leq \epsilon\}$ approaches 1 as $n \to \infty$. Lévy's lemma quantifies this: Lipschitz functions on $S^n$ are nearly constant. Concentration of measure is foundational in asymptotic geometric analysis and probability.

\term{Condition Number}
A condition number measures how sensitive a problem's output is to small changes in its input. For an invertible matrix, $\kappa(A)=\|A\|\|A^{-1}\|$; a large value indicates an ill-conditioned problem. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} condition numbers estimate lost numerical accuracy and guide the choice of stable algorithms and better measurements.

\term{Conditional Expectation}
The conditional expectation $\mathbb E[X\mid\mathcal F]$ is the best average prediction of $X$ using the information in the $\sigma$-algebra $\mathcal F$. It is itself a random variable determined by that information. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} conditional expectation is used in prediction, filtering, regression, finance, and stochastic processes.

\term{Conditional Probability}
The conditional probability of $A$ given $B$ is $P(A|B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}$ for $P(B) > 0$. Bayes' theorem relates conditional probabilities: $P(A|B) = \frac{P(B|A)P(A)}{P(B)}$. Conditional expectation $E[X|Y]$ is a random variable measurable with respect to $\sigma(Y)$ satisfying $\int_A X\,dP = \int_A E[X|Y]\,dP$ for all $A \in \sigma(Y)$.

\term{Conditioning}
Conditioning describes the sensitivity of a problem's exact answer to small changes in its input; the condition number measures this sensitivity. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} conditioning tells scientists whether uncertainty in measurements will be greatly amplified by a computation.

\term{Condorcet Paradox}
Even when each voter's preferences are transitive, majority voting can produce a cycle: A beats B, B beats C, and C beats A. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} the paradox matters in voting systems, preference aggregation, committee decisions, and ranking algorithms.

\term{Cone}
A cone is a solid with a circular base and one vertex; in convex geometry it is a set closed under multiplication by nonnegative scalars. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} cones describe feasible directions, light cones, inequality constraints, optimization problems, and three-dimensional objects.

\term{Conformal Map}
A holomorphic function $f: D \to \mathbb{C}$ is conformal at $z_0$ if $f'(z_0) \neq 0$. Conformal maps preserve angles (in magnitude and orientation) between curves. By the Riemann Mapping Theorem, every simply connected proper open subset of $\mathbb{C}$ is conformally equivalent to the unit disk. Conformal maps are used in fluid dynamics, electrostatics, and cartography.

\term{Conjugate}
The complex conjugate of $z=x+iy$ is $\bar z=x-iy$. In a group, conjugate elements have the form $aga^{-1}$ and represent the same symmetry in a different coordinate frame. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} conjugation is used in signal processing, inner products, quantum mechanics, and group symmetry.

\term{Connected Space}
A space is connected if it cannot be split into two nonempty disjoint open pieces. It is path-connected if every two points can be joined by a continuous path; path-connectedness implies connectedness, but not conversely. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} connectedness identifies coherent regions in topology, geometry, image segmentation, and network models.

\term{Connectedness}
A space is connected if it cannot be split into two nonempty disjoint open pieces. An interval is connected, whereas two separated intervals are not. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} connectedness identifies coherent regions in geometry, image analysis, materials science, and network models.

\term{Connection}
A connection is a rule for comparing vectors or other geometric data at different points of a curved space. It defines parallel transport and covariant differentiation. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} connections describe curvature and motion in differential geometry, general relativity, robotics, and gauge theory.

\term{Consistency}
For a numerical differential-equation scheme, consistency means that its truncation error tends to zero as the mesh spacing tends to zero. Consistency alone does not guarantee stability or convergence. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} it is one of the basic checks used when designing reliable simulations of heat, waves, and fluid flow.

\term{Contact Manifold}
A contact manifold is an odd-dimensional smooth manifold $M^{2n+1}$ with a contact structure: a maximally non-integrable hyperplane field locally the kernel of a 1-form $\alpha$ with $\alpha \wedge (d\alpha)^n$ nowhere vanishing. By Darboux's theorem every contact structure is locally equivalent to $\alpha = dz - \sum_{i=1}^n y_i\,dx_i$ in suitable coordinates. The Reeb vector field determined by $\alpha$ generates a flow preserving $\alpha$, the Reeb flow. Contact geometry is the odd-dimensional counterpart of symplectic geometry, with applications in Hamiltonian dynamics, low-dimensional topology, and geometric quantisation.

\term{Continuity}
See \term{Continuous Function}. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} continuity is used to justify limits, optimization, numerical approximation, and the stable dependence of outputs on inputs.

\term{Continuous Function}
A function $f:X\to Y$ is continuous if inverse images of open sets are open; in metric spaces this is the familiar $\epsilon$--$\delta$ condition. Informally, small changes in the input produce small changes in the output. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} continuity is required for stable modeling, optimization, numerical approximation, and physical laws.

\term{Continuum}
A connected compact metric space; often used loosely for the real line as an uncountable continuum.

\term{Continuum Hypothesis}
The continuum hypothesis asserts that no set has cardinality strictly between that of $\mathbb N$ and that of $\mathbb R$. Gödel showed it is consistent with the usual ZFC axioms, while Cohen showed that its negation is also consistent; therefore ZFC neither proves nor disproves it. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} it illustrates the limits of axiomatic systems and the role of assumptions in set theory and logic.

\term{Contour Integral}
A contour integral integrates a function along an oriented curve $\gamma$ in the complex plane: $\int_\gamma f(z)\,dz$. The value depends on both the function and the path unless additional conditions make it path-independent. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} contour integrals compute residues, solve complex integrals, and model two-dimensional potential and fluid flow.

\term{Contraction Mapping Principle}
Also called the Banach Fixed-Point Theorem. If $(X,d)$ is a complete metric space and $T: X \to X$ satisfies $d(Tx, Ty) \leq c\, d(x,y)$ for some $0 \leq c < 1$, then $T$ has a unique fixed point, and the iterates $x_{n+1} = Tx_n$ converge to it for any starting point $x_0$. This is the foundation for existence-uniqueness proofs in ODEs, integral equations, and numerical analysis.

\term{Contradiction}
A contradiction is a statement or pair of assumptions that cannot both be true. In proof by contradiction, one assumes the negation of the desired claim and derives an impossibility. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} contradiction is used to prove irrationality, uniqueness, impossibility results, and the correctness of algorithms.

\term{Convergence in Distribution}
Random variables $X_n$ converge in distribution to $X$ when their cumulative distribution functions converge at every continuity point of the distribution of $X$. This is weaker than convergence in probability or almost surely. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} it supports distributional approximations in statistics and the central limit theorem.

\term{Convergence Almost Everywhere}
Also called almost sure convergence, this means $P(\{\omega:X_n(\omega)\to X(\omega)\})=1$. It is stronger than convergence in probability and implies it. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} it describes long-run sample behavior in probability, simulation, and stochastic modeling.

\term{Convergence in Probability}
Random variables $X_n$ converge in probability to $X$ if $P(|X_n-X|>\epsilon)\to0$ for every $\epsilon>0$. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} this notion formalizes the consistency of estimators and the reliability of repeated simulations.

\term{Convergence (Mode of)}
Common modes include almost sure convergence, convergence in probability, convergence in $L^p$, and convergence in distribution. They have different strengths: almost sure convergence implies convergence in probability, which implies convergence in distribution; $L^p$ convergence also implies convergence in probability. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} choosing the right mode determines what can be concluded about statistical estimators and numerical approximations.

\term{Convergent Series}
An infinite series $\sum a_n$ converges when its partial sums $a_1+\cdots+a_n$ approach a finite limit. The limit is the sum of the series. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} convergent series represent functions, approximate numerical values, solve differential equations, and describe probabilities.

\term{Convex Function}
A function $f$ is convex if $f(tx+(1-t)y)\le tf(x)+(1-t)f(y)$ for $0\le t\le1$. Its graph lies below its chords; when twice differentiable, this is equivalent to $f''\ge0$. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} convexity supports global optimization and models costs, risk, energy, and diminishing returns.

\term{Convex Hull}
The convex hull of a set $S \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ is the smallest convex set containing $S$, equivalently the set of all convex combinations $\sum \lambda_i x_i$ with $x_i \in S$, $\lambda_i \geq 0$, $\sum \lambda_i = 1$. Carathéodory's theorem states that any point in the convex hull of $S \subset \mathbb{R}^n$ is a convex combination of at most $n+1$ points of $S$.

\term{Convex Optimization}
Convex optimization minimizes a convex function over a convex feasible set. Every local minimum is then a global minimum, although algorithms may still need conditions for existence and efficient computation. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} convex optimization is used in machine learning, portfolio design, signal recovery, control, and engineering.

\term{Convex Polytope}
The convex hull of finitely many points in $\mathbb{R}^n$, equivalently a bounded intersection of finitely many closed half-spaces by the Minkowski--Weyl theorem. Its faces form a lattice: vertices, edges, and facets are the 0-, 1-, and $(n-1)$-dimensional faces, encoded by the $f$-vector. The polar, or dual, polytope reverses the face lattice. Euler's formula $V - E + F = 2$ holds in three dimensions. Polytopes are central to linear programming and combinatorial geometry.

\term{Convex Set}
A set $C$ in a vector space is convex if for any $x, y \in C$, the line segment $[x,y] = \{tx + (1-t)y : 0 \leq t \leq 1\}$ lies entirely in $C$. Examples: balls, half-spaces, simplices, the positive orthant. The intersection of convex sets is convex. A function is convex iff its epigraph is a convex set. Convex sets are central to optimisation and functional analysis.

\term{Coordinate Chart}
A coordinate chart on a manifold $M$ is a pair $(U,\varphi)$ where $U$ is open and $\varphi:U\to\mathbb R^n$ gives local coordinates. An atlas is a collection of charts covering $M$, with smooth transition maps between overlapping charts. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} charts let scientists describe curved surfaces with local coordinates in robotics, relativity, computer graphics, and navigation.

\term{Copula}
A copula is a multivariate distribution function whose one-dimensional margins are uniform on $[0,1]$. By Sklar's theorem, every joint distribution function $F$ with margins $F_1, \ldots, F_n$ admits the decomposition $F(x_1, \ldots, x_n) = C(F_1(x_1), \ldots, F_n(x_n))$ with $C$ unique when the margins are continuous, so the copula captures dependence independently of the marginal laws. Principal families include the Archimedean copulas (Clayton, Frank, Gumbel) and the Gaussian copula. Copulas are widely used in finance, risk management, and dependence modelling.

\term{Corollary}
A corollary is a result that follows quickly from a theorem or proposition, often by applying it to a special case. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} corollaries make a general theorem useful by recording consequences that can be cited directly in later proofs or models.

\term{Correlation Coefficient}
The Pearson correlation coefficient is $\rho_{XY}=\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)/(\sigma_X\sigma_Y)$ and measures linear association, with values in $[-1,1]$. Values $1$ and $-1$ indicate perfect positive and negative linear relationships; zero means no linear relationship, not necessarily independence. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} correlation summarizes data relationships in statistics, finance, science, and machine learning.

\term{Correspondence Theorem}
For a homomorphism $f:G\to H$, subgroups of $G$ containing $\ker f$ correspond to subgroups of the image $f(G)$, preserving inclusion, normality, and quotients. Analogous results hold for ideals and modules. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} the theorem simplifies the classification of quotient groups, quotient rings, and quotient modules.

\term{Coset}
If $H$ is a subgroup of $G$ and $g \in G$, the left coset is $gH = \{gh : h \in H\}$ and the right coset is $Hg = \{hg : h \in H\}$. Left cosets partition $G$ into disjoint sets of equal size $|H|$. Lagrange's theorem states $[G:H] = |G|/|H|$. A subgroup is normal iff left and right cosets coincide.

\term{Cotangent Bundle}
The cotangent bundle $T^*M$ is the union of all cotangent spaces $T_p^*M=(T_pM)^*$ over points $p$ of a manifold. It has dimension $2n$ when $M$ has dimension $n$ and carries a natural symplectic form. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} it combines position and momentum and is the natural phase space for Hamiltonian mechanics, control theory, and geometric optics.

\term{Cotangent Complex}
The cotangent complex $L_{B/A}$ is a complex of $B$-modules governing the deformation theory of a morphism of algebras (or schemes) $A \to B$; it is a derived replacement of the module of Kähler differentials $\Omega_{B/A} = H^0(L_{B/A})$. Its cohomology modules $H^i(L_{B/A})$ govern derivations and extensions: obstructions to lifting $B$ over square-zero extensions of $A$ live in $H^2(L_{B/A})$, which also computes André--Quillen homology. For smooth morphisms $L_{B/A}$ is concentrated in degree zero, and under étale localisation it behaves like classical differentials. It was constructed by Quillen and developed by Illusie.

\term{Cotangent Space}
The cotangent space at a point is the dual of its tangent space; its elements are covectors that measure directional change. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} cotangent spaces contain gradients and differentials and are used in optimization, mechanics, and differential geometry.

\term{Countable Set}
A set is countable if it can be put in one-to-one correspondence with a subset of the natural numbers. Finite sets and $\mathbb N$ are countable, while $\mathbb R$ is not. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} countability distinguishes listable data from continua and is central to probability, computability, and analysis.

\term{Counterexample}
A counterexample is one valid example for which a proposed general statement fails. A single counterexample is enough to disprove a universal claim. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} counterexamples test algorithms, expose missing assumptions, and help refine mathematical models.

\term{Covariance}
The covariance $\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=\mathbb E[(X-\mathbb EX)(Y-\mathbb EY)]$ measures how two random variables vary together. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} covariance is used in regression, portfolio risk, principal component analysis, and uncertainty propagation.

\term{Covariant Derivative}
A covariant derivative differentiates vector fields and tensors on a curved space while respecting the geometry; it is linear and satisfies the product rule. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} it defines acceleration, curvature, geodesics, and field equations in differential geometry and physics.

\term{Covering Space}
A covering space is a space $\widetilde X$ with a map $p:\widetilde X\to X$ such that each point of $X$ has a neighborhood whose preimage is a disjoint union of copies of that neighborhood. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} covering spaces compute fundamental groups and model periodic motion, wave phase, and multivalued functions.

\term{Cramer's Rule}
For a linear system $Ax = b$ with $\det(A) \neq 0$, the unique solution is $x_i = \frac{\det(A_i)}{\det(A)}$, where $A_i$ is $A$ with column $i$ replaced by $b$. Cramer's rule is theoretically important but numerically impractical for large systems due to the cost of computing determinants. It is useful for symbolic computation and proving theoretical results.

\term{Critical Point}
A critical point is a point where the derivative or gradient is zero, or where the derivative does not exist. It is a candidate for a local maximum, local minimum, or saddle point. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} critical points are central to optimization, equilibrium analysis, bifurcation theory, and the study of physical energy landscapes.

\term{Crofton Formula}
The Crofton formula relates the length of a curve to the measure of lines intersecting it. In the plane, the length of a rectifiable curve $\gamma$ is $\frac12\iint n(\gamma\cap\ell)\,d\mu(\ell)$. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} it estimates lengths and areas from intersections and is used in geometric probability, tomography, and image analysis.

\term{Cross Product}
For vectors $a,b\in\mathbb R^3$, the cross product $a\times b$ is perpendicular to both, has magnitude $\|a\|\|b\|\sin\theta$, and is oriented by the right-hand rule. Its magnitude is the area of the parallelogram spanned by the vectors. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} cross products calculate torque, angular momentum, surface normals, and areas in three-dimensional geometry.

\term{Cross Ratio}
For four distinct points on the Riemann sphere, the cross ratio is $(z_1,z_2;z_3,z_4)=\frac{(z_1-z_3)(z_2-z_4)}{(z_1-z_4)(z_2-z_3)}$. It is invariant under Möbius transformations. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} it provides a projective invariant in geometry and is used in camera calibration, computer vision, and conformal mapping.

\term{Cubic Reciprocity}
Cubic reciprocity is the reciprocity law governing solutions of $x^3 \equiv a \pmod{p}$ for rational primes $p \equiv 1 \pmod{3}$. Working in the Eisenstein integers $\mathbb{Z}[\omega]$, where $\omega$ is a primitive cube root of unity, one defines the cubic residue symbol $(\alpha/\beta)_3$ on primary elements; the reciprocity law states $(\alpha/\beta)_3 = (\beta/\alpha)_3$ for primary $\alpha, \beta$. It is an analogue of quadratic reciprocity and was used by Eisenstein in his proof that $x^3 + y^3 = z^3$ has no non-trivial integer solutions. The theory is due to Eisenstein and Jacobi.

\term{Curry's Paradox}
In a system with self-reference and ordinary logical rules, the sentence “If this sentence is true, then $X$” can force any proposition $X$. It shows that unrestricted self-reference and implication can make a logical system inconsistent. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} Curry's paradox informs formal logic, programming-language semantics, and automated reasoning.

\term{Curvature}
Curvature measures how rapidly a curve changes direction. For a plane curve, curvature is the reciprocal of the radius of its best-fitting osculating circle; surfaces have more general curvature measures. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} curvature describes bending in roads, robot paths, optics, fluid flow, and the geometry of spacetime.

\term{Curvature Tensor}
The Riemann curvature tensor $R^a_{\ bcd}$ measures the failure of parallel transport around a small loop to return a vector unchanged; Ricci and scalar curvature are derived from it. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} it describes intrinsic curvature in general relativity, geodesic deviation, and the geometry of curved spaces.

\term{CW Complex}
A CW complex is a space built inductively by attaching cells $e^n_\alpha \cong D^n$ along their boundary spheres to the $(n-1)$-skeleton $X^{n-1}$, beginning with a discrete 0-skeleton, and equipped with the weak topology and the closure-finiteness condition. CW complexes form the natural combinatorial category for homotopy theory: by the Whitehead theorem a weak equivalence between CW complexes is a homotopy equivalence, and cellular approximation models maps between them. Cellular homology computes $H_*(X)$ from the attaching maps, and every space is weakly equivalent to a CW complex.

\term{Cycle}
A cycle is a closed path in a graph with no repeated vertices, or a homology class in $Z_n/B_n$. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} graph cycles detect loops in networks, while homology cycles detect holes in geometric data and support topological data analysis.

\term{Cyclotomic Field}
The $n$-th cyclotomic field is $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_n)$ where $\zeta_n = e^{2\pi i/n}$ is a primitive $n$-th root of unity. It is a Galois extension of $\mathbb{Q}$ with Galois group $(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^\times$, abelian of order $\varphi(n)$. Cyclotomic fields are central to algebraic number theory; Kummer used them to study Fermat's Last Theorem via cyclotomic units and ideal class groups.

\term{Cyclotomic Polynomial}
The $n$th cyclotomic polynomial $\Phi_n(x)$ is the polynomial whose roots are the primitive $n$th roots of unity. It satisfies $x^n-1=\prod_{d\mid n}\Phi_d(x)$ and has degree $\varphi(n)$. For a prime $p$, $\Phi_p(x)=x^{p-1}+\cdots+x+1$. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} cyclotomic polynomials construct regular polygons, study roots of unity, and support finite-field and cryptographic algorithms.

\term{Cylinder (Topology)}
The topological cylinder is $S^1 \times \mathbb{R}$, homeomorphic to $\mathbb{R}^2 \setminus \{0\}$. More generally, the cylinder over a space $X$ is $X \times [0,1]$. The cylinder is used to define homotopy: a homotopy between $f$ and $g$ is a continuous map $H: X \times [0,1] \to Y$ with $H(x,0) = f(x)$ and $H(x,1) = g(x)$.

\term{Cyclic Group}
A cyclic group is generated by one element: $G=\langle g\rangle=\{g^n:n\in\mathbb Z\}$. Every cyclic group is abelian, and a finite one of order $n$ is isomorphic to $\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z$. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} cyclic groups model periodic phenomena, modular arithmetic, rotations, and the additive structure of integers.

\term{Cyclic Homology}
Cyclic homology $HC_*(A)$ is a homology theory for associative algebras, introduced by Connes (1985). It is related to Hochschild homology via the Connes exact sequence: $\cdots \to HC_{n-2}(A) \to HH_n(A) \to HC_n(A) \to HC_{n-1}(A) \to \cdots$. Cyclic homology computes invariants of non-commutative spaces and appears in index theory and deformation quantisation.

\term{Cylinder Set}
In measure theory on product spaces, a cylinder set is a set of the form $\{x \in X^I : (x_{i_1}, \ldots, x_{i_n}) \in B\}$ where $B$ is measurable in the finite product. Cylinder sets generate the product $\sigma$-algebra. Kolmogorov's extension theorem uses cylinder sets to construct measures on infinite product spaces from consistent finite-dimensional distributions.
\term{Adjacency Matrix}
A square matrix $A=(a_{ij})$ represents a graph when $a_{ij}$ records whether vertices $i$ and $j$ are connected, or records an edge weight. Its eigenvalues form the graph spectrum. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} adjacency matrices support network ranking, community detection, graph clustering, and connectivity analysis.
\term{Categorical Product}
The categorical product of $A$ and $B$ has projection maps to both and the property that any object mapping to $A$ and $B$ maps uniquely to the product. For sets it is the Cartesian product; for groups it is the direct product. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} categorical products unify ways of combining objects across algebra, topology, logic, and computer science.
\term{Clique}
A subset of vertices in a graph such that every two distinct vertices are adjacent; equivalently, a complete subgraph. The clique number $\omega(G)$ is the maximum size of a clique in $G$. Finding maximum cliques is NP-hard, and cliques appear in extremal graph theory and in the study of perfect graphs.
\term{Connectivity}
A measure of how connected a graph is. The vertex connectivity $\kappa(G)$ is the minimum number of vertices whose removal disconnects the graph or leaves a trivial graph. The edge connectivity $\kappa'(G)$ is defined analogously for edges. A graph is $k$-connected if its connectivity is at least $k$. By Menger's theorem, $k$-connectivity is equivalent to the existence of $k$ internally vertex-disjoint paths between every pair of vertices.
\term{Covariant Functor}
A functor $F: \mathcal{C} \to \mathcal{D}$ that preserves the direction of morphisms: $F(f \circ g) = F(f) \circ F(g)$ and $F(\operatorname{id}_X) = \operatorname{id}_{F(X)}$. The hom-functor $\operatorname{Hom}(X, -): \mathcal{C} \to \mathbf{Set}$ is a fundamental example. Covariant functors are the default notion of functor.
\term{Cut Vertex}
A cut vertex is a vertex whose removal increases the number of connected components of a graph. It is also called an articulation point. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} identifying cut vertices reveals vulnerable points in communication, transport, power, and dependency networks.
\term{Generating Function}
A formal power series $\sum_{n=0}^\infty a_n x^n$ whose coefficients encode a sequence $a_n$. Generating functions transform combinatorial problems into analytic ones. Ordinary generating functions count objects by size; exponential generating functions $\sum a_n x^n/n!$ are natural for labelled structures. The method is due to Euler and de Moivre and is central to enumerative combinatorics.
\term{Chromatic Number}
The smallest number of colours needed to colour the vertices of a graph so that no adjacent vertices share a colour. It is denoted $\chi(G)$. A graph is $k$-chromatic if $\chi(G) = k$. By Brooks' theorem, $\chi(G) \leq \Delta(G)$ for connected graphs other than complete graphs and odd cycles, where $\Delta$ is the maximum degree.
