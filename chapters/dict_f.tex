\chapter*{F}
\label{chap:f}

\term{Faà di Bruno's Formula}
Faà di Bruno's formula is the chain rule for derivatives taken many times. It tells how to differentiate a function placed inside another function, by combining the derivatives of the outside and inside functions. \par\noindent Applications: it helps calculate higher-order derivatives in probability, combinatorics, differential equations, and automatic differentiation.

\term{Face (Polytope)}
A face of a convex polytope is the part cut out by a supporting hyperplane. Faces include vertices, edges, facets, and the whole polytope. \par\noindent Applications: faces describe the geometry of polyhedra and are used in linear programming, computer graphics, and computational geometry.

\term{Factorial Function}
The factorial is $n!=1\cdot2\cdots n$ for a nonnegative integer, with $0!=1$. Stirling's approximation gives $n!\sim\sqrt{2\pi n}(n/e)^n$, and $n!=\Gamma(n+1)$. \par\noindent Applications: factorials count arrangements, enter probability formulas, and support series expansions and numerical approximations.

\term{Fadeev--Popov Determinant}
The Fadeev--Popov determinant is a correction factor used when several mathematical descriptions represent the same physical state. It prevents that repeated description from being counted many times in a calculation. \par\noindent Applications: it makes calculations in gauge theories and quantum field theory consistent.

\term{Fan Graph}
A fan graph joins one central vertex to every vertex of a path. \par\noindent Applications: fan graphs model hub-and-spoke networks and appear in chemical graph theory, connectivity studies, and graph spectra.

\term{Fan (Toric Geometry)}
A fan is a carefully arranged collection of cones that fit together along shared faces. In toric geometry, this simple arrangement acts like a blueprint for building a more complicated algebraic shape. \par\noindent Applications: fans describe algebraic varieties, polyhedra, compactifications, and combinatorial geometry.

\term{Fatou's Lemma}
Fatou's lemma says that for nonnegative functions, the integral of the eventual lower value cannot exceed the eventual lower value of the integrals. In plain terms, taking a limit cannot create more total mass than the approximations already allow. \par\noindent Applications: it justifies limiting arguments in probability, integration, optimization, and differential equations.

\term{Fault-Tolerant Quantum Computation}
Fault-tolerant quantum computation keeps a quantum calculation reliable even when its physical parts make occasional errors. Information is spread across several qubits so that errors can be detected and corrected before they ruin the computation. \par\noindent Applications: it is needed for practical quantum computers, secure quantum communication, and long computations on noisy hardware.

\term{Feasible Region}
The feasible region is the set of all choices that obey the rules of an optimization problem. For a linear program, it is often a polygon or polyhedron, and an optimum may occur at a corner. \par\noindent Applications: feasible regions describe legal schedules, safe designs, budgets, routes, and resource allocations.

\term{Fermat's Last Theorem}
Fermat's Last Theorem states that $x^n+y^n=z^n$ has no positive integer solutions when $n>2$. \par\noindent Applications: its proof connects elliptic curves, modular forms, and number theory and illustrates how elementary equations can require deep mathematics.

\term{Fermat's Little Theorem}
If $p$ is prime and $p\nmid a$, then $a^{p-1}\equiv1\pmod p$. \par\noindent Applications: Fermat's little theorem supports primality tests, modular inverses, and public-key cryptography.

\term{Fiber Bundle}
A fiber bundle is a space $E$ with a projection $\pi:E\to B$ that locally looks like a product $U\times F$, where $B$ is the base and $F$ is the fiber. Globally, the fibers may twist, as in a Möbius strip. \par\noindent Applications: bundles model fields, orientations, configuration spaces, and gauge potentials in geometry and physics.

\term{Fibonacci Sequence}
The Fibonacci sequence satisfies $F_0=0$, $F_1=1$, and $F_n=F_{n-1}+F_{n-2}$. The ratio of consecutive terms approaches the golden ratio $\phi=(1+\sqrt5)/2$. \par\noindent Applications: Fibonacci numbers appear in combinatorics, algorithms, continued fractions, coding, and models of branching and growth.

\term{Fibrant Object}
In a model category, an object is fibrant when its map to the terminal object has the required lifting property. Informally, a fibrant object is one on which homotopy-theoretic constructions behave smoothly. In simplicial sets, the fibrant objects are Kan complexes. Weak equivalences between objects that are both fibrant and cofibrant have homotopy inverses. \par\noindent Applications: fibrant replacements let algebraic models represent the homotopy type of spaces without changing their essential information.

\term{Fibration}
A fibration is a map with a homotopy lifting property: a deformation in the base can be lifted after an initial lift is chosen. Fiber bundles are common examples. \par\noindent Applications: fibrations organize homotopy groups, fibered spaces, and the deformation of geometric or topological models.

\term{Field (Algebra)}
A field is a commutative ring in which every nonzero element has a multiplicative inverse. Examples include $\mathbb Q$, $\mathbb R$, $\mathbb C$, and finite fields. \par\noindent Applications: fields provide the scalars for linear algebra and are central to coding theory, cryptography, and polynomial equations.

\term{Field Extension}
A field extension is a larger number system containing a smaller one, with all the old arithmetic rules still working. It is used when a polynomial needs new numbers in order to have all its roots. \par\noindent Applications: field extensions support the study of polynomial equations, geometric constructions, symmetry, and coding.

\term{Fierz Identity}
The Fierz identity says that a product of two spinor pairings can be rearranged into a sum of pairings in a different order. It is an algebraic way to rewrite the same interaction so that a different feature becomes visible. \par\noindent Applications: it simplifies calculations involving particles, spin, four-fermion interactions, and supersymmetry.

\term{Filtration}
A filtration is a nested sequence $\mathcal F_0\subseteq\mathcal F_1\subseteq\cdots$ that records how an object is built in stages. In probability, a filtration represents the information available over time; in algebra, it records layers of a module or complex. \par\noindent Applications: filtrations are used in stochastic processes, spectral sequences, persistent homology, and multiscale data analysis.

\term{Final Object}
A final object is an object that receives exactly one allowed map from every other object. In the category of sets, a one-element set is final because every set has one function to it. \par\noindent Applications: final objects provide universal endpoints in algebra, geometry, logic, and computer science.

\term{Finite Difference}
Finite differences approximate a derivative using function values at nearby grid points, such as $[f(x+h)-f(x)]/h$. They replace a continuous differential equation by equations that a computer can calculate. \par\noindent Applications: finite differences simulate heat, fluid flow, waves, weather, and other physical systems.

\term{Finite-difference Method}
A finite-difference method approximates derivatives by differences of function values on a grid. \par\noindent Applications: it solves heat, wave, fluid, and structural equations numerically.

\term{Finite-element Method}
A finite-element method approximates a PDE solution by piecewise polynomial functions on a mesh, usually through a variational formulation. \par\noindent Applications: it is widely used for stress, heat, fluid, electromagnetic, and structural simulations.

\term{Finite Element Method}
A finite element method solves a difficult equation by dividing a region into small pieces and using a simple approximate formula on each piece. The pieces turn the equation into a large system that a computer can solve. \par\noindent Applications: finite elements model stress, heat, fluid flow, electromagnetic fields, and structures.

\term{Finite Field}
A finite field is a number system with only finitely many elements, where addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division by nonzero elements are possible. Its size is always a power of a prime number. \par\noindent Applications: finite fields make error-correcting codes, digital signatures, secure communication, and computer arithmetic possible.

\term{Finite Group}
A finite group has finitely many elements, and its order is the number of elements. \par\noindent Applications: finite groups describe symmetries of molecules, crystals, codes, puzzles, and finite computational systems.

\term{Finitely Presented Group}
A finitely presented group is described using a finite list of building elements and a finite list of rules telling how those elements combine. The presentation gives a compact recipe for generating every group element. \par\noindent Applications: it describes symmetries, fundamental groups, geometric spaces, and groups used in computation.

\term{Finite Set}
A finite set has a fixed number of elements and is in bijection with $\{1,2,\ldots,n\}$ for some $n$. \par\noindent Applications: finite sets model stored data, finite state machines, discrete experiments, and combinatorial objects.

\term{Finite-volume Method}
A finite-volume method integrates a conservation law over small control volumes and balances fluxes across their boundaries. \par\noindent Applications: its conservation property makes it effective for computational fluid dynamics, weather, combustion, and traffic flow.

\term{First Isomorphism Theorem}
The first isomorphism theorem says that a structure can be simplified by identifying everything sent to the same neutral output. The resulting quotient has exactly the same structure as the part reached by the map. \par\noindent Applications: it simplifies group, ring, and module calculations and explains how algebraic maps lose information.

\term{First-order Logic}
A formal system using variables, relations, logical connectives, and quantifiers over elements of a specified domain. \par\noindent Applications: first-order logic underlies mathematical foundations, database queries, automated reasoning, and formal verification.

\term{Fischer--Burmeister Function}
The Fischer--Burmeister function turns three conditions—two quantities are nonnegative and at least one is zero—into one equation. This lets an optimization problem use ordinary equation-solving tools. \par\noindent Applications: it handles contact, resource allocation, equilibrium models, and constrained optimization.

\term{Fixed Point}
A fixed point is an input that a function leaves unchanged: $f(x)=x$. It represents a state that remains steady after the rule is applied. \par\noindent Applications: fixed points describe equilibria, steady temperatures, stable algorithms, game outcomes, and recurring patterns.

\term{Fixed-Point Theorem}
A fixed-point theorem guarantees a point $x$ with $f(x)=x$ under specified conditions, as in the Banach and Brouwer theorems. \par\noindent Applications: fixed points represent equilibria in economics, games, dynamical systems, and nonlinear equations.

\term{Flat Module}
An $R$-module $M$ is flat if tensoring with $M$ preserves exact sequences. Flatness is weaker than being free: over a local ring, a finitely presented flat module is free, but an arbitrary flat module need not be. Projective modules are flat, and flatness makes the algebraic behavior of fibers change without sudden jumps. \par\noindent Applications: flat modules and flat maps are central in algebraic geometry, where they describe families whose fibers vary consistently.

\term{Flatness}
Flatness means that tensoring a module with $M$ preserves exact sequences; over an integral domain it generalizes being torsion-free. \par\noindent Applications: flatness describes algebraic families whose fibers change without sudden jumps in algebraic geometry.

\term{Floating-Point Arithmetic}
Floating-point arithmetic represents real numbers approximately as $m\times\beta^e$ using a finite mantissa and exponent. Rounding and overflow are unavoidable and are standardized by IEEE 754. \par\noindent Applications: understanding floating point is essential for reliable scientific computing, graphics, finance, and machine learning.

\term{Flow}
A flow is a family of maps describing how states evolve with time; its paths are integral curves of a vector field. \par\noindent Applications: flows model motion, fluid transport, population change, and continuous-time control systems.

\term{Foata Transform}
The Foata transform rearranges a permutation into another form while preserving important counting information. It makes a pattern of rises and falls easier to compare with the cycles of the permutation. \par\noindent Applications: it helps count permutations, study word patterns, and analyze combinatorial statistics.

\term{Focal Point (Optics)}
A focal point is the place where parallel light rays meet after passing through a lens or reflecting from a mirror, or the place they appear to come from. The distance to this point is the focal length. \par\noindent Applications: focal points determine image sharpness, magnification, camera design, telescopes, and eyeglasses.

\term{Fold Catastrophe}
A fold catastrophe occurs when two possible states merge and disappear as a parameter changes. A small gradual change in the input can therefore cause a sudden jump in the observed outcome. \par\noindent Applications: folds model sudden changes in optics, mechanics, economics, biology, and dynamical systems.

\term{Formula}
A formula is a symbolic rule relating quantities, such as the quadratic formula. \par\noindent Applications: formulas make models computable, communicate mathematical relationships, and support prediction and parameter estimation.

\term{Four Color Theorem}
The four color theorem says that every map drawn on a flat surface can be colored with at most four colors so that neighboring regions have different colors. Some maps really do need all four. \par\noindent Applications: it supports map coloring, scheduling problems, frequency assignment, and graph coloring algorithms.

\term{Fourier Coefficients}
Fourier coefficients measure how much of each basic sine and cosine wave is present in a periodic function. Together, the coefficients give a recipe for rebuilding the original signal from its frequencies. \par\noindent Applications: they analyze sound, images, vibrations, electricity, and periodic data.

\term{Fourier--Motzkin Elimination}
Fourier--Motzkin elimination removes variables from a collection of linear inequalities. It combines upper and lower bounds for the variable being removed, leaving conditions on the variables that remain. \par\noindent Applications: it projects feasible regions and supports scheduling, optimization, logic, and constraint solving.

\term{Fourier Series}
A Fourier series represents a repeating function as a sum of simple sine and cosine waves. The slower waves describe the broad shape, while faster waves describe finer details. \par\noindent Applications: Fourier series model sound, heat, vibrations, alternating current, and repeating signals.

\term{Fourier's Law}
Fourier's law says that heat flows from warmer places toward cooler places, and that the flow is stronger when the temperature changes more sharply. \par\noindent Applications: it models heat in buildings, engines, electronics, insulation, and the Earth's climate.

\term{Fourier Transform}
The Fourier transform changes a signal from a description by position or time to a description by frequency. It shows which slow and fast waves make up the signal. \par\noindent Applications: it supports audio filtering, image processing, communications, medical scanning, and quantum mechanics.

\term{Four-Square Theorem}
The four-square theorem says that every positive whole number can be written as the sum of four integer squares. For example, $23=3^2+3^2+2^2+1^2$. \par\noindent Applications: it studies representations of numbers, quadratic equations, number theory, and integer algorithms.

\term{Fractal}
A fractal is a set with detail or patterns that repeat across scales, often with a non-integer dimension. Examples include the Cantor and Mandelbrot sets. \par\noindent Applications: fractals model coastlines, branching networks, porous materials, textures, and financial or biological patterns.

\term{Fractal Dimension}
Fractal dimension measures how a complicated or repeating shape fills space. Unlike an ordinary dimension, it can be between whole numbers: a branching shape may be more than a line but less than a flat surface. \par\noindent Applications: it describes coastlines, cracks, porous materials, branching networks, images, and natural patterns.

\term{Frame}
A frame is a possibly redundant family of vectors that stably represents every vector in a Hilbert space. Unlike a basis, it may contain more vectors than necessary. \par\noindent Applications: frames enable robust signal reconstruction, image compression, sampling, and noise-resistant measurements.

\term{Frattini Subgroup}
The Frattini subgroup contains the group elements that are unnecessary in every smallest generating set. Removing such elements does not reduce the ability to build the whole group. \par\noindent Applications: it identifies essential generators and simplifies the study of finite groups and their subgroups.

\term{Fréchet Derivative}
The Fréchet derivative is the best linear approximation to a function between spaces of vectors or functions. Near an input $x$, it describes the change by $f(x+h)\approx f(x)+Df(x)h$. \par\noindent Applications: it supports infinite-dimensional optimization, differential equations, sensitivity analysis, and numerical methods.

\term{Fredholm Alternative}
The Fredholm alternative says that a certain linear equation either has exactly one solution for every input, or its possible failures are controlled by a finite set of compatibility conditions. There is no uncontrolled middle case. \par\noindent Applications: it tests solvability of integral and differential equations and guides numerical solution methods.

\term{Fredholm Theory}
Fredholm theory studies equations of the form $(I-K)x=y$, where $K$ is often a compact operator. It gives a solvability alternative: an equation is solvable precisely when $y$ satisfies compatibility conditions coming from the corresponding adjoint equation. \par\noindent Applications: the theory helps analyze integral equations, boundary-value problems, and linearized partial differential equations.

\term{Free Group}
A free group is generated by symbols with no relations except cancellation of a symbol with its inverse. Every group is a quotient of a free group. \par\noindent Applications: free groups describe paths, symmetries, automata, and the presentations used in computational group theory.

\term{Free Module}
A free module has a basis, so every element has a unique finite linear combination of basis elements. Equivalently, it is a direct sum of copies of its ring. \par\noindent Applications: free modules provide coordinates for algebraic systems and are used in linear algebra, coding, and algebraic geometry.

\term{Fremlin Measure}
Fremlin measure is a method for extending a preliminary notion of size to a larger collection of sets while retaining local information. It is designed for settings where ordinary measure constructions need extra care. \par\noindent Applications: it supports advanced integration, function spaces, and the study of ordered spaces of functions.

\term{Frenet--Serret Formulas}
The Frenet--Serret formulas describe how a moving curve turns and twists. They track its forward direction, its direction of bending, and the perpendicular direction to both. \par\noindent Applications: they model paths of particles, roads, robot arms, camera motion, and the shape of space curves.

\term{Frequency}
Frequency is the number of cycles per unit time of a periodic phenomenon; it is the reciprocal of the period. \par\noindent Applications: frequency is fundamental in sound, radio, vibration, image analysis, and Fourier-based signal processing.

\term{Freudenthal's Theorem (Homotopy)}
Freudenthal's theorem says that after repeatedly adding a simple new dimension to a sufficiently connected space, its pattern of holes eventually stops changing in the relevant range. This creates a stable version of the space's topology. \par\noindent Applications: it organizes homotopy groups and supports the study of spheres and stable shapes.

\term{Frobenius Coin Problem}
The Frobenius coin problem asks for the largest amount that cannot be made using unlimited coins of given denominations. With two coprime denominations $a$ and $b$, the answer is $ab-a-b$. \par\noindent Applications: it studies change-making, integer combinations, scheduling, and gaps in number systems.

\term{Frobenius Endomorphism}
The Frobenius endomorphism raises each element to the power $p$ in a number system whose characteristic is the prime $p$. In that setting, raising a sum to the $p$th power gives the sum of the $p$th powers. \par\noindent Applications: it reveals symmetry in finite fields and is central to counting solutions of algebraic equations over finite fields.

\term{Frobenius Reciprocity}
Frobenius reciprocity says that extending a representation from a smaller symmetry group and then comparing it with a larger one gives the same answer as first restricting the larger representation and then comparing. It is a precise compatibility between enlarging and shrinking symmetry descriptions. \par\noindent Applications: it simplifies representation theory, symmetry calculations, and the study of group actions.

\term{Frobenius Theorem (Distributions)}
The Frobenius theorem tells when a collection of allowed directions forms actual surfaces or layers. The key requirement is that moving in one allowed direction and then another does not lead outside the collection. \par\noindent Applications: it identifies constraint surfaces, coordinate layers, flows, and integrable systems.

\term{Fubini Property}
The Fubini property says that, under suitable conditions, a two-variable total can be calculated by adding in either order. Thus $\int\!\int f\,dx\,dy=\int\!\int f\,dy\,dx$. \par\noindent Applications: it simplifies area, probability, volume, statistics, and multivariable integration.

\term{Fubini's Theorem}
Fubini's theorem says that a well-behaved multiple integral can be evaluated one variable at a time, in either order. This turns a difficult high-dimensional calculation into a sequence of simpler integrations. \par\noindent Applications: it is used for probabilities, volumes, averages, statistics, and solutions of differential equations.

\term{Fuchsian Differential Equation}
A Fuchsian differential equation is a linear differential equation whose exceptional points are mild enough that its solutions grow in a controlled way near them. This makes the behavior near each exceptional point possible to classify. \par\noindent Applications: it appears in special functions, wave equations, complex analysis, and mathematical physics.

\term{Fuchsian Group}
A Fuchsian group is a collection of fractional-linear transformations that acts on a curved half-plane without accumulating transformations at ordinary points. The resulting quotient produces an important kind of curved surface. \par\noindent Applications: Fuchsian groups describe repeating patterns on curved surfaces, modular forms, and hyperbolic geometry.

\term{Fullerene}
A fullerene is a carbon molecule whose atoms form a closed cage made of five-sided and six-sided rings. The famous buckyball, $C_{60}$, has the shape of a truncated soccer ball. \par\noindent Applications: fullerenes support nanotechnology, materials science, molecular modeling, and the study of carbon structures.

\term{Functional}
A functional is a rule that takes a vector or function as input and returns one number. Integration and taking a weighted average are common examples. \par\noindent Applications: functionals represent measurements, costs, constraints, and objectives in analysis and optimization.

\term{Functional Analysis}
Functional analysis studies spaces of functions and the rules that act on them. It extends familiar ideas such as length, distance, and perpendicularity to spaces with infinitely many coordinates. \par\noindent Applications: it provides the language for differential equations, quantum mechanics, signal analysis, and infinite-dimensional optimization.

\term{Functional Calculus}
Functional calculus lets us apply an ordinary function to a matrix or operator, producing objects such as $e^A$, $\sin A$, or $\log A$. \par\noindent Applications: it solves linear systems, describes time evolution, and studies matrices, differential equations, and quantum observables.

\term{Functional Determinant}
A functional determinant extends the idea of a matrix determinant to certain operations on functions. It summarizes the combined effect of all the operation's natural modes or eigenvalues. \par\noindent Applications: functional determinants occur in differential equations, geometry, quantum theory, and stability calculations.

\term{Function Space}
A function space is a collection whose “points” are functions. A distance or other comparison tells when two functions are close. \par\noindent Applications: function spaces provide settings for differential equations, signal processing, approximation, probability, and optimization.

\term{Functor}
A functor is a translation between two mathematical settings that sends objects and maps to corresponding objects and maps while preserving how maps are combined. \par\noindent Applications: functors organize connections between algebra, geometry, topology, logic, and computer science.

\term{Fundamental Group}
The fundamental group records how loops in a space can be continuously deformed into one another. A nontrivial loop that cannot be shrunk to a point signals a hole, as with loops around a circle. \par\noindent Applications: it detects holes, classifies surfaces, studies knots, and analyzes spaces through their loops.

\term{Fundamental Solution}
A fundamental solution is the response of a differential equation to a single point source. Responses to more complicated sources can then be built by combining shifted copies of this basic response. \par\noindent Applications: fundamental solutions solve heat, wave, electrostatic, and diffusion problems.

\term{Fundamental Theorem of Algebra}
The fundamental theorem of algebra says that every nonconstant polynomial has a complex root. A degree-$n$ polynomial has exactly $n$ complex roots when repeated roots are counted. \par\noindent Applications: it guarantees that polynomial equations can be fully solved in the complex numbers and supports algebra, control, and signal analysis.

\term{Fundamental Theorem of Algebraic Topology}
The fundamental theorem of algebraic topology says that continuous maps between spaces induce corresponding maps between their algebraic measurements of holes. Deforming a space without tearing or joining it leaves those measurements unchanged. \par\noindent Applications: it turns questions about shape into calculations with groups and supports topology, geometry, and data analysis.

\term{Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic}
The fundamental theorem of arithmetic says that every whole number greater than $1$ can be written in exactly one way as a product of primes, apart from the order of the factors. Primes are therefore the basic building blocks of integer multiplication. \par\noindent Applications: it supports divisibility, greatest common divisors, fractions, cryptography, and number algorithms.

\term{Fundamental Theorem of Calculus}
The fundamental theorem of calculus says that differentiation and integration undo each other. The derivative of accumulated area is the original rate, and a definite integral can be found by subtracting endpoint values of an antiderivative. \par\noindent Applications: it calculates areas, distances, totals, work, probabilities, and accumulated change.

\term{Fundamental Theorem of Finite Abelian Groups}
The fundamental theorem of finite abelian groups says that every finite commutative group can be built as a product of simple cyclic groups, and that the building is unique apart from order. \par\noindent Applications: it classifies finite symmetries and simplifies calculations in number theory, coding, and algebra.

\term{Fundamental Theorem of Galois Theory}
The fundamental theorem of Galois theory matches the intermediate number systems inside a field extension with the symmetry groups that keep those systems fixed. Larger intermediate fields correspond to smaller symmetry groups. \par\noindent Applications: it solves polynomial equations by studying their symmetries and connects field theory with group theory.

\term{Fundamental Theorem of Lie Algebras}
The fundamental theorem of Lie algebras says that a finite-dimensional algebra of infinitesimal symmetries splits into a well-behaved symmetry part and a simpler part built from repeated commutators. \par\noindent Applications: it classifies continuous symmetries and supports geometry, mechanics, and mathematical physics.

\term{Fundamental Theorem of Linear Algebra}
The fundamental theorem of linear algebra describes the four main spaces attached to a matrix: inputs sent to zero, outputs that can be reached, and the corresponding perpendicular spaces. Their dimensions explain exactly how many independent directions a system keeps or loses. \par\noindent Applications: it solves linear equations and supports least squares, data analysis, graphics, and engineering models.

\term{Fundamental Theorem of Symmetric Functions}
The fundamental theorem of symmetric functions says that every polynomial unchanged by reordering its variables can be built uniquely from the elementary symmetric polynomials. This explains why polynomial coefficients can be expressed through sums and products of roots. \par\noindent Applications: it simplifies equations, root calculations, polynomial identities, and algebraic combinatorics.

\term{Future Cone}
The future cone is the set of events that can be reached or influenced from a given event without exceeding the speed of light. In flat spacetime it satisfies $t>0$ and $t^2\ge x^2+y^2+z^2$. \par\noindent Applications: future cones describe cause and effect in relativity, spacetime geometry, and relativistic physics.
