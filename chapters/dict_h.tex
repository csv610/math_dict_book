\chapter*{H}
\label{chap:h}

\term{Haar Measure}
A Haar measure is a fair way to measure parts of a group: shifting every part by the same group element does not change its size. On $\mathbb{R}^n$ it is ordinary length, area, or volume; on a finite group it is counting measure. \par\noindent Applications: Haar measure allows integration and probability to be defined consistently on groups and supports harmonic analysis.

\term{Hadamard Factorization Theorem}
The Hadamard factorization theorem says that a well-behaved complex function can be reconstructed from the places where it is zero, together with a factor that records how fast it grows. \par\noindent Applications: it helps classify complex functions and appears in complex analysis, differential equations, and spectral theory.

\term{Hadamard Matrix}
A Hadamard matrix is a square table of $1$s and $-1$s whose rows are mutually perpendicular. For an $n\times n$ matrix, $HH^{\mathsf T}=nI$, so its rows can carry separate signals with little interference. \par\noindent Applications: Hadamard matrices are used in error-correcting codes, fast transforms, experiments, and signal processing.

\term{Hadamard Product}
The Hadamard product, or Schur product, multiplies matrices entry by entry: $(A\circ B)_{ij}=A_{ij}B_{ij}$. It differs from ordinary matrix multiplication. The Schur product theorem says that the Hadamard product of positive semidefinite matrices is positive semidefinite. \par\noindent Applications: it is used for masks, covariance matrices, image processing, and kernel methods.

\term{Hahn--Banach Theorem}
The Hahn--Banach theorem says that a bounded linear functional defined on a subspace of a normed vector space can be extended to the whole space without increasing its norm. Geometrically, it also provides separating hyperplanes for disjoint convex sets. \par\noindent Applications: it supplies duality tools in optimization, functional analysis, and the study of weak convergence.

\term{Hairy Ball Theorem}
The hairy ball theorem says that you cannot comb the hair on an even-dimensional sphere so that every hair lies smoothly and flatly without a bald spot or swirl. In mathematics, every continuous tangent direction must be zero somewhere. \par\noindent Applications: it explains unavoidable still points in wind fields and restrictions on continuous motion over spheres.

\term{Half-plane}
A half-plane is one of the two regions into which a line divides $\mathbb{R}^2$, including or excluding the boundary according to the inequality used. For example, $ax+by\leq c$ defines a closed half-plane. \par\noindent Applications: half-planes describe linear constraints in optimization, feasible regions, and geometric algorithms.

\term{Half-space}
A half-space is one of the two regions into which a hyperplane divides $\mathbb{R}^n$, such as $\{x:a\cdot x\leq b\}$. It is the basic building block of a convex polyhedron. \par\noindent Applications: half-spaces model linear inequalities in optimization, classification, and computational geometry.

\term{Hall's Marriage Theorem}
Hall's marriage theorem states that a finite family of sets $A_1,\ldots,A_n$ has distinct representatives $a_i\in A_i$ exactly when every subfamily of $k$ sets has a union containing at least $k$ elements. This is a matching criterion for bipartite graphs. \par\noindent Applications: it solves assignment, scheduling, school-choice, and resource-allocation problems.

\term{Halting Problem}
The halting problem asks whether a given program will eventually stop. No single algorithm can answer this correctly for every possible program and input. \par\noindent Applications: it shows the limits of automatic program checking and is central to computability theory.

\term{Hamiltonian Cycle}
A Hamiltonian cycle is a route through a network that visits every location exactly once and returns to the start. Finding whether such a route exists can be very difficult for large networks. \par\noindent Applications: Hamiltonian cycles model delivery routes, travel planning, circuit design, and the traveling-salesperson problem.

\term{Hamiltonian (Mechanics)}
A Hamiltonian $H(p,q,t)$ is a function of position or generalized coordinates $q$, momenta $p$, and possibly time. The motion is governed by Hamilton's equations, $\dot q=\partial H/\partial p$ and $\dot p=-\partial H/\partial q$. When $H$ has no explicit time dependence, it is often conserved. \par\noindent Applications: Hamiltonians organize classical mechanics, optics, statistical mechanics, and the transition to quantum mechanics.

\term{Hamiltonian Operator}
The Hamiltonian operator represents the total energy of a quantum system. Its energy states satisfy $\hat H\psi=E\psi$, where $E$ is the measured energy. \par\noindent Applications: Hamiltonian operators describe atoms, molecules, quantum devices, and the evolution of quantum states.

\term{Hamiltonian System}
A Hamiltonian system is a dynamical system with coordinates $(q,p)$ whose evolution satisfies Hamilton's equations $\dot q=\partial H/\partial p$ and $\dot p=-\partial H/\partial q$. Its flow preserves the symplectic structure of phase space, which constrains how volumes and trajectories evolve. \par\noindent Applications: such systems model celestial mechanics, molecular dynamics, waves, and control systems.

\term{Hamming Code}
A Hamming code is a linear error-correcting code that adds check bits to data. The $(7,4)$ code stores four data bits in seven bits, corrects any one-bit error, and detects many two-bit errors. Its parity checks locate the erroneous position through a syndrome. \par\noindent Applications: Hamming codes illustrate reliable data storage and communication with limited redundancy.

\term{Hamming Distance}
The Hamming distance between two equal-length strings is the number of positions in which they differ. For example, the distance between $10110$ and $10011$ is $2$. \par\noindent Applications: it measures transmission errors and determines how many errors an error-correcting code can detect or correct.

\term{Hankel Transform}
A Hankel transform has the form $F(r)=\int_0^\infty f(t)J_n(rt)t\,dt$, where $J_n$ is a Bessel function. It is a Fourier transform adapted to functions with radial or cylindrical symmetry. \par\noindent Applications: it simplifies wave, heat, and electrostatic problems in circular geometries and is used in imaging and signal processing.

\term{Hardy Space}
Hardy spaces collect holomorphic functions that remain suitably controlled as they approach the edge of a domain. For the unit disk, $H^p$ measures the size of boundary values using an $L^p$ average. \par\noindent Applications: Hardy spaces support complex analysis, signal processing, prediction, and boundary-value problems.

\term{Harmonic Analysis}
Harmonic analysis studies how functions and signals can be decomposed into basic oscillations, using Fourier series, Fourier transforms, and related transforms. It also examines how these decompositions behave under translation and scaling. \par\noindent Applications: it underlies audio and image compression, filtering, communications, PDEs, and quantum mechanics.

\term{Harmonic Function}
A harmonic function satisfies Laplace's equation $\Delta u=0$. In a region, its value at the center of a ball equals the average of its values on the boundary, so it cannot have a strict interior maximum or minimum unless it is constant. \par\noindent Applications: harmonic functions describe steady temperature, gravitational and electric potentials, and fluid flow.

\term{Harmonic Mean}
For positive values $x_1,\ldots,x_n$, the harmonic mean is $H=n/(\sum_i1/x_i)$. It gives more weight to small values than the arithmetic mean, so it is appropriate for averaging rates when the same distance or task is performed at different speeds. \par\noindent Applications: it is used for average speeds, ratios, and performance measures such as precision and recall.

\term{Harmonic Number}
The $n$th harmonic number is $H_n=\sum_{k=1}^n1/k$. Although the terms become small, $H_n$ grows without bound, with $H_n=\ln n+\gamma+o(1)$. \par\noindent Applications: harmonic numbers occur in algorithm analysis, expected values in random processes, and the study of series and asymptotic estimates.

\term{Harmonic Series}
The harmonic series $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}1/n$ diverges, even though its individual terms tend to zero. Its partial sums grow approximately like $\ln n$. \par\noindent Applications: it is a basic test case showing that termwise convergence to zero is not sufficient for convergence of a series.

\term{Harnack's Inequality}
Harnack's inequality says that a positive solution of Laplace's equation cannot change too sharply inside a connected region. Its values at two interior points are bounded by fixed multiples of one another when the points stay away from the boundary. \par\noindent Applications: it supports potential theory, heat flow, elliptic equations, and regularity estimates.

\term{Hausdorff Dimension}
Hausdorff dimension extends the idea of dimension to irregular sets and fractals. It is the critical exponent at which the $s$-dimensional Hausdorff measure changes from infinite to zero. A line has dimension $1$, a plane has dimension $2$, and some fractals have non-integer dimension. \par\noindent Applications: it quantifies roughness, scaling, and complexity in dynamical systems, geometry, and data analysis.

\term{Hausdorff Distance}
For compact subsets $A,B$ of a metric space, the Hausdorff distance is
\[
d_H(A,B)=\max\{\sup_{a\in A}d(a,B),\sup_{b\in B}d(b,A)\}.
\]
It measures the largest mismatch between the two sets. \par\noindent Applications: it compares shapes, contours, point clouds, and approximations produced by geometric algorithms.

\term{Hausdorff Measure}
Hausdorff measure extends the ideas of length, area, and volume to rough or fractal sets. It estimates size by covering a set with very small pieces and adding powers of their diameters. \par\noindent Applications: it measures fractals, irregular boundaries, geometric objects, and sets in data analysis.

\term{Hausdorff Paradox}
The Hausdorff paradox says that a sphere can be split into strange, non-measurable pieces and rotated to make two copies of the sphere, up to a small exceptional set. The result depends on the axiom of choice and does not apply to ordinary measurable pieces. \par\noindent Applications: it clarifies the limits of volume and the role of choice in set theory.

\term{Hausdorff Space}
A topological space is Hausdorff if any two distinct points have disjoint open neighborhoods. This condition ensures that limits of sequences, when they exist in the relevant setting, are unique. \par\noindent Applications: Hausdorff spaces provide the standard setting for analysis, geometry, and the separation of distinct states or configurations.

\term{Heat Equation}
The heat equation $\partial u/\partial t=\alpha\nabla^2u$ describes diffusion of temperature or another quantity through a medium. The constant $\alpha>0$ measures diffusivity, and boundary or initial conditions determine the solution. \par\noindent Applications: it models heat conduction, diffusion of chemicals, smoothing of images, and probabilistic Brownian motion.

\term{Heaviside Step Function}
The Heaviside step function is $H(x)=0$ for $x<0$ and $H(x)=1$ for $x>0$; its value at $0$ is chosen by convention. In the distributional sense, its derivative is the Dirac delta. \par\noindent Applications: it models switching, delayed signals, sudden loads, and piecewise forcing in differential equations.

\term{Hecke Operator}
A Hecke operator is a rule that transforms a modular form while preserving its main symmetry. Its eigenvalues often encode arithmetic information about primes and related equations. \par\noindent Applications: Hecke operators connect modular forms with elliptic curves, prime numbers, and number-theoretic representations.

\term{Hedgehog Space}
A hedgehog space is made by joining many line segments at one common point, like the spikes of a toy hedgehog. \par\noindent Applications: it gives simple examples and counterexamples in topology and metric-space theory.

\term{Heegner Number}
A Heegner number is a positive integer $d$ for which the imaginary quadratic field $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-d})$ has class number $1$. The nine values are $1,2,3,7,11,19,43,67,163$. Class number $1$ means that ideals factor uniquely in the appropriate ring of integers. \par\noindent Applications: Heegner numbers connect algebraic number theory with elliptic curves, modular forms, and explicit Diophantine equations.

\term{Heine--Borel Theorem}
In $\mathbb{R}^n$, a set is compact if and only if it is closed and bounded. Compactness gives strong existence properties: every continuous function on a compact set is bounded and attains its maximum and minimum. \par\noindent Applications: the theorem is used to justify solutions of optimization problems and convergence arguments in analysis.

\term{Heisenberg Group}
The Heisenberg group consists of matrices
\[
\begin{pmatrix}1&x&z\\0&1&y\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad x,y,z\in\mathbb{R}.
\]
It is non-abelian: the order of multiplication matters, and its commutator records the central coordinate. \par\noindent Applications: it models the position--momentum commutation relations of quantum mechanics and appears in harmonic analysis and signal processing.

\term{Helly's Theorem}
Helly's theorem says that a finite collection of convex sets in $\mathbb{R}^d$ has a common point if every subcollection of at most $d+1$ sets has a common point. A global intersection is therefore certified by small local checks. \par\noindent Applications: it supports feasibility tests in convex optimization, geometric databases, and sensor-location problems.

\term{Hensel's Lemma}
Hensel's lemma lifts solutions from modular arithmetic to higher powers of a prime. If $f(a)\equiv0\pmod p$ and $f'(a)\not\equiv0\pmod p$, then the root lifts uniquely to a root modulo $p^k$ for every $k\geq1$. \par\noindent Applications: it solves polynomial congruences, studies $p$-adic numbers, and factors polynomials over local fields.

\term{Hereditary Property}
A hereditary property is a feature that remains true when part of an object is taken away. For graphs, a property is hereditary if every smaller induced graph has it too. \par\noindent Applications: hereditary properties organize graph classes, pattern avoidance, and structural classification.

\term{Hereditary Ring}
A hereditary ring is a ring whose ideals behave as cleanly as independent coordinate pieces: every ideal is a projective module. \par\noindent Applications: hereditary rings simplify module classification and homological algebra.

\term{Hermite Normal Form}
Hermite normal form is a canonical form for an integer matrix obtained by integer-preserving column or row operations. For a full-rank matrix it is triangular with controlled non-negative entries, so it represents the same lattice in a standard way. \par\noindent Applications: it solves integer linear systems, computes lattice bases, and supports integer programming and cryptography.

\term{Hermite Polynomials}
Hermite polynomials form an orthogonal sequence with respect to a Gaussian weight. One common convention is $H_n(x)=(-1)^ne^{x^2}\frac{d^n}{dx^n}e^{-x^2}$. They are eigenfunctions of a second-order differential operator. \par\noindent Applications: they describe the quantum harmonic oscillator, Gaussian random variables, spectral methods, and signal expansions.

\term{Hermitian Form}
A Hermitian form on a complex vector space is a sesquilinear form satisfying $h(u,v)=\overline{h(v,u)}$. If $h(v,v)>0$ for every nonzero $v$, it is a complex inner product. \par\noindent Applications: Hermitian forms define lengths and angles in complex spaces and describe energy, covariance, and quantum-mechanical observables.

\term{Hermitian Matrix}
A complex square matrix is Hermitian if $A=A^*$, its conjugate transpose. The spectral theorem says it has real eigenvalues and an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors. \par\noindent Applications: Hermitian matrices represent covariance and energy matrices, support stable numerical algorithms, and model observables in quantum mechanics.

\term{Hessian}
See \termref{Hessian Matrix}. It records the second-order curvature information of a function. \par\noindent Applications: the Hessian helps identify maxima, minima, saddle points, and the behavior of optimization algorithms.

\term{Hessian Matrix}
For a twice-differentiable scalar function, the Hessian is the matrix of second partial derivatives, $\nabla^2f=(\partial^2f/\partial x_i\partial x_j)$. At a critical point, a positive-definite Hessian indicates a strict local minimum, while a negative-definite one indicates a strict local maximum; an indefinite Hessian indicates a saddle. \par\noindent Applications: Hessians measure curvature in optimization, stability analysis, statistics, and mechanics.

\term{Heuristics}
Heuristics are practical rules or strategies that search for useful solutions without guaranteeing an optimum or a proof of correctness in every case. Their value is often measured by solution quality and computation time. \par\noindent Applications: heuristics guide route planning, scheduling, combinatorial optimization, and search problems that are too large for exhaustive methods.

\term{Hilbert Basis Theorem}
The Hilbert basis theorem states that if $R$ is Noetherian, then the polynomial ring $R[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$ is also Noetherian. Equivalently, every ideal in this ring is finitely generated. \par\noindent Applications: it guarantees finite algebraic descriptions and is a foundation for algebraic geometry, Gröbner-basis methods, and polynomial ideal computation.

\term{Hilbert Curve}
A Hilbert curve is a continuous path that fills a square in the limiting sense. It is built by repeatedly replacing a path with a finer folded version. \par\noindent Applications: Hilbert curves preserve locality in data indexing, image storage, spatial databases, and computer graphics.

\term{Hilbert Polynomial}
A Hilbert polynomial counts how many independent algebraic pieces appear at each large degree in a geometric object. Its degree and leading coefficient reveal the object's dimension and size. \par\noindent Applications: it measures algebraic varieties and supports classification, dimension calculations, and algebraic geometry.

\term{Hilbert--Schmidt Norm}
The Hilbert--Schmidt norm measures the total size of an operator by adding the squared lengths of the outputs of an orthonormal set of inputs. It is the infinite-dimensional analogue of the matrix Frobenius norm. \par\noindent Applications: it measures errors and sizes of operators in quantum theory, integral equations, and numerical analysis.

\term{Hilbert--Schmidt Operator}
A Hilbert--Schmidt operator is an operator whose total squared effect on an orthonormal basis is finite. It is therefore a particularly well-behaved infinite-dimensional transformation. \par\noindent Applications: such operators appear in integral equations, quantum mechanics, statistics, and approximation.

\term{Hilbert's Nullstellensatz}
Hilbert's Nullstellensatz connects polynomial equations with the set of points that solve them. It says, roughly, that the equations vanishing on a solution set contain exactly the information encoded by the original equations after repeated factors are ignored. \par\noindent Applications: it turns geometric solution questions into algebraic calculations and supports symbolic computation.

\term{Hilbert Space}
A Hilbert space is a complete inner-product space. Completeness ensures that Cauchy sequences converge within the space, extending Euclidean geometry to infinite dimensions. \par\noindent Applications: Hilbert spaces are the natural setting for Fourier series, PDEs, least-squares approximation, quantum mechanics, and statistical learning.

\term{Hilbert's Tenth Problem}
Hilbert's tenth problem asked for an algorithm deciding whether an integer-coefficient polynomial equation has an integer solution. Matiyasevich, building on work by Davis, Putnam, and Robinson, proved in 1970 that no such algorithm exists. \par\noindent Applications: the result marks a fundamental limit on automated equation solving and connects number theory with computability.

\term{Hilbert Transform}
The Hilbert transform shifts the phase of each frequency component by approximately $90^\circ$ and is defined, in the principal-value sense, by a singular integral. Combining a signal with its Hilbert transform produces an analytic signal with an instantaneous amplitude and phase. \par\noindent Applications: it is used in communications, envelope detection, radar, speech analysis, and signal demodulation.

\term{Hille--Yosida Theorem}
The Hille--Yosida theorem gives conditions ensuring that an operator generates a continuous time-evolution that does not enlarge errors. \par\noindent Applications: it proves existence and stability for evolution equations, diffusion, control systems, and time-dependent models.

\term{H-index}
The h-index is $h$ when a researcher has at least $h$ papers, each cited at least $h$ times. It combines publication count and citation count into one rough measure. \par\noindent Applications: it is used in research assessment, bibliometrics, and comparisons of publication records.

\term{Hochschild Cohomology}
Hochschild cohomology measures how the multiplication in an algebra can be changed, and what prevents those changes from fitting together consistently. Its second level records small deformations of the algebra. \par\noindent Applications: it studies deformations, noncommutative geometry, algebraic structures, and mathematical physics.

\term{Hodge-Laplacian}
The Hodge--Laplacian is a second-derivative operator on functions or differential forms. Its zero-output objects are the “balanced” or harmonic parts that have no remaining local variation. \par\noindent Applications: it studies shape, harmonic functions, differential equations, and the topology of surfaces.

\term{Hodge Star Operator}
On an oriented Riemannian $n$-manifold, the Hodge star maps a $k$-form to an $(n-k)$-form by comparing their wedge product with the volume form. It defines the codifferential, the adjoint of the exterior derivative, and the Hodge Laplacian. \par\noindent Applications: it expresses divergence, curl, and electromagnetic duality in coordinate-free form.

\term{Hodge Theory}
Hodge theory studies differential forms, cohomology, and harmonic representatives on Riemannian and especially Kähler manifolds. For a compact Kähler manifold, $H^k(X,\mathbb C)=\bigoplus_{p+q=k}H^{p,q}(X)$ relates topology to complex structure. \par\noindent Applications: it extracts geometric information from cohomology and is used in algebraic geometry, topology, and mathematical physics.

\term{Hofstadter's Butterfly}
Hofstadter's butterfly is a striking fractal picture showing the allowed energy levels of electrons in a repeating lattice under a magnetic field. \par\noindent Applications: it reveals the interaction of quantum motion, periodic structure, and magnetic fields.

\term{Hölder's Inequality}
Hölder's inequality states that $\int|fg|\leq\|f\|_p\|g\|_q$ when $p,q\geq1$ and $1/p+1/q=1$. The case $p=q=2$ is the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality. \par\noindent Applications: it bounds integrals and products in probability, Fourier analysis, PDE estimates, and the theory of $L^p$ spaces.

\term{Hölder Space}
A Hölder space contains functions whose changes are controlled by a power of the distance between inputs: $|f(x)-f(y)|\leq C|x-y|^\alpha$. The exponent records how smoothly the function changes. \par\noindent Applications: Hölder spaces measure regularity in differential equations, images, surfaces, and approximation.

\term{Holomorphic Function}
A holomorphic function is complex-differentiable in a neighborhood of every point in its domain. This stronger form of differentiability implies a power-series expansion and strong integral identities. \par\noindent Applications: holomorphic functions model complex potentials, fluid flow, conformal maps, and frequency-domain solutions of differential equations.

\term{Holonomy}
Holonomy is the group of transformations obtained by parallel transporting vectors or other geometric data around closed loops. Nontrivial holonomy measures how curvature causes a vector to return changed. \par\noindent Applications: holonomy detects geometric structure, classifies special Riemannian manifolds, and appears in gauge theory and general relativity.

\term{Homeomorphism}
A homeomorphism is a bijection $f:X\to Y$ such that both $f$ and $f^{-1}$ are continuous. It means that the spaces have the same topology: they can be stretched or bent without cutting or gluing. \par\noindent Applications: homeomorphisms classify shapes in topology and identify when two meshes or configuration spaces have the same qualitative structure.

\term{Hom Functor}
The Hom functor collects all structure-preserving maps from one object to another. It also shows how these maps change when either object is changed. \par\noindent Applications: Hom organizes maps in algebra, geometry, topology, category theory, and the study of extensions.

\term{Homogeneous Coordinate}
Homogeneous coordinates represent a point using a vector whose nonzero scalar multiples describe the same point. This lets ordinary equations describe points at infinity as well as points in a plane. \par\noindent Applications: they support perspective in computer graphics, projective geometry, cameras, and geometric transformations.

\term{Homogeneous Polynomial}
A homogeneous polynomial has the same total degree in every term; for example, $x^2+xy+y^2$ has degree $2$. It satisfies $p(tx)=t^d p(x)$ when its degree is $d$. \par\noindent Applications: homogeneous polynomials describe projective varieties, scaling laws, quadratic forms, and leading terms in approximations.

\term{Homogeneous Space}
A homogeneous space is a space in which every point has the same geometric status: a symmetry can move any point to any other. Spheres are familiar examples. \par\noindent Applications: homogeneous spaces model symmetric geometries, rotations, directions, and spaces in physics.

\term{Homological Algebra}
Homological algebra studies algebraic chains and the patterns of what is lost when objects are connected by maps. It turns complicated relationships into sequences of groups and maps that can be calculated. \par\noindent Applications: it supports algebraic topology, geometry, number theory, and the study of differential equations.

\term{Homology}
Homology assigns algebraic groups $H_n(X)$ to a space to record holes of different dimensions. An $n$-cycle is a closed $n$-dimensional object, and homology identifies cycles that differ by a boundary. \par\noindent Applications: homology detects connected components, loops, and voids in topology and is used in computational topology and data analysis.

\term{Homomorphism}
A homomorphism is a map between algebraic systems that respects their operations. For groups, it satisfies $f(ab)=f(a)f(b)$, so calculations can be carried out before or after applying the map. \par\noindent Applications: homomorphisms simplify algebra, symmetry, coding, and structure-preserving transformations.

\term{Homotopy}
Two maps $f,g:X\to Y$ are homotopic if there is a continuous deformation $F(x,t)$ from $f(x)$ to $g(x)$ as $t$ runs from $0$ to $1$. Homotopy records shape-level information while ignoring continuous bending. \par\noindent Applications: it classifies paths and spaces, supports obstruction theory, and is used in robotics to study collision-free motions.

\term{Homotopy Category}
The homotopy category treats two continuous maps as the same when one can be continuously deformed into the other. It focuses on shape up to bending and stretching rather than exact distances. \par\noindent Applications: it simplifies algebraic topology and the study of spaces up to deformation.

\term{Homotopy Fiber}
The homotopy fiber of a map records not only which points map to a chosen point, but also the paths needed to connect their images to it. It is a flexible version of an ordinary fiber that keeps deformation information. \par\noindent Applications: it detects holes and obstructions in maps and supports algebraic topology and geometric constructions.

\term{Homotopy Group}
A homotopy group records how $n$-dimensional spheres can be mapped into a space and continuously deformed. The first group records loops; higher groups record higher-dimensional holes. \par\noindent Applications: homotopy groups classify shapes and support the study of spheres, surfaces, and topological obstructions.

\term{Hopf Algebra}
A Hopf algebra is an algebra with extra rules that describe how its elements can be combined, separated, and reversed. It generalizes the algebra of a symmetry group. \par\noindent Applications: Hopf algebras organize quantum symmetries, transformations, combinatorics, and tensor calculations.

\term{Hopf Fibration}
The Hopf fibration is a fiber bundle $S^1\to S^3\to S^2$: every point of the 2-sphere has a circular fiber, and these fibers are linked in $S^3$. It is a central example of a nontrivial bundle. \par\noindent Applications: it illustrates fiber bundles, characteristic classes, and the topology of spheres and appears in geometry and theoretical physics.

\term{Hopfian Group}
A Hopfian group is a group that cannot be reproduced by collapsing it to a genuinely smaller quotient. Every finite group has this property. \par\noindent Applications: Hopfian groups help study infinite groups, self-similarity, and when algebraic information can be lost.

\term{Hopf Link}
The Hopf link consists of two circles linked together once, like two chain links. Neither circle can be separated from the other without cutting one. \par\noindent Applications: it is a basic example in knot theory, topology, molecular modeling, and linked fields.

\term{Hopf--Rinow Theorem}
For a connected Riemannian manifold, the Hopf--Rinow theorem equates metric completeness, geodesic completeness, and the existence of minimizing geodesics between points. Closed and bounded metric balls are then compact. \par\noindent Applications: it provides the existence and extension results needed in Riemannian geometry and geometric mechanics.

\term{Horned Sphere}
A horned sphere is a distorted three-dimensional sphere whose surface grows long interlocking horns. Although it has the same basic topological shape as a sphere, its surface is not smoothly embedded. \par\noindent Applications: it shows that topological sameness and smooth geometric behavior can be very different.

\term{H-space}
An H-space is a space with a continuous way to combine two points, behaving like addition up to continuous deformation. It need not have exact inverses or an exact identity. \par\noindent Applications: H-spaces provide examples and tools in homotopy theory and the study of loop spaces.

\term{Hurewicz Theorem}
The Hurewicz theorem says that, for a sufficiently connected space, its first genuinely nontrivial hole can be measured in two different ways and the measurements agree. \par\noindent Applications: it translates difficult deformation questions into more calculable algebraic-topology questions.

\term{Hurwitz Zeta Function}
For suitable $s$ and $a>0$, the Hurwitz zeta function is $\zeta(s,a)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(n+a)^{-s}$. It extends meromorphically in $s$ and reduces to the Riemann zeta function when $a=1$. \par\noindent Applications: it appears in analytic number theory, spectral sums, lattice problems, and formulas involving the Gamma function.

\term{Hyperbolic Cosine (cosh)}
The hyperbolic cosine is the average of exponential growth and decay: $\cosh x=(e^x+e^{-x})/2$. It satisfies $\cosh^2x-\sinh^2x=1$. \par\noindent Applications: it describes hanging cables, special relativity, differential equations, and wave shapes.

\term{Hyperbolic Equation}
A hyperbolic equation is a differential equation describing effects that travel, such as waves. Information moves along characteristic paths, much as a sound wave moves through air. \par\noindent Applications: hyperbolic equations model sound, light, water waves, traffic, elasticity, and relativity.

\term{Hyperbolic Function}
Hyperbolic functions are built from the exponential function and play for a hyperbola a role similar to that played by sine and cosine for a circle. The main examples are $\sinh$, $\cosh$, and $\tanh$. \par\noindent Applications: they describe hanging cables, growth and decay, relativity, differential equations, and neural networks.

\term{Hyperbolic Geometry}
Hyperbolic geometry is a non-Euclidean geometry of constant negative curvature. Through a point not on a line there are infinitely many non-intersecting lines, and the angles of a triangle sum to less than $\pi$. \par\noindent Applications: it models navigation on negatively curved spaces, geometric group theory, conformal mapping, and some network and graphics problems.

\term{Hyperbolic Group}
A hyperbolic group is a finitely generated group whose Cayley graph is negatively curved in Gromov's coarse sense: geodesic triangles are uniformly thin. This property controls the large-scale geometry of the group without requiring a smooth metric. \par\noindent Applications: hyperbolic groups connect algebra with geometry, topology, algorithms, and the study of groups acting on curved spaces.

\term{Hyperbolic Paraboloid}
A hyperbolic paraboloid is a saddle-shaped surface. It curves upward in one direction and downward in the perpendicular direction. \par\noindent Applications: it appears in roofs, structural design, optics, computer graphics, and the study of curved surfaces.

\term{Hyperbolic Sine (sinh)}
The hyperbolic sine is $\sinh x=(e^x-e^{-x})/2$. It measures the odd part of exponential growth and decay and has a graph shaped like a stretched S. \par\noindent Applications: it solves differential equations and models waves, hanging cables, and relativistic motion.

\term{Hyperbolic Space}
Hyperbolic space is the standard complete simply connected geometry of constant sectional curvature $-1$. The Poincaré disk and upper half-plane are conformal models, so angles are represented correctly even though distances are distorted in the picture. \par\noindent Applications: it provides models for non-Euclidean geometry, complex analysis, relativity, and geometric group theory.

\term{Hyperbolic Tangent (tanh)}
The hyperbolic tangent is $\tanh x=\sinh x/\cosh x$. Its values stay between $-1$ and $1$, even when $x$ becomes very large. \par\noindent Applications: it models saturation and is used in differential equations, probability, and neural-network activation.

\term{Hypercube}
A hypercube is the $n$-dimensional analogue of a square or cube. It has two opposite copies of an $(n-1)$-dimensional cube joined in each coordinate direction. \par\noindent Applications: hypercubes organize binary data, networks, algorithms, geometry, and high-dimensional visualization.

\term{Hyperelliptic Curve}
A hyperelliptic curve is a curved algebraic shape that can be mapped two-to-one onto a line. It extends the idea of an elliptic curve to shapes with more holes. \par\noindent Applications: hyperelliptic curves appear in number theory, cryptography, coding, and algebraic geometry.

\term{Hypergeometric Function}
The hypergeometric function is a flexible function defined by a power series whose coefficients follow a simple product pattern. Many familiar functions and polynomial families occur as special cases. \par\noindent Applications: it solves differential equations and appears in waves, probability, geometry, physics, and combinatorics.

\term{Hypergraph}
A hypergraph generalizes a graph by allowing a hyperedge to contain any number of vertices rather than exactly two. It represents group relationships that cannot be reduced to pairwise links. \par\noindent Applications: hypergraphs model database relations, co-authorship, chemical interactions, set systems, and higher-order networks.

\term{Hyperplane}
A hyperplane is an $(n-1)$-dimensional affine or linear subspace of an $n$-dimensional space. In $\mathbb{R}^3$ it is an ordinary plane, and it can be written as $a\cdot x=b$ with $a\ne0$. \par\noindent Applications: hyperplanes define linear decision boundaries, constraints, projections, and separating surfaces in optimization and machine learning.

\term{Hyperplane Arrangement}
A hyperplane arrangement is a finite collection of flat dividing surfaces in a space. The pieces left after removing those surfaces form a pattern that can be studied combinatorially and geometrically. \par\noindent Applications: arrangements support geometry, optimization, robotics, braid groups, and the analysis of regions cut by constraints.

\term{Hyperreal Number}
The hyperreal numbers extend $\mathbb{R}$ with infinitesimal and infinitely large numbers while preserving first-order statements through the transfer principle. Nonstandard analysis uses them to give a rigorous meaning to calculations with infinitesimals. \par\noindent Applications: they provide an alternative foundation for limits, derivatives, integrals, and probability.

\term{Hypersphere}
A hypersphere is the set of points in $\mathbb{R}^n$ at a fixed distance $r$ from a center $c$, namely $\{x:\|x-c\|=r\}$. It has dimension $n-1$; the circle and ordinary sphere are the cases $n=2$ and $n=3$. \par\noindent Applications: hyperspheres occur in geometry, statistics, clustering, optimization, and high-dimensional probability.

\term{Hypersurface}
A hypersurface is a surface whose dimension is one less than the space containing it. Ordinary surfaces in three-dimensional space are examples. \par\noindent Applications: hypersurfaces describe boundaries, level sets, interfaces, walls, and constraint surfaces.

\term{Hypervolume}
Hypervolume is the $n$-dimensional measure of a region in $\mathbb{R}^n$, especially for $n>3$. For example, an $n$-dimensional ball of radius $r$ has volume $\pi^{n/2}r^n/\Gamma(n/2+1)$. \par\noindent Applications: hypervolume measures feasible regions, uncertainty sets, and dominated regions in high-dimensional optimization.
