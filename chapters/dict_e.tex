\chapter*{E}
\label{chap:e}

\term{Cayley Graph}
A Cayley graph turns a group into a network: each vertex is a group element, and edges show multiplication by chosen generators. For example, the Cayley graph of $\mathbb Z^n$ with standard generators is an infinite grid. \par\noindent Applications: Cayley graphs visualize algebraic structure and support network analysis, symmetry studies, and geometric group theory.

\term{Eccentricity}
For a conic section, eccentricity measures how far the shape is from a circle: for an ellipse with semiaxes $a\ge b$, it is $e=\sqrt{1-b^2/a^2}$. A graph-theory eccentricity is different: it is the greatest shortest-path distance from one vertex to any other. \par\noindent Applications: conic eccentricity classifies ellipses, parabolas, and hyperbolas, while graph eccentricity identifies central and peripheral locations in networks.

\term{Edge}
An edge is a connection between two vertices in a graph; it may have a direction or a weight. \par\noindent Applications: edges represent roads, communication links, relationships, and transitions between states.

\term{Eigenvalue}
An eigenvalue tells how much a transformation stretches or reverses a special direction. If $Tv=\lambda v$ for a nonzero vector $v$, then $\lambda$ is the eigenvalue and $v$ is its eigenvector. \origin{The idea grew from the study of linear equations and was developed by mathematicians including Leonhard Euler and Augustin-Louis Cauchy to understand special directions of transformations.} \par\noindent Applications: eigenvalues reveal vibration modes, long-term system behavior, important data directions, and stability.

\term{Eigenvalue Problem}
The eigenvalue problem seeks scalars $\lambda$ and nonzero vectors $v$ satisfying $Av=\lambda v$. \par\noindent Applications: eigenvalues identify natural frequencies, growth rates, stability, and important directions in data.

\term{Eigenvector}
An eigenvector of $T$ is a nonzero vector $v$ satisfying $Tv=\lambda v$; the transformation changes its size or direction only by the scalar $\lambda$. \par\noindent Applications: eigenvectors describe vibration modes, principal components, quantum states, and long-term behavior of dynamical systems.

\term{Eilenberg--MacLane Space}
An Eilenberg--MacLane space is a space designed to have one chosen kind of hole and no others. For a group $G$ and level $n$, the space $K(G,n)$ stores exactly the information represented by $G$ at level $n$. \origin{Samuel Eilenberg and Saunders Mac Lane introduced these spaces to represent cohomology classes through maps and to simplify algebraic topology.} \par\noindent Applications: these spaces turn cohomology questions into questions about maps and help organize obstruction theory.

\term{Eilenberg--Steenrod Axioms}
The Eilenberg--Steenrod axioms are rules that a reliable theory of holes should obey. They require that the measurements behave sensibly under continuous deformation, cutting, joining, and adding separate spaces. \origin{Samuel Eilenberg and Norman Steenrod formulated these rules in the mid-twentieth century to give topology a common foundation for measuring holes.} \par\noindent Applications: the axioms organize algebraic topology and let mathematicians compare different theories of holes.

\term{Eisenstein Series}
An Eisenstein series is an infinite sum with strong repeating symmetry. Its coefficients often count divisors, so a function built from an analytic-looking sum also carries arithmetic information. \origin{Gotthold Eisenstein studied these series in the nineteenth century to connect complex functions with number patterns and modular symmetry.} \par\noindent Applications: Eisenstein series connect modular forms, elliptic curves, Fourier expansions, and the arithmetic of integers.

\term{Elementary Function}
A function built from polynomials, exponentials, logarithms, trigonometric functions, and their inverses using finitely many operations and compositions. \par\noindent Applications: elementary functions form the standard vocabulary of calculus, modeling, engineering, and scientific computation.

\term{Elementary Symmetric Polynomials}
Elementary symmetric polynomials add products of different variables in every possible way. For example, $e_1=x_1+x_2$ and $e_2=x_1x_2$. They do not change when the variables are rearranged. \par\noindent Applications: they connect polynomial coefficients with roots and simplify equations that are unchanged by reordering.

\term{Ellipse}
An ellipse is the set of points whose sum of distances to two fixed foci is constant. A circle is the special case in which the foci coincide. \par\noindent Applications: ellipses model planetary orbits, optical mirrors, acoustic reflectors, and uncertainty regions in statistics.

\term{Elliptic Curve}
A smooth curve of genus 1 with a chosen point at infinity. Over a field of characteristic different from $2$ and $3$, it can be written as $y^2=x^3+ax+b$ with $4a^3+27b^2\ne0$. Its points form an abelian group under a geometric addition rule. \par\noindent Applications: elliptic curves are used in public-key cryptography, integer factorization, and the study of rational solutions to equations.

\term{Elliptic Curve Cryptography}
Elliptic curve cryptography protects messages using points on a special curve. It is easy to combine a point with itself many times, but very difficult to reverse that process when the multiplier is hidden. \par\noindent Applications: it provides secure key exchange, digital signatures, and smaller keys for phones, browsers, and other devices.

\term{Elliptic Function}
A meromorphic function on the complex plane with two independent periods, such as the Weierstrass $\wp$ function. \par\noindent Applications: elliptic functions describe periodic phenomena in two directions and appear in complex analysis, mechanics, and the geometry of elliptic curves.

\term{Elliptic Integral}
An elliptic integral is an integral such as $\int R(x,\sqrt{P(x)})\,dx$, where $P$ is a cubic or quartic and $R$ is rational; it is not generally expressible using elementary functions. \par\noindent Applications: elliptic integrals calculate pendulum periods, arc lengths, planetary motion, and electromagnetic quantities.

\term{Elliptic PDE}
An elliptic PDE has a principal part that behaves like a positive-definite quadratic form; Laplace's equation is the standard example. Under suitable boundary conditions, elliptic equations smooth their solutions. \par\noindent Applications: they model steady temperature, electrostatics, elasticity, fluid potential, and image smoothing.

\term{Ellsberg Paradox}
The Ellsberg paradox shows that people often prefer a gamble whose chances are known to one whose chances are unclear, even when the possible rewards look similar. It separates measurable risk from uncertainty about the probabilities themselves. \par\noindent Applications: it informs decision theory, insurance, economics, finance, and experiments on human judgment.

\term{Embedding Theorem (Nash)}
Nash's embedding theorem says that every suitably smooth curved space can be placed inside some ordinary Euclidean space without changing its measured distances. A space defined abstractly can therefore be represented as a possibly higher-dimensional shape. \par\noindent Applications: it links intrinsic geometry with coordinates and supports geometric modeling and visualization.

\term{Endomorphism}
An endomorphism is a structure-preserving map from an object to itself. \par\noindent Applications: endomorphisms describe repeated transformations, linear systems, dynamical updates, and symmetries of algebraic structures.

\term{Entire Function}
An entire function is a complex-valued function that is smooth and well-defined everywhere in the complex plane. Polynomials, $e^z$, $\sin z$, and $\cos z$ are examples. \par\noindent Applications: entire functions describe growth, oscillation, and zeros in complex analysis, differential equations, and number theory.

\term{Enumeration}
Enumeration is the process of listing objects or assigning them natural-number labels. \par\noindent Applications: enumeration generates combinatorial objects, indexes data, tests algorithms, and distinguishes countable from uncountable sets.

\term{Envelope}
An envelope is a curve tangent to each member of a family of curves, at least locally. \par\noindent Applications: envelopes describe wavefronts, caustics in optics, reachable boundaries, and families of solutions to differential equations.

\term{Epimorphism}
In category theory, an epimorphism is a morphism $f$ such that $g\circ f=h\circ f$ implies $g=h$. It need not be surjective in every category, although epimorphisms of sets are surjective. \par\noindent Applications: epimorphisms formalize quotient and “onto-like” constructions in algebra and geometry.

\term{Equality}
Equality means that two expressions denote the same object; it is reflexive, symmetric, and transitive. \par\noindent Applications: equality is the basic relation used in equations, symbolic computation, formal proof, and program verification.

\term{Equalizer}
An equalizer collects the inputs on which two rules give the same result. For functions on a set, it is simply $\{a\in A:f(a)=g(a)\}$. \par\noindent Applications: equalizers identify agreement between rules and are useful in algebra, logic, and category theory.

\term{Equivalence of Categories}
Two categories are equivalent when they may use different names and presentations but preserve the same mathematical relationships. Each can be translated into the other and back without losing the information that matters. \par\noindent Applications: equivalence lets mathematicians replace complicated settings by simpler ones in algebra, geometry, topology, and computer science.

\term{Equivalence Relation}
An equivalence relation says when two objects should count as the same for a particular purpose. It is reflexive, symmetric, and transitive, and it divides a set into groups of mutually equivalent objects. \par\noindent Applications: equivalence relations create useful classes such as remainders, shapes up to deformation, and objects with the same structure.

\term{Equivalent}
Two objects are equivalent when they are related by an equivalence relation; two statements are logically equivalent when each implies the other. \par\noindent Applications: equivalence lets mathematicians replace a difficult object or statement with a simpler one having the same relevant behavior.

\term{Erdős--Ko--Rado Theorem}
The Erdős--Ko--Rado theorem says that a large collection of equally sized subsets, where every two overlap, is largest when all of them contain one fixed element. It gives a precise limit on how many pairwise-intersecting choices are possible. \par\noindent Applications: it studies intersecting families in combinatorics, scheduling, coding, and selection problems.

\term{Erdős--Szekeres}
The Erdős--Szekeres theorem says that a sufficiently long list of distinct numbers must contain a long increasing part or a long decreasing part. Disorder cannot continue forever without one of these two patterns appearing. \par\noindent Applications: it gives guarantees in combinatorics, pattern detection, algorithms, and the study of ordered data.

\term{Ergodicity}
Ergodicity is the property that long-run averages along almost every trajectory equal averages over the whole invariant state space. \par\noindent Applications: it justifies replacing repeated time measurements by ensemble averages in physics, probability, and dynamical simulations.

\term{Ergodic Theorem (Birkhoff)}
Birkhoff's ergodic theorem says that, in a sufficiently mixed system, the average seen by following one point for a long time equals the average over the whole system. Long observation can therefore reveal the system-wide average. \par\noindent Applications: it supports statistical mechanics, dynamical systems, simulation, and the use of time averages in probability.

\term{Ergodic Theory}
Ergodic theory studies long-term statistical behavior in systems that preserve a measure. \par\noindent Applications: it connects time averages with ensemble averages in statistical mechanics, dynamical systems, information theory, and data modeling.

\term{Essential Singularity}
An essential singularity is an isolated singularity that is neither removable nor a pole. Near it, the function behaves extremely irregularly and approaches almost every complex value. \par\noindent Applications: essential singularities classify complex functions and determine local behavior in contour integration and complex models.

\term{Étale Cohomology}
Étale cohomology is a way to measure holes in algebraic shapes using algebraic neighborhoods rather than ordinary open sets. It is designed to work well over finite fields and with number-theoretic symmetries. \par\noindent Applications: it helps count solutions of equations over finite fields and study algebraic varieties, primes, and Galois symmetries.

\term{Étale Morphism}
An étale morphism is a map between algebraic shapes that locally behaves like a smooth map with no extra dimension or branching. Near each point it resembles a collection of separate, well-behaved copies of the target. \par\noindent Applications: étale maps define algebraic coverings and provide the local pieces used in arithmetic geometry and étale cohomology.

\term{Euclidean Domain}
An Euclidean domain is an integral domain with a division algorithm based on a size function. It is therefore a principal ideal domain and a unique factorization domain. \par\noindent Applications: Euclidean domains support efficient gcd calculations and factorization in integers and polynomial rings.

\term{Euclid's Algorithm}
Euclid's algorithm repeatedly divides with remainders to compute the greatest common divisor of two integers. \par\noindent Applications: it supports fraction reduction, modular inverses, polynomial arithmetic, and cryptographic algorithms.

\term{Euclid's Theorem}
There are infinitely many prime numbers. Euclid's proof multiplies any proposed finite list of primes and adds one, producing a number with a prime divisor absent from the list. \par\noindent Applications: the theorem establishes the basic supply of prime building blocks used in arithmetic and cryptography.

\term{Euler Characteristic}
The Euler characteristic is a single number that summarizes a shape's pattern of holes. For a polyhedron it is $V-E+F$, the number of vertices minus edges plus faces; for many shapes it remains unchanged under bending or stretching. \par\noindent Applications: it helps classify surfaces, check geometric constructions, and connect topology with curvature.

\term{Eulerian Circuit}
An Eulerian circuit is a route through a network that uses every edge exactly once and returns to its starting point. A connected graph has one exactly when every vertex has even degree. \par\noindent Applications: Eulerian circuits model inspection routes, street sweeping, circuit testing, and the Chinese postman problem.

\term{Eulerian Graph}
A connected graph is Eulerian if it has a closed walk using every edge exactly once; equivalently, every vertex has even degree. \par\noindent Applications: Eulerian graphs model inspection routes, street sweeping, circuit testing, and the Chinese postman problem.

\term{Eulerian Path}
An Eulerian path travels along every edge of a graph exactly once. If it finishes where it started, it is an Eulerian circuit. A connected graph has such a circuit exactly when every vertex has even degree. \par\noindent Applications: Eulerian paths plan routes that inspect roads, streets, wires, or network links without repeating an edge.

\term{Euler--Lagrange Equation}
The Euler--Lagrange equation $\partial L/\partial f-d(\partial L/\partial f')/dx=0$ is satisfied by functions that make a variational quantity stationary. \par\noindent Applications: it gives the equations of motion in mechanics and is used in optics, optimal control, and PDEs.

\term{Euler--Maclaurin Formula}
The Euler--Maclaurin formula improves the approximation of a sum by an integral. It adds endpoint and derivative corrections, so a discrete calculation can be estimated accurately from continuous information. \par\noindent Applications: it estimates large sums, improves numerical integration, and studies errors and asymptotic behavior.

\term{Euler's Formula (Polyhedra)}
Euler's formula says that a convex polyhedron has $V-E+F=2$, where $V$, $E$, and $F$ count vertices, edges, and faces. The same relationship holds for a connected planar drawing when its outside region is counted as a face. \par\noindent Applications: it checks maps and polyhedral models and helps connect graph structure with the topology of surfaces.

\term{Euler's Identity}
Euler's identity $e^{i\pi}+1=0$ follows from $e^{i\theta}=\cos\theta+i\sin\theta$ at $\theta=\pi$. It connects exponential, trigonometric, and complex notation. \par\noindent Applications: this relationship powers Fourier analysis, alternating-current calculations, signal processing, and quantum mechanics.

\term{Euler's Number}
Euler's number is the transcendental constant $e=\lim_{n\to\infty}(1+1/n)^n\approx2.71828$, the base of the natural logarithm. \par\noindent Applications: $e^x$ models continuous growth and decay, compound interest, radioactive decay, and probability distributions.

\term{Euler's Theorem}
(Number theory) If $\gcd(a,n)=1$, then $a^{\varphi(n)}\equiv1\pmod n$. \par\noindent Applications: Euler's theorem supports modular inverses, primality tests, and the RSA cryptosystem.

\term{Euler Totient Function}
The Euler totient function $\phi(n)$ counts the integers from $1$ to $n$ that share no factor with $n$ except $1$. For a prime $p$, $\phi(p)=p-1$, and if $a$ is coprime to $n$, then $a^{\phi(n)}\equiv1\pmod n$. \par\noindent Applications: it supports modular arithmetic, primality tests, cryptography, and counting rotations.

\term{Event}
An event is a subset of the sample space to which a probability is assigned. \par\noindent Applications: events represent outcomes of experiments, alerts in monitoring systems, medical conditions, and statements tested by data.

\term{Exact Sequence}
An exact sequence is a sequence of homomorphisms in which the image of each map equals the kernel of the next. \par\noindent Applications: exact sequences track information through algebraic constructions and are used in homology, differential equations, and extension problems.

\term{Excision}
Excision says that removing a portion safely hidden inside another portion does not change the holes measured outside it. This lets a complicated space be simplified before its topology is calculated. \par\noindent Applications: excision makes homology computations manageable and supports the Mayer--Vietoris method.

\term{Existence}
Existence means that at least one object satisfies a condition, written with the quantifier $\exists$. \par\noindent Applications: existence theorems guarantee that a model, solution, equilibrium, or optimum actually exists before it is computed.

\term{Existence and Uniqueness Theorem}
An existence and uniqueness theorem gives conditions that guarantee a problem has a solution and that no two allowed solutions can differ. For an initial-value differential equation, suitable regularity of the rule usually gives one nearby solution through the starting point. \par\noindent Applications: it justifies predictions from differential equations and distinguishes a well-defined model from one with ambiguity.

\term{Expectation (Probability)}
The expectation of a random quantity is its long-run average value. For a finite list of outcomes, multiply each outcome by its probability and add the results. \par\noindent Applications: expectation predicts average cost, gain, measurement, risk, and behavior over many repetitions.

\term{Expected Value}
The expected value $\mathbb E[X]=\int x\,dP$ is the probability-weighted average of a random variable. \par\noindent Applications: expected values describe long-run averages in risk, finance, statistical estimation, games, and decision-making.

\term{Explicit Formula}
A formula is explicit when it directly gives the quantity of interest rather than defining it indirectly or recursively. \par\noindent Applications: explicit formulas allow direct evaluation, symbolic analysis, and efficient numerical implementation.

\term{Exponential Distribution}
The exponential distribution has density $f(x)=\lambda e^{-\lambda x}$ for $x\ge0$ and models waiting times between events of a Poisson process. \par\noindent Applications: it describes service times, radioactive decay, reliability, queueing, and time-to-failure models.

\term{Exponential Function}
The exponential function $e^x$ describes continuous proportional growth and satisfies $(e^x)'=e^x$ and $e^{a+b}=e^ae^b$. It starts at $1$ and grows by an amount equal to its current value. \par\noindent Applications: exponentials model population growth, decay, interest, cooling, waves, and probability.

\term{Exponential Map (Lie Theory)}
The exponential map turns an infinitesimal motion into a finite motion. For a matrix $X$, it is defined by the series $\exp(X)=I+X+X^2/2!+\cdots$, and it describes continuous motion generated by $X$. \par\noindent Applications: it models rotations, rigid-body motion, robotics, differential equations, and continuous symmetries.

\term{Extension}
An extension is a larger mathematical structure that contains a given smaller one. For fields, $K/F$ means that every element of $F$ is also an element of $K$, while $K$ may contain additional numbers. \par\noindent Applications: extensions enlarge number systems and allow equations to have roots that were missing before.

\term{Extension Field}
An extension field is a larger field containing a smaller field. It adds new numbers while keeping the old addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division rules consistent. \par\noindent Applications: extension fields supply roots of polynomials and are central to algebraic equations, geometry, and coding theory.

\term{Exterior Algebra}
The exterior algebra creates an antisymmetric product of vectors: swapping two factors changes the sign, and using the same vector twice gives zero. This makes the product naturally represent an oriented area, volume, or higher-dimensional size. \par\noindent Applications: it describes areas and volumes, differential forms, orientation, determinants, and geometry.

\term{Exterior Derivative}
The exterior derivative is a rule that turns a differential form into the next-degree form describing its change. Applying it twice gives zero: $d^2=0$. \par\noindent Applications: it unifies gradient, curl, and divergence and supports calculus on curved spaces and conservation laws.

\term{Exterior Product}
The exterior product combines directed quantities while remembering orientation. It is antisymmetric, so $dx\wedge dy=-dy\wedge dx$, and it represents an oriented area or higher-dimensional volume. \par\noindent Applications: it is used for determinants, differential forms, flux, integration, and geometry.

\term{Ext Functor}
The Ext functor measures ways one algebraic object can be built as an extension of another, including ways that are not obviously split into separate pieces. Its first level records short exact sequences and higher levels record deeper obstructions. \par\noindent Applications: Ext is used in homological algebra, module classification, algebraic geometry, and cohomology.

\term{Extremal Graph Theory}
Extremal graph theory asks how many connections a graph can have while avoiding a chosen pattern. It finds sharp limits and describes the graphs that reach them. \par\noindent Applications: it studies network design, forbidden patterns, communication links, and large combinatorial systems.

\term{Extreme Value Theorem}
The extreme value theorem says that a continuous function on a closed bounded interval actually reaches both its highest and lowest values. It cannot keep getting closer to an extreme without attaining it. \par\noindent Applications: it guarantees best and worst cases in optimization, geometry, engineering, and calculus.

\term{Extremum}
A maximum or minimum is a value where a function is larger or smaller than nearby values, or than all values in its domain. \par\noindent Applications: extrema help find best designs, lowest costs, highest profits, and stable states.
