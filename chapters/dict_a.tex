\chapter*{A}
\label{chap:a}

\term{Abelian Category}
An abelian category is a setting where objects can be added and where kernels, cokernels, images, and exact sequences behave much as they do for vector spaces. Abelian groups, modules, and sheaves of abelian groups are standard examples. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} abelian categories provide the language for homological algebra, which studies how algebraic structures fit together in chains and complexes.

\term{Abelian Group}
A group is abelian if its operation is commutative: $ab=ba$ for all elements $a$ and $b$. The integers under addition and vectors under addition are examples. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} abelian groups model quantities that can be combined in either order, including charges, displacements, and modular arithmetic.

\term{Abel's Theorem}
One form of Abel's theorem says that if a power series converges at a boundary point, its sum approaches the value of the series as the point is approached from inside its circle of convergence. Abel also proved that the general polynomial equation of degree five cannot be solved by radicals. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} the power-series result is used in complex analysis and Fourier methods; the quintic result explains limits of formula-based equation solving.

\term{Abilene Paradox}
The Abilene paradox occurs when a group chooses an action that most members privately dislike because each person incorrectly assumes that everyone else supports it. It is a social, rather than mathematical, paradox. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} it helps explain poor decisions in organizations and motivates anonymous polling and explicit discussion of individual preferences.

\term{Absolute Continuity}
A function on an interval is absolutely continuous if small total lengths of disjoint intervals always produce a small total change in the function. Equivalently, $f(x)=f(a)+\int_a^x g(t)\,dt$ for some integrable function $g$. For measures, $\mu\ll\nu$ means that every $\nu$-null set is also a $\mu$-null set. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} absolute continuity justifies differentiation and integration and expresses the existence of a probability density.

\term{Absolute Convergence}
A series $\sum a_n$ is absolutely convergent when $\sum |a_n|$ converges. Absolute convergence guarantees ordinary convergence and allows the terms to be rearranged without changing the sum. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} it gives reliable convergence tests in numerical computation, power series, and Fourier analysis.

\term{Absolutely Continuous}
A function or measure is absolutely continuous when it has the property described by \term{Absolute Continuity}. For functions, this is stronger than ordinary continuity and implies differentiability almost everywhere. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} it is used in probability densities, signal reconstruction, and the fundamental theorem of calculus.

\term{Absorbing Element}
An element $z$ is absorbing for an operation if $z*x=x*z=z$ for every $x$. Zero is absorbing for multiplication because $0x=x0=0$. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} absorbing states in Markov chains and failure states in algorithms use the same idea: once reached, the state cannot be changed by later operations.

\term{Abstract Algebra}
Abstract algebra studies systems such as groups, rings, fields, and modules by their rules rather than by the nature of their elements. The same structure can therefore describe numbers, symmetries, matrices, or transformations. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} abstract algebra underlies cryptography, coding theory, error correction, and the symmetry analysis of physical systems.

\term{Accumulation Point}
A point $p$ is an accumulation point of a set $S$ if every neighborhood of $p$ contains a point of $S$ different from $p$. For example, $0$ is an accumulation point of $(0,1)$. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} accumulation points describe limits, continuity, convergence, and the boundaries of data or geometric sets.

\term{Adaptive Mesh Refinement}
Adaptive mesh refinement changes a computational grid by adding small cells where the current error is large and using larger cells where the solution is smooth. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} it saves computation in simulations of fluid flow, weather, structural stress, and electromagnetic fields while preserving accuracy near sharp features.

\term{Additive Inverse}
The additive inverse of $a$ is the element $-a$ satisfying $a+(-a)=0$, where $0$ is the additive identity. For example, the inverse of $7$ is $-7$. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} additive inverses are used to solve equations, define subtraction, balance physical quantities, and compute differences in data.

\term{Adjoint}
For a linear map $A$ between inner-product spaces, its adjoint $A^*$ is defined by $\langle Ax,y\rangle=\langle x,A^*y\rangle$. For a real matrix it is the transpose, and for a complex matrix it is the conjugate transpose. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} adjoints appear in least-squares fitting, optimization, quantum mechanics, and the reverse pass of automatic differentiation.

\term{Adjunction}
An adjunction is a pair of constructions $F$ and $G$, written $F\dashv G$, for which maps from $F(X)$ to $Y$ correspond naturally to maps from $X$ to $G(Y)$. It formalizes the idea that two operations are best understood as opposite directions of the same relationship. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} adjunctions organize free constructions, optimization duality, logic, and many transformations in algebra and computer science.

\term{Affine Connection}
An affine connection is a rule for differentiating vector fields along curves on a smooth manifold. It defines parallel transport and geodesics, the manifold's generalization of straight lines. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} connections are used to describe motion and curvature in differential geometry and general relativity.

\term{Affine Variety}
An affine variety is the set of common solutions of polynomial equations in affine space, usually with a specified algebraic structure. For example, $x^2+y^2=1$ defines a circle over the real numbers. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} affine varieties model solution sets in algebraic geometry and appear in robotics, control theory, and algebraic statistics.

\term{Albanese}
The Albanese variety of a suitable algebraic variety $X$ is an abelian variety that records global information about maps from $X$ into abelian varieties. For a smooth projective curve, it agrees with the curve's Jacobian. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} Albanese varieties connect geometry with integrals, topology, and the study of rational points on algebraic varieties.

\term{Algebra}
An algebra is a vector space equipped with a multiplication that is bilinear, so multiplication distributes over addition and respects scalar multiplication. The word also names the broader study of structures defined by operations and equations. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} algebras describe matrices, polynomial systems, symmetries, and models in physics and engineering.

\term{Algebraic Closure}
An algebraic closure $\overline{k}$ of a field $k$ is a larger field in which every polynomial has a root and every element satisfies a polynomial equation over $k$. Every field has an algebraic closure, unique up to isomorphism. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} algebraic closures allow polynomial equations to be solved in a common setting and are essential in Galois theory and algebraic geometry.

\term{Algebraic Extension}
A field extension $K/F$ is algebraic if every element of $K$ is a root of a nonzero polynomial with coefficients in $F$. The extension $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})/\mathbb{Q}$ is an example. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} algebraic extensions describe how number systems grow when equations acquire new solutions.

\term{Algebraic Number}
An algebraic number is a complex number that satisfies a nonzero polynomial equation with rational coefficients. Numbers such as $\sqrt{2}$ are algebraic; $\pi$ and $e$ are transcendental. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} algebraic numbers are central to number theory, exact computation, and the study of polynomial equations.

\term{Algebraic Structure}
An algebraic structure is a set together with operations and rules, such as a group, ring, field, or lattice. The rules determine what calculations are allowed. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} algebraic structures provide reusable models for arithmetic, symmetry, logic, coding, and computation.

\term{Algorithm}
An algorithm is a finite, precise procedure that turns input into output through specified steps. An algorithm may be studied for correctness, running time, and memory use. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} algorithms power search engines, encryption, numerical simulation, data analysis, and everyday software.

\term{Allais Paradox}
The Allais paradox is a pattern of choices between gambles that conflicts with the independence principle of expected-utility theory. People often prefer a guaranteed prize in one comparison but then prefer a risky option in a closely related comparison. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} it influences models of decision-making, economics, finance, and behavioral data analysis.

\term{Almost Everywhere}
A property holds almost everywhere if it fails only on a set of measure zero. Two functions that differ only on such a set are treated as equal in many integration theories. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} the phrase is fundamental in probability, statistics, signal processing, and the theory of differential equations.

\term{Almost Sure Convergence}
Random variables $X_n$ converge almost surely to $X$ if $X_n(\omega)\to X(\omega)$ for all outcomes except possibly a set of probability zero. This is a strong form of probabilistic convergence. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} it describes the long-run behavior of estimators, averages, simulations, and stochastic processes.

\term{Alternating Group}
The alternating group $A_n$ consists of the even permutations of $n$ objects, meaning permutations made from an even number of swaps. It is a normal subgroup of the symmetric group $S_n$ and is simple for $n\ge5$. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} alternating groups are important in the theory of polynomial equations, symmetry, and the limits of solving equations by radicals.

\term{Analysis}
Analysis studies limits, continuity, derivatives, integrals, and infinite processes. Its main branches include real analysis, complex analysis, and functional analysis. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} analysis supplies the mathematical foundations of physics, differential equations, numerical methods, probability, and signal processing.

\term{Analytic Continuation}
Analytic continuation extends an analytic function from one domain to a larger connected domain while preserving its power-series description wherever the two definitions overlap. The extension is unique when it exists. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} analytic continuation gives meaning to functions beyond their original convergence regions, including the zeta function in number theory and complex models in physics.

\term{Analytic Function}
An analytic function is locally equal to a convergent power series. In complex analysis, analytic functions are the same as holomorphic functions. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} their strong regularity makes them useful for contour integration, conformal mapping, fluid flow, and the solution of differential equations.

\term{Annihilator}
For a subspace $S$ of a vector space $V$, its annihilator $S^\circ$ is the set of linear functionals in $V^*$ that are zero on every vector in $S$. It records which measurements cannot detect anything in $S$. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} annihilators are used in duality, constrained optimization, control theory, and the study of linear equations.

\term{Annulus}
An annulus is the region between two concentric circles, $\{z:r<|z|<R\}$. Functions on an annulus are naturally represented by Laurent series, which may contain positive and negative powers. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} annuli model regions around holes and occur in complex analysis, potential flow, and two-dimensional field problems.

\term{Antiderivative}
An antiderivative of $f$ is a function $F$ with $F'=f$. Any two antiderivatives on an interval differ by a constant, so indefinite integration is written $\int f(x)\,dx=F(x)+C$. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} antiderivatives calculate accumulated quantities such as distance from velocity, work from force, and probability from a density.

\term{Antisymmetric Relation}
A relation $R$ is antisymmetric if $xRy$ and $yRx$ together imply $x=y$. The relation $\le$ is antisymmetric, while equality of distances is not generally an antisymmetric relation on points. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} antisymmetry is one of the defining properties of partial orders used in scheduling, optimization, databases, and dependency analysis.

\term{Approximation Error}
Approximation error is the difference between an exact value and a computed or simplified value. Truncation error comes from stopping an infinite process, while round-off error comes from finite numerical precision. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} tracking these errors is essential in numerical simulation, engineering design, scientific computing, and reliable data analysis.

\term{Archimedean Property}
An ordered field has the Archimedean property if, for any positive numbers $a$ and $b$, some natural number $n$ satisfies $na>b$. The real numbers have this property; non-Archimedean systems contain infinitesimal or infinitely large elements. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} the property supports the usual scale of measurement and is used in the foundations of limits and real-number computation.

\term{Argument Principle}
If $f$ is meromorphic and nonzero on a closed curve $\gamma$, then $\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_\gamma f'(z)/f(z)\,dz=N-P$, where $N$ and $P$ count zeros and poles inside the curve with multiplicity. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} it counts complex roots, analyzes stability of control systems, and supports the Nyquist criterion.

\term{Arrow's Impossibility Theorem}
Arrow's theorem states that, with at least three choices, no voting rule can satisfy unrestricted preferences, unanimity, independence of irrelevant alternatives, and non-dictatorship all at once. It does not say that voting is useless; it identifies unavoidable trade-offs. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} the theorem guides the design and interpretation of elections, ranking systems, and group decision procedures.

\term{Artinian Ring}
A ring is left Artinian if every descending chain of left ideals eventually stops. Artinian rings have strong finiteness properties; for example, a left Artinian ring is left Noetherian and its Jacobson radical is nilpotent. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} Artinian rings make many algebraic classification problems finite and appear in representation theory and the study of finite-dimensional algebras.

\term{Arzelà--Ascoli Theorem}
The Arzelà--Ascoli theorem gives conditions under which a family of continuous functions has a uniformly convergent subsequence. On a compact metric space, equicontinuity and pointwise boundedness are the key conditions. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} it is used to prove that approximate solutions of differential equations converge to an actual solution.

\term{Asymptote}
An asymptote is a line or curve that a function approaches as its input tends to a limit or infinity. For example, $y=0$ is a horizontal asymptote of $1/x$. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} asymptotes describe limiting behavior in calculus, model long-term trends, and help analyze rational functions and physical laws.

\term{Automorphic Form}
An automorphic form is a function on a geometric space that changes in a controlled way, or remains unchanged, under a discrete group of symmetries. Modular forms are an important special case. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} automorphic forms connect symmetry with number theory, Fourier expansions, cryptographic ideas, and the study of elliptic curves.

\term{Automorphism}
An automorphism is an isomorphism from a mathematical structure to itself. All automorphisms of one structure form a group under composition. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} automorphisms describe symmetries of graphs, equations, geometric objects, codes, and physical systems.

\term{Axiom}
An axiom is a starting rule accepted within a mathematical system. Theorems are then proved from the axioms using logic; different axiom choices can lead to different theories. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} explicit axioms clarify the assumptions behind geometry, set theory, probability, and formal verification.

\term{Axiom of Choice}
The axiom of choice says that, given any collection of nonempty sets, one can choose one element from each set, even when no explicit rule for choosing is known. It is independent of the other usual Zermelo--Fraenkel axioms. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} it supports results about bases, compactness, and infinite products, but it also leads to counterintuitive constructions such as non-measurable sets.

\term{Axiom Schema}
An axiom schema is a rule that produces many axioms by substituting different formulas into one pattern. In set theory, separation and replacement are standard examples. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} axiom schemas allow first-order formal systems to express broad mathematical principles while keeping each individual axiom finite.

\term{Choice Function}
For a family $\mathcal A$ of nonempty sets, a choice function selects one element from each set: $f(A)\in A$. The axiom of choice asserts that every such family has a choice function. \par\noindent\textbf{Applications:} choice functions appear in proofs involving bases, representatives of equivalence classes, optimization over infinite sets, and product constructions.
