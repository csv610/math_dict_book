\chapter*{M}
\label{chap:m}

\term{Maclaurin Series}
The Taylor series of a function at $x = 0$: $f(x) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{f^{(n)}(0)}{n!} x^n$.

\term{Manifold}
A topological space that locally resembles Euclidean space $\mathbb{R}^n$. For every point $p$, there exists a neighborhood $U$ and a homeomorphism $\varphi: U \to \mathbb{R}^n$. Manifolds can be smooth, Riemannian, complex, or symplectic.

\term{Metric Tensor}
A symmetric, non-degenerate $(0, 2)$-tensor field on a manifold that defines an inner product on each tangent space, allowing the measurement of lengths, angles, and volumes.

\term{Map}
A synonym for function; a rule associating to each element of one set an element of another.

\term{Markov Chain}
A stochastic process where the probability of transitioning to the next state depends only on the current state and not on the sequence of events that preceded it (the Markov property).

\term{Markov Chain Monte Carlo}
A class of algorithms that sample from a target distribution $\pi$ by simulating an ergodic Markov chain whose stationary distribution is $\pi$. Standard variants are the Metropolis--Hastings algorithm and Gibbs sampling; convergence is assessed through diagnostics applied to the simulated chain. MCMC is the dominant computational tool in Bayesian inference, where posterior distributions rarely admit closed forms.

\term{Markov Process}
A stochastic process in which the future evolution depends on the past only through the present state.

\term{Markov Property}
The property that the future state of a process depends only on its present state, not on its history. This "memoryless" property is the core of Markov processes.

\term{Martingale}
A sequence of random variables where the conditional expectation of the next value, given all prior values, is equal to the current value. It models a "fair game" in gambling.

\term{Martingale Convergence Theorem}
A submartingale that is bounded in $L^1$ converges almost surely to an integrable limit. For martingales, $L^1$-boundedness guarantees almost sure convergence, with convergence in $L^1$ under uniform integrability. It underlies the almost sure convergence of branching processes, reversed martingales, and many limit theorems in probability.

\term{Mathematics}
The abstract science of number, quantity, structure, space, and change, developed through rigorous logical deduction.

\term{Matroid}
A finite matroid is a ground set $E$ with a family of independent subsets satisfying the hereditary property and the exchange axiom. Bases, circuits, and the rank function give equivalent axiomatizations; duality and representability over fields are central notions. The greedy algorithm yields a maximum-weight basis precisely for matroids. Matroids axiomatize linear independence in vector spaces and cycle-free subgraphs in graphs, linking combinatorics with optimization and algebraic geometry.

\term{Matrix}
A rectangular array of numbers, symbols, or expressions arranged in rows and columns. Matrices are used to represent linear transformations and solve systems of linear equations.

\term{Matrix Multiplication}
The operation $(AB)_{ij} = \sum_k A_{ik} B_{kj}$, associative but generally not commutative.

\term{Matrix Norm}
A function $\|\cdot\|$ that assigns a non-negative real number to a matrix, measuring its "size" or "length." Common norms include the Frobenius norm and the operator norm (spectral norm).

\term{Maximal Ideal}
A proper ideal contained in no larger proper ideal; the quotient ring by a maximal ideal is a field.

\term{Maximum Flow Problem}
The problem of finding the maximum amount of flow that can be sent from a source node to a sink node in a network with capacity constraints on the edges. Solved by the Ford-Fulkerson algorithm.

\term{Maximum Likelihood Estimator (MLE)}
An estimator of a population parameter that maximizes the likelihood function, meaning it finds the parameter value that makes the observed data most probable.

\term{Maximum Modulus Principle}
If a holomorphic function is nonconstant, its modulus cannot have a local maximum at an interior point of its domain. If a maximum exists on the closure of a bounded domain, it occurs on the boundary. Applications: the principle gives bounds for analytic functions and helps prove uniqueness results such as the Schwarz lemma.

\term{Maximum Principle}
For harmonic or holomorphic functions, the property that a non-constant function attains no interior maximum.

\term{Mayer--Vietoris}
The long exact sequence relating the homology of a union $X = A \cup B$ to the homology of the pieces and their intersection: $\cdots \to H_n(A \cap B) \to H_n(A) \oplus H_n(B) \to H_n(X) \to H_{n-1}(A \cap B) \to \cdots$. Together with excision, the Mayer--Vietoris sequence is the fundamental computational tool for computing homology of spaces built from simpler pieces; it has analogous formulations for cohomology and for sheaves.

\term{Mean Value Theorem}
The theorem that for a differentiable $f$ on $[a,b]$ there exists $c$ with $f'(c) = \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}$.

\term{Measure}
A function $\mu$ that assigns a non-negative real number to subsets of a set, satisfying $\mu(\emptyset) = 0$ and countable additivity. Measure theory provides a rigorous foundation for integration (the Lebesgue integral).

\term{Measure Theory}
The study of measures and their associated integrals. It generalizes the notion of length, area, and volume to more abstract sets, allowing for the integration of highly discontinuous functions.

\term{Mellin Transform}
An integral transform that maps a function $f(x)$ to a complex function $F(s) = \int_0^\infty x^{s-1} f(x)\,dx$. It is closely related to the Laplace transform and appears in the study of zeta functions.

\term{Member}
An element of a set; the objects contained in a set.

\term{Menger's Theorem}
In a finite graph, the maximum number of internally vertex-disjoint paths between two non-adjacent vertices equals the minimum number of vertices needed to separate them. The edge version states the analogous equality for edge-disjoint paths and edge cuts. Both are special cases of the max-flow min-cut theorem, and Menger's theorem is often proved via flow arguments.

\term{Meromorphic Function}
A function that is holomorphic on a domain except at isolated poles.

\term{Method of Characteristics}
A technique for solving first-order quasilinear partial differential equations by reducing them to systems of ordinary differential equations along characteristic curves. The solution is transported along the characteristics, whose crossing can lead to shocks and the breakdown of smooth solutions. The method underlies the theory of the Hamilton--Jacobi equation and of hyperbolic conservation laws.

\term{Metric Space}
A set $X$ equipped with a distance function $d: X \times X \to [0, \infty)$ satisfying identity of indiscernibles, symmetry, and the triangle inequality. It provides a formal framework for the concepts of limit, continuity, and compactness.

\term{Microlocal Analysis}
The refined analysis of partial differential equations in phase space, combining spectral and geometric methods.

\term{Minimal Polynomial}
The unique monic polynomial $p(x)$ of smallest degree such that $p(A) = 0$ for a linear operator $A$. Every eigenvalue of $A$ is a root of its minimal polynomial.

\term{Minimal Spanning Tree}
In a weighted connected graph, a spanning tree with the minimum possible total edge weight. Examples include algorithms like Prim's and Kruskal's.

\term{Minkowski Space}
A four-dimensional Lorentzian manifold used in special relativity to model spacetime, with a metric signature $(-,+,+,+)$ or $(+,-,-,-)$. It preserves the spacetime interval between events.

\term{Minkowski Sum}
The set of all possible sums of vectors from two sets $A$ and $B$: $A \oplus B = \{a + b : a \in A, b \in B\}$. In geometry, it is used to create "offset" surfaces and in collision detection algorithms.

\term{Möbius Inversion Formula}
A result in number theory that allows one to recover a function $f(n)$ from its summatory function $g(n) = \sum_{d|n} f(d)$ using the Möbius function $\mu(n)$.

\term{Möbius Strip}
A non-orientable surface with only one side and one boundary component. It can be constructed by taking a strip of paper and joining the ends with a half-twist.

\term{Model Category}
A category with three distinguished classes of morphisms, the weak equivalences, cofibrations, and fibrations, satisfying the model axioms of Quillen. It provides an abstract framework for homotopy theory in which the localization of the category at the weak equivalences is the homotopy category. Model structures exist for topological spaces, simplicial sets, and chain complexes, and model categories formalize derived functors.

\term{Model (Logic)}
A structure that satisfies the axioms of a given formal theory. A statement is "true in a model" if the interpretation of the statement in that structure is true.

\term{Model Theory}
The branch of mathematical logic that studies structures and the first-order theories describing them. Central tools include the compactness theorem, the Löwenheim--Skolem theorems, elementary equivalence, saturated models, and type spaces. Morley's categoricity theorem and stability theory classify theories through the spectrum of their models. Model theory has deep applications in algebra, real and $p$-adic geometry, and analytic number theory.

\term{Modular Arithmetic}
A system of arithmetic for integers, where numbers "wrap around" upon reaching a certain value, the modulus. It is fundamental to number theory, cryptography (e.g., RSA), and computer science.

\term{Modular Form}
A modular form of weight $k$ is a holomorphic function $f$ on the upper half-plane satisfying $f((az+b)/(cz+d))=(cz+d)^k f(z)$ for matrices in the modular group, together with a suitable holomorphy condition at the cusps. Applications: Fourier coefficients of modular forms encode arithmetic information and appear in the study of elliptic curves and number theory.

\term{Modular Group}
The modular group is $\operatorname{PSL}(2,\mathbb{Z})=\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})/\{I,-I\}$. Here $\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})$ consists of the $2\times2$ integer matrices with determinant $1$, and the quotient identifies a matrix with its negative. It acts on the upper half-plane by $z\mapsto(az+b)/(cz+d)$. Applications: this symmetry is fundamental in modular forms, elliptic curves, and arithmetic geometry.

\term{Module}
A generalization of a vector space where the scalars come from a ring $R$ instead of a field. A left $R$-module $M$ is an abelian group equipped with a scalar multiplication $R \times M \to M$ satisfying specific axioms.

\term{Moduli Space}
A space, scheme, stack, or analytic space whose points parametrize isomorphism classes of geometric objects, such as algebraic curves, vector bundles, or varieties. A coarse moduli space may lack a universal family, whereas a fine moduli space carries one; stacks are needed when objects have nontrivial automorphisms. Deformation theory describes the local structure of moduli spaces. Examples include the moduli $\mathcal{M}_g$ of curves and the moduli of elliptic curves.

\term{Modulus of Continuity}
The function $\omega(\delta) = \sup_{|x-y|<\delta} |f(x)-f(y)|$ measuring the uniform continuity of $f$.

\term{Moment (Statistics)}
A moment is a numerical summary of a probability distribution. The raw $k$th moment is $\mathbb{E}[X^k]$, while the $k$th central moment is $\mathbb{E}[(X-\mu)^k]$. The second central moment is the variance; standardized third and fourth central moments describe skewness and kurtosis. Applications: moments summarize location, spread, asymmetry, and tail behavior in data analysis.

\term{Moment Generating Function}
A function $M_X(t) = \mathbb{E}[e^{tX}]$ that encodes all the moments of a random variable $X$. The $n$-th derivative of $M_X(t)$ at $t=0$ gives the $n$-th moment.

\term{Monad}
In category theory, a monad on a category $\mathcal{C}$ is an endofunctor $T$ with natural transformations $\eta: \mathrm{id} \to T$ (the unit) and $\mu: T^2 \to T$ (the multiplication) satisfying associativity and unit laws. Monads formalize algebraic theories and, in computer science, computational effects such as exceptions and state. Every adjunction induces a monad; the Eilenberg--Moore and Kleisli categories recover algebras and free algebras, respectively.

\term{Monoid}
A semigroup with an identity element. Equivalently, a set $S$ with an associative binary operation $\cdot$ and an element $e$ such that $e \cdot a = a \cdot e = a$ for all $a \in S$.

\term{Monoidal Category}
A category equipped with a bifunctor $\otimes: \mathcal{C} \times \mathcal{C} \to \mathcal{C}$ (the tensor product) and a unit object $I$, satisfying coherence conditions. They model structures like tensor products of vector spaces.

\term{Monomial}
An algebraic expression consisting of a product of variables raised to non-negative integer powers, optionally multiplied by a coefficient. A polynomial is a sum of monomials.

\term{Monomorphism}
In category theory, a morphism $f: X \to Y$ that is left-cancellable: $f \circ g = f \circ h \implies g = h$. In the category of sets, monomorphisms are exactly the injective functions.

\term{Monotone Convergence Theorem}
A fundamental result in analysis: if a sequence of real numbers is monotonically increasing and bounded above, it must converge to its supremum.

\term{Monotone Function}
A function that is either non-decreasing or non-increasing throughout its domain.

\term{Monty Hall Problem}
A probability puzzle based on a game show: after you pick one of three doors, the host opens a losing door and offers you the chance to switch. Switching wins with probability 2/3 and sticking wins with probability 1/3, contrary to the intuition that both are equal; the host's action conveys information about the winning door.

\term{Moore--Penrose Pseudoinverse}
A generalization of the inverse of a matrix to non-square matrices. For any matrix $A$, the pseudoinverse $A^+$ is the unique matrix satisfying four specific conditions, including $AA^+A = A$.

\term{Morphism}
A general arrow between two objects $X$ and $Y$ in a category. Depending on the category, a morphism could be a function, a linear map, a continuous map, or a group homomorphism.

\term{Morse Theory}
Morse theory studies a smooth manifold $M$ through a Morse function $f: M \to \mathbb{R}$ whose critical points are non-degenerate. The Morse lemma gives a normal form near each critical point, and $M$ admits a handle decomposition producing a CW structure; the Morse inequalities bound its homology in terms of the critical points of each index. Infinite-dimensional variants, including Morse--Witten and Floer theory, underlie modern symplectic and gauge theory.

\term{Motives}
Grothendieck's conjectural universal cohomology theory underlying all Weil cohomology theories. The category of pure motives is constructed from smooth projective varieties by adjoining correspondences as morphisms, and the Chow and Künneth standard conjectures concern its structure. Mixed motives extend the construction to arbitrary varieties, motivic cohomology provides the associated cohomology theory, and motives are central to the Langlands program.

\term{Multigrid Method}
A numerical method that accelerates iterative solvers by working on multiple grid resolutions.

\term{Multilinear Map}
A function of several vectors that is linear in each argument independently. The most common example is the determinant, which is a multilinear map of the columns of a matrix.

\term{Multinomial Distribution}
A generalization of the binomial distribution to a discrete random variable with $k$ possible outcomes, each with a fixed probability.

\term{Multiple Integral}
The integral of a function of two or more real variables over a multi-dimensional domain, such as a double integral over a region in $\mathbb{R}^2$ or a triple integral over a volume in $\mathbb{R}^3$.

\term{Multiplicative Function}
An arithmetic function $f$ such that $f(mn) = f(m)f(n)$ for any two coprime integers $m$ and $n$. Examples include the Euler totient function $\varphi(n)$ and the Möbius function $\mu(n)$.

\term{Multiplicative Inverse}
An element $a^{-1}$ such that $a \cdot a^{-1} = 1$. In a field, every non-zero element has a multiplicative inverse. In $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$, an element has an inverse iff it is coprime to $n$.

\term{Multiplicity}
The number of times a root is repeated in a polynomial, or the algebraic multiplicity of an eigenvalue.

\term{Multiset}
A generalization of a set where members can appear more than once. The number of times a member appears is called its multiplicity.

\term{Myhill--Nerode Theorem}
A result in theoretical computer science that characterizes regular languages in terms of the number of equivalence classes of a specific congruence relation on strings.
