\chapter*{Z}
\label{chap:z}

\term{Zappa--Szép Product}
A Zappa--Szép product combines two groups when each is allowed to act on the other. The result is a larger group that records both interactions at once. \par\noindent Applications: it builds complex symmetry groups from simpler ones and supports algebra and geometry.

\term{Zariski Closure}
The Zariski closure is the smallest polynomial-defined shape containing a chosen set of points. It adds every point forced by the polynomial relationships already present. \par\noindent Applications: it turns arbitrary data into an algebraic object and supports algebraic geometry and polynomial fitting.

\term{Zariski Topology}
The Zariski topology declares the solution sets of polynomial equations to be closed sets. Its open sets are usually large, so it captures algebraic relationships rather than ordinary distance. \par\noindent Applications: it provides the natural notion of nearness in algebraic geometry.

\term{Zeno's Paradoxes}
A set of puzzles about motion and infinity. Zeno argued that reaching a destination requires completing infinitely many smaller steps, raising the question of how motion can finish. \par\noindent Applications: the paradoxes motivate limits, infinite series, continuity, and the mathematical description of motion.

\term{Zero Divisor}
A zero divisor is a nonzero number in a ring that becomes zero when multiplied by another nonzero number. \par\noindent Applications: zero divisors reveal when multiplication loses information and help distinguish special rings from ordinary number systems.

\term{Zero-one Law}
A zero-one law says that as a random structure becomes very large, a chosen statement is almost surely either always true or always false; intermediate probabilities disappear. \par\noindent Applications: zero-one laws reveal regularity in random graphs, logic, probability, and large systems.

\term{Zero-point Energy}
Zero-point energy is the smallest energy a quantum system can have, even when it is in its lowest state. For a quantum oscillator, it is $E_0=\frac12\hbar\omega$. \par\noindent Applications: it affects quantum oscillators, molecular motion, fields, materials, and low-temperature physics.

\term{Zero-set}
The zero-set of a function is the collection of inputs where the function equals zero. \par\noindent Applications: zero-sets describe boundaries, level surfaces, equation solutions, and geometric shapes.

\term{Zeros of a Function}
The zeros of a function are the inputs where its output is zero. \par\noindent Applications: zeros find equation solutions, x-intercepts, resonances, equilibrium points, and roots of polynomials.

\term{Zero-sum Game}
In a zero-sum game, every gain by one player is exactly matched by a loss for another. The total payoff is always zero. \par\noindent Applications: zero-sum games model competition, bargaining, security, sports, and adversarial decision-making.

\term{Zeta Function (Riemann)}
The Riemann zeta function is an analytic function whose values encode information about the prime numbers. Its zeros are connected to how regularly primes are distributed. \par\noindent Applications: it supports number theory, prime-counting estimates, complex analysis, and the study of the Riemann hypothesis.

\term{Zigzag}
A zigzag is a path that repeatedly changes direction. \par\noindent Applications: zigzags model alternating steps, sawtooth signals, paths in graphs, and patterns in combinatorics.

\term{Z-module}
A Z-module is an abelian group viewed as a system where integer multiplication is allowed. Adding an element to itself is multiplication by a positive integer. \par\noindent Applications: Z-modules provide the algebraic language for integer lattices, abelian groups, and linear combinations with whole-number coefficients.

\term{Zonal Polynomial}
A zonal polynomial is a symmetric polynomial adapted to several variables and rotational symmetry. \par\noindent Applications: zonal polynomials occur in multivariable statistics, random matrices, and representation theory.

\term{Zonohedron}
A zonohedron is a convex solid whose faces are centrally symmetric. It can be built by adding line segments in different directions. \par\noindent Applications: zonohedra model feasible regions, crystals, optimization problems, and geometric sums.

\term{Zorn's Lemma}
Zorn's lemma says that if every totally ordered collection of objects can be extended upward, then at least one object is maximal. \par\noindent Applications: it proves the existence of bases, maximal ideals, and other objects that may not be constructible explicitly.

\term{Z-score}
A z-score tells how far a data value lies from the average, measured in standard deviations: $z=(x-\mu)/\sigma$. \par\noindent Applications: z-scores compare values from different scales and identify unusually high or low observations.

\term{Z-test}
A z-test checks whether an observed difference between averages is large enough to be unlikely if the populations really had the same average. \par\noindent Applications: it compares groups, tests measurements, and supports quality control and scientific studies.

\term{Z-transform}
The Z-transform changes a sequence of sampled values into a function that reveals its growth, decay, and oscillation. \par\noindent Applications: it analyzes digital filters, signals, control systems, and difference equations.

\term{Zygmund Class}
A function belongs to a Zygmund class when its second difference satisfies a bound such as $|f(x+h)+f(x-h)-2f(x)|\le C|h|$ for all relevant $x$ and $h$. This describes a form of smoothness close to, but slightly weaker than, Lipschitz continuity. \par\noindent Applications: Zygmund classes help analyze Fourier series, approximation error, and the behavior of functions near singularities.

\term{Zygmund's Theorem}
Zygmund's theorem gives conditions under which a function with controlled second differences has a well-behaved Fourier series. \par\noindent Applications: it supports Fourier analysis, approximation, signal reconstruction, and the study of function smoothness.
