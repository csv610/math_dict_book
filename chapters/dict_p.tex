\chapter*{P}
\label{chap:p}

\term{P-adic Number}
The $p$-adic numbers are the completion of the rationals using the distance $|x|_p=p^{-v_p(x)}$, which measures divisibility by a prime $p$ rather than ordinary size. Numbers are close when their difference is highly divisible by $p$. \par\noindent Applications: $p$-adic numbers support Diophantine equations, arithmetic geometry, local-to-global methods, and cryptography.

\term{P-adic Valuation}
The $p$-adic valuation $v_p(n)$ is the exponent of the highest power of $p$ dividing an integer, extended to rationals and with $v_p(0)=\infty$. It satisfies $v_p(x+y)\geq\min(v_p(x),v_p(y))$. \par\noindent Applications: valuations measure divisibility and support congruences, factorization, and $p$-adic analysis.

\term{Painter's Paradox}
Painter's paradox is the surprising fact that Gabriel's horn has finite volume but infinite surface area. It can be filled with a finite amount of paint, while coating every part of its ideal surface would require infinitely much. \par\noindent Applications: it illustrates the difference between area and volume and the care needed with infinite processes.

\term{Paley--Wiener}
The Paley--Wiener theorem links how quickly a complex function grows with how limited its frequency content is. A signal confined to a finite range of locations has a transform with controlled complex growth, and the reverse is also true. \par\noindent Applications: it supports Fourier analysis, sampling, signal processing, and the study of band-limited data.

\term{Palindromic Sequence}
A palindromic sequence reads the same from left to right and right to left. Palindromes can be studied through recursive construction, symmetry, and word combinatorics. \par\noindent Applications: they occur in string algorithms, coding, molecular sequence analysis, and pattern detection.

\term{Parabolic PDE}
A parabolic PDE has a time-like direction and diffusion-like behavior, as in the heat equation. It generally smooths irregular initial data as time evolves. \par\noindent Applications: parabolic PDEs model heat flow, diffusion, pricing, population spread, and image denoising.

\term{Paracompact}
A space is paracompact when any cover by open pieces can be replaced by another cover in which each point belongs to only finitely many pieces. This allows local descriptions to be combined smoothly. \par\noindent Applications: paracompactness supports manifolds, partitions of unity, differential geometry, and the gluing of local data.

\term{Paradox}
A paradox is an argument or situation that seems to lead to an impossible or self-contradictory conclusion. It often exposes a hidden assumption or a limit of ordinary intuition. \par\noindent Applications: paradoxes sharpen definitions and reveal issues in logic, infinity, probability, and the foundations of mathematics.

\term{Parallelepiped}
A parallelepiped is a solid generated by three vectors, with each pair of opposite faces parallel. Its volume is $V=|\mathbf a\cdot(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c)|$. \par\noindent Applications: parallelepipeds describe unit cells in crystals, coordinate changes, and volumes in vector calculus.

\term{Parallel Transport}
Parallel transport moves a vector along a curve while making its covariant derivative along the curve zero. On a curved manifold the result can depend on the path, revealing curvature. \par\noindent Applications: parallel transport is used in differential geometry, general relativity, robotics, and gauge theory.

\term{Parameter}
A parameter is a quantity that remains fixed during one calculation but can vary between models or cases. It can also label points on a parametric curve. \par\noindent Applications: parameters describe model uncertainty, geometric families, physical constants, and statistical coefficients.

\term{Parametric Equation}
A parametric equation describes coordinates as functions of a parameter, such as $x=\cos t$ and $y=\sin t$ for a circle. It can represent motion, orientation, and curves that are not graphs of a single-valued function. \par\noindent Applications: parametric equations are used in kinematics, computer graphics, geometry, and robotics.

\term{Parity}
The parity of an integer is whether it is even or odd. A function is even when $f(-x)=f(x)$ and odd when $f(-x)=-f(x)$. \par\noindent Applications: parity simplifies symmetry arguments, permutation signs, Fourier expansions, and selection rules in physics.

\term{Parrondo's Paradox}
Parrondo's paradox shows that two games, each unfavorable when played alone, can become favorable when alternated in the right pattern. The switching changes the player's state and therefore changes the later odds. \par\noindent Applications: it illustrates probability, random processes, alternating strategies, and ratchet effects in physics.

\term{Parseval's Identity}
Parseval's identity states that the squared norm of a function equals the sum of the squared magnitudes of its Fourier coefficients, with normalization depending on convention. It expresses conservation of energy under Fourier expansion. \par\noindent Applications: it supports signal power calculations, Fourier analysis, and error estimates for approximations.

\term{Partial Derivative}
A partial derivative differentiates a multivariable function with respect to one variable while holding the others fixed. It measures change along a coordinate direction. \par\noindent Applications: partial derivatives form gradients and Jacobians and are used in optimization, sensitivity analysis, thermodynamics, and PDEs.

\term{Partial Differential Equation}
A partial differential equation relates a quantity depending on several variables to its rates of change in those variables. Heat, waves, fluid flow, and vibrations are described by such equations. \par\noindent Applications: PDEs model weather, sound, heat, elasticity, fluid motion, electricity, and image processing.

\term{Partial Fraction}
A partial-fraction decomposition writes a rational function as a sum of simpler fractions associated with the factors of its denominator. \par\noindent Applications: it simplifies integration, inverse Laplace transforms, recurrence solutions, and symbolic algebra.

\term{Partial Order}
A partial order is a relation that is reflexive, antisymmetric, and transitive. It allows some elements to be incomparable, unlike a total order. \par\noindent Applications: partial orders represent dependencies, task schedules, lattices, version histories, and optimization constraints.

\term{Partial Sum}
A partial sum is $S_n=a_1+\cdots+a_n$, the sum of the first $n$ terms of a series. A series converges when its partial sums approach a finite limit. \par\noindent Applications: partial sums provide finite approximations and are used to study convergence and numerical error.

\term{Partition (Integer)}
An integer partition writes a whole number as a sum of positive whole numbers, without caring about order. For example, $4$ has the partitions $4$, $3+1$, $2+2$, $2+1+1$, and $1+1+1+1$. \par\noindent Applications: partitions count arrangements, resource allocations, musical rhythms, and combinatorial objects.

\term{Path-Connected Space}
A space is path-connected if every two points can be joined by a continuous path. Path-connectedness implies connectedness, although the converse can fail. \par\noindent Applications: it describes reachable configurations, connected regions, and continuous motions in topology and robotics.

\term{Path Integral}
A path integral describes a quantum process by combining contributions from every possible path between two events. Paths whose effects reinforce one another are most visible in the final result. \par\noindent Applications: path integrals support quantum mechanics, quantum field theory, statistical physics, and semiclassical approximations.

\term{Pattern}
A pattern is a structure that repeats or follows a recognizable rule. Mathematics studies patterns in numbers, shapes, words, graphs, and data. \par\noindent Applications: patterns support prediction, compression, design, combinatorics, and the discovery of regularity in data.

\term{Pearson Correlation Coefficient}
The Pearson correlation is $\rho_{XY}=\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)/(\sigma_X\sigma_Y)$ and lies between $-1$ and $1$. It measures linear association, not causation or general dependence. \par\noindent Applications: correlation summarizes data relationships and is used in regression, screening, and exploratory statistics.

\term{Perfect Field}
A perfect field is a number system in which polynomial roots do not acquire unwanted repetitions. All finite fields and the usual real and complex number systems are perfect. \par\noindent Applications: perfect fields simplify polynomial equations, field extensions, and algebraic geometry.

\term{Perfect Matching}
A perfect matching is a set of edges in which every vertex is incident to exactly one selected edge. It is also called a 1-factor. \par\noindent Applications: perfect matchings model pairings, assignment, scheduling, molecular bonding, and resource allocation.

\term{Perfect Number}
A perfect number equals the sum of its positive divisors other than itself. For example, $6=1+2+3$. \par\noindent Applications: perfect numbers illustrate divisor patterns and support research in number theory and computational mathematics.

\term{Permutation}
A permutation is a bijection from a set to itself. A finite set of $n$ elements has $n!$ permutations. \par\noindent Applications: permutations model rearrangements, schedules, symmetries, randomized algorithms, and group actions.

\term{Permutation Group}
A permutation group is a collection of rearrangements of a set that can be combined and undone. \par\noindent Applications: permutation groups describe symmetry, shuffling, puzzles, molecule structures, and the rearrangement of coordinates.

\term{Permutation Matrix}
A permutation matrix has exactly one $1$ in each row and column and zeros elsewhere. Multiplication by it reorders rows or columns without changing their values. \par\noindent Applications: permutation matrices represent reindexing, pivoting, graph relabeling, and sparse numerical algorithms.

\term{Perron--Frobenius Theorem}
The Perron--Frobenius theorem says that a matrix with nonnegative entries has a largest growth direction with nonnegative entries. Repeatedly applying the matrix tends to make this dominant direction visible. \par\noindent Applications: it studies population growth, search ranking, networks, Markov chains, and economic input-output models.

\term{Phase Space}
Phase space is the set of all possible states of a system. In classical mechanics, a state usually includes position and momentum, and evolution traces a path through phase space. \par\noindent Applications: phase space supports the study of planetary motion, gases, control systems, and statistical mechanics.

\term{Phi ($\phi$) (Golden Ratio)}
The golden ratio is $\phi=(1+\sqrt5)/2\approx1.618$. It has the special property that $\phi=1+1/\phi$, and it appears in pentagons and Fibonacci numbers. \par\noindent Applications: it appears in geometry, design, growth patterns, art, and the analysis of recursive sequences.

\term{Picard--Lindelöf Theorem}
The Picard--Lindelöf theorem says that an initial-value differential equation has one and only one nearby solution when its rule changes continuously and does not change too sharply with the unknown. \par\noindent Applications: it justifies predictions from differential equations and guarantees that a model has a well-defined local future.

\term{Picard Theorem}
Picard's theorem says that a nonconstant complex function takes almost every complex value. Near a severe singularity, it takes almost every value infinitely many times, however small the neighborhood. \par\noindent Applications: it describes the richness of complex functions and supports complex analysis and the study of singularities.

\term{Pigeonhole Principle}
The pigeonhole principle states that placing more than $m$ objects into $m$ containers forces some container to contain at least two objects. \par\noindent Applications: it proves existence results in combinatorics, number theory, hashing, scheduling, and collision analysis.

\term{Planar Graph}
A planar graph can be drawn on a flat surface without edges crossing except at shared endpoints. \par\noindent Applications: planar graphs model maps, printed circuits, road networks, layouts, and diagrams.

\term{Poincaré Conjecture}
The Poincaré conjecture, now a theorem, says that every closed three-dimensional space with no essential loops has the same topological shape as a three-sphere. \par\noindent Applications: it is a landmark in topology and guides the classification of three-dimensional spaces.

\term{Poincaré Disk Model}
The Poincaré disk model represents the whole hyperbolic plane inside an ordinary circular disk. Straightest paths appear as circular arcs that meet the boundary at right angles. \par\noindent Applications: it visualizes hyperbolic geometry, tilings, networks, and curved spaces.

\term{Poincaré Duality}
Poincaré duality says that on a closed, oriented $n$-dimensional space, information about $k$-dimensional holes matches information about $(n-k)$-dimensional holes. \par\noindent Applications: it links geometry and topology and simplifies calculations on surfaces and manifolds.

\term{Poincaré--Hopf Theorem}
The Poincaré--Hopf theorem says that the total turning behavior of a vector field at its isolated zero points equals the Euler characteristic of the whole space. \par\noindent Applications: it guarantees stationary points and connects vector fields, curvature, and topology.

\term{Poincaré Recurrence Theorem}
The Poincaré recurrence theorem says that a system with finite total size and preserved probability will, for almost every starting state, eventually return arbitrarily close to earlier states. \par\noindent Applications: it supports statistical mechanics, dynamical systems, and the study of long-term motion.

\term{Pointwise Convergence}
The sequence $f_n$ converges pointwise to $f$ on $S$ if $f_n(x)\to f(x)$ for each fixed $x\in S$. The rate may depend on $x$, so pointwise convergence is weaker than uniform convergence. \par\noindent Applications: it appears in approximation, probability, integration, and the study of function limits.

\term{Poisson Bracket}
The Poisson bracket combines two quantities in Hamiltonian mechanics to describe how their interaction changes over time. \par\noindent Applications: it models motion, conserved quantities, canonical coordinates, and the transition from classical to quantum mechanics.

\term{Poisson Distribution}
The Poisson distribution models the number of independent events in a fixed interval with mean rate $\lambda$: $P(X=k)=e^{-\lambda}\lambda^k/k!$. \par\noindent Applications: it models arrivals, defects, mutations, calls, and other count data.

\term{Poisson Equation}
The Poisson equation is $\Delta u=f$, where the source $f$ determines how the quantity $u$ bends or spreads. \par\noindent Applications: it models electric potential, gravity, pressure, steady heat, and fluid flow.

\term{Poisson Process}
A Poisson process $N(t)$ counts events whose independent increments have Poisson distributions proportional to interval length. Its waiting times are independent exponential variables. \par\noindent Applications: it models queues, radioactive decay, insurance claims, network traffic, and random arrivals.

\term{Poisson Summation Formula}
The Poisson summation formula says that adding a function at all integer points can equal adding its frequency transform at all integer frequencies. It connects a discrete view with a frequency view. \par\noindent Applications: it estimates sums, studies sampling, and supports Fourier analysis and number theory.

\term{Polar Coordinates}
Polar coordinates represent a planar point by radius $r$ and angle $\theta$, with $x=r\cos\theta$ and $y=r\sin\theta$. The area element is $r\,dr\,d\theta$. \par\noindent Applications: polar coordinates simplify circular geometry, wave equations, orbital motion, and double integrals.

\term{Polar Decomposition}
Polar decomposition writes a matrix as a rotation-like part times a stretching part, $A=UP$. It is the matrix version of writing a complex number as size times direction. \par\noindent Applications: it supports numerical linear algebra, data analysis, geometry, signal processing, and quantum mechanics.

\term{Polyhedron}
A polyhedron is a solid bounded by flat polygonal faces. A cube and a pyramid are familiar examples. \par\noindent Applications: polyhedra model buildings, crystals, 3D computer objects, geometric optimization, and physical structures.

\term{Polynomial}
A polynomial is a finite sum of terms involving coefficients, variables, and nonnegative integer powers. Its degree controls growth and algebraic complexity. \par\noindent Applications: polynomials model data, approximate functions, define algebraic varieties, and support symbolic and numerical computation.

\term{Polynomial Ring}
A polynomial ring is the collection of all polynomials whose coefficients come from a chosen number system or ring. \par\noindent Applications: polynomial rings provide the basic setting for equations, factorization, algebraic geometry, and symbolic computation.

\term{Polytope}
A polytope is a finite-dimensional generalization of a polygon and polyhedron. A convex polytope is the convex hull of finitely many points, or equivalently a bounded intersection of finitely many half-spaces. \par\noindent Applications: polytopes describe feasible regions in linear programming, geometry, combinatorics, and computer graphics.

\term{Pontryagin Duality}
Pontryagin duality says that a suitable commutative group can be recovered from the collection of its continuous wave-like characters. Taking the character collection twice returns the original group. \par\noindent Applications: it unifies Fourier analysis, harmonic analysis, signals, and locally compact groups.

\term{Population}
A population is the complete collection a statistical study wants to understand. A sample is selected from that population. \par\noindent Applications: populations define the scope of surveys, experiments, medical studies, and quality control.

\term{Positive-Definite Matrix}
A positive-definite matrix gives a positive result $x^TAx$ for every nonzero direction $x$. It behaves like a multidimensional upward-opening bowl. \par\noindent Applications: positive-definite matrices support optimization, distances, covariance, stability, and numerical computation.

\term{Power Series}
A power series represents a function as an infinite polynomial, $\sum a_n(x-c)^n$. It converges within a certain distance from its center. \par\noindent Applications: power series approximate functions and solve differential equations, probability problems, and physical models.

\term{Power Set}
The power set of $S$ is the collection of every subset of $S$, including the empty set and $S$ itself. \par\noindent Applications: power sets describe all possible selections, events in probability, and the foundations of set theory.

\term{Preconditioner}
A preconditioner is a simple transformation that makes a difficult linear system easier for a numerical algorithm to solve. \par\noindent Applications: preconditioners speed up simulations, engineering calculations, optimization, and scientific computing.

\term{Preconditioning}
Preconditioning changes a linear system into an equivalent but easier one before solving it. A good change reduces the number of steps needed by an iterative solver. \par\noindent Applications: it accelerates large simulations, fluid models, optimization, and finite-element calculations.

\term{Predicate Logic}
Predicate logic extends statements such as “$P$ is true” by allowing variables, relationships, and phrases such as “for every” and “there exists.” \par\noindent Applications: it expresses mathematics, databases, computer programs, automated reasoning, and formal proofs.

\term{Preimage}
The preimage of a set is the collection of inputs whose outputs land in that set. \par\noindent Applications: preimages describe solution sets, level regions, event probabilities, and how maps transfer constraints backward.

\term{Pre-order}
A pre-order is a relation that says an object can come before another, with every object before itself and the relation chaining consistently. Two different objects may still count as equivalent in both directions. \par\noindent Applications: pre-orders organize tasks, information, logic, approximation, and computer programs.

\term{Presheaf}
A presheaf assigns data to each region of a space and tells how to restrict that data to a smaller region. It records local information before requiring pieces to fit together globally. \par\noindent Applications: presheaves organize local functions, geometry, topology, and algebraic data.

\term{Primality Test}
A primality test decides whether a whole number has exactly two positive divisors, $1$ and itself. \par\noindent Applications: primality tests generate cryptographic keys, check number-theory calculations, and support fast computer arithmetic.

\term{Primal Problem}
The primal problem is the original optimization task: choose variables to minimize or maximize an objective subject to given restrictions. \par\noindent Applications: primal problems allocate resources, schedule work, design systems, and fit models.

\term{Prime Ideal}
A prime ideal is a collection of ring elements with the property that if a product belongs to it, at least one factor already belongs to it. It behaves like the prime numbers do under multiplication. \par\noindent Applications: prime ideals identify algebraic points and support factorization and algebraic geometry.

\term{Prime Number Theorem}
The prime number theorem says that primes become less frequent in a predictable way: the number of primes up to $x$ is approximately $x/\ln x$. \par\noindent Applications: it estimates prime counts, guides number theory, and supports algorithms involving large primes.

\term{Primitive Element Theorem}
The primitive element theorem says that many finite number-system extensions can be generated by adding just one carefully chosen element. \par\noindent Applications: it simplifies field extensions, polynomial equations, and Galois theory.

\term{Primitive Root}
A primitive root modulo $n$ is a number whose successive powers produce every nonzero remainder that has a multiplicative inverse modulo $n$. \par\noindent Applications: primitive roots support modular arithmetic, cyclic patterns, cryptography, and number-theoretic algorithms.

\term{Principal Component Analysis (PCA)}
Principal component analysis rotates data to find the directions containing the most variation. It can replace many correlated measurements by a smaller number of informative summaries. \par\noindent Applications: PCA supports visualization, compression, noise reduction, image analysis, and machine learning.

\term{Principal Fiber Bundle}
A principal fiber bundle is a space built from a base space and identical copies of a symmetry group attached above each point. The attachments may twist globally even when they look simple locally. \par\noindent Applications: principal bundles describe gauge fields, connections, curvature, and geometry.

\term{Principal Ideal}
A principal ideal is the collection of all multiples of one chosen ring element: $(a)=\{ra:r\in R\}$. \par\noindent Applications: principal ideals simplify divisibility, factorization, and calculations in rings.

\term{Principal Ideal Domain (PID)}
A principal ideal domain is a ring in which every ideal is generated by one element. The integers are the basic example. \par\noindent Applications: these domains make divisibility, factorization, and module calculations especially manageable.

\term{Principal Value (Cauchy)}al
The Cauchy principal value assigns a balanced value to some improper integrals by approaching a singular point equally from both sides. \par\noindent Applications: it is used in Fourier analysis, wave propagation, fluid flow, and physics.

\term{Probability Density Function}
A probability density function describes how likely values of a continuous random quantity are. The area under its graph over an interval gives the probability of landing there. \par\noindent Applications: densities model measurement error, heights, lifetimes, finance, and scientific uncertainty.

\term{Probability Space}
A probability space specifies all possible outcomes, which collections of outcomes count as events, and the probability assigned to each event. \par\noindent Applications: it provides the foundation for statistics, risk, experiments, random processes, and probability models.

\term{Product Measure}
A product measure assigns a size or probability to pairs of outcomes using the measures of their separate parts. For independent events, the combined probability is the product of the individual probabilities. \par\noindent Applications: product measures model several random variables, regions in higher dimensions, and repeated experiments.

\term{Product Space}
A product space combines several spaces by forming every possible tuple of one element from each. \par\noindent Applications: product spaces describe multi-variable states, positions, parameters, experiments, and systems with several components.

\term{Product Topology}
The product topology defines nearness in a tuple by checking only finitely many coordinates at a time. It is the weakest topology that makes each coordinate-reading map continuous. \par\noindent Applications: it gives a natural notion of convergence in spaces of tuples and functions.

\term{Projection Operator}
A projection operator sends every vector to its component in a chosen subspace and leaves that component unchanged. Applying the projection twice has the same effect as applying it once. \par\noindent Applications: projections extract data components, fit least-squares models, and split vector spaces.

\term{Projective Geometry}
Projective geometry studies properties that remain visible under perspective changes. Parallel lines are allowed to meet at points at infinity. \par\noindent Applications: it supports perspective drawing, cameras, computer vision, geometry, and algebraic curves.

\term{Projective Module}
A projective module behaves like a part of a free module, so equations involving it can be lifted and solved without unexpected obstruction. \par\noindent Applications: projective modules generalize vector spaces and describe vector bundles, algebraic geometry, and homological calculations.

\term{Projective Scheme}
A projective scheme is an algebraic shape that can be described inside projective space by homogeneous polynomial equations. \par\noindent Applications: projective schemes provide a setting for complete algebraic shapes, families of solutions, and algebraic geometry.

\term{Projective Space}
Projective space is the collection of all directions through the origin. Vectors that differ only by nonzero scaling represent the same point. \par\noindent Applications: it describes perspective, points at infinity, camera images, and algebraic geometry.

\term{Projective Variety}
A projective variety is the set of projective points that satisfy a collection of homogeneous polynomial equations. \par\noindent Applications: projective varieties describe algebraic shapes, curves, surfaces, and families of polynomial solutions.

\term{Proper Map}
A proper map is a map for which bounded, closed pieces of the target pull back to bounded, closed pieces of the source. It prevents relevant parts of the source from escaping to infinity. \par\noindent Applications: proper maps support compactness, geometry, optimization, and the study of continuous solutions.

\term{Proportionality}
Two quantities are proportional when one is always a fixed multiple of the other: $y=kx$. \par\noindent Applications: proportionality models scale, cost, distance, speed, mixtures, and physical laws.

\term{Proximal Operator}
A proximal operator replaces a point by a nearby point that balances closeness with a desired objective. \par\noindent Applications: it solves constrained and nonsmooth optimization problems, including sparse-data and machine-learning models.

\term{Pullback}
A pullback brings a quantity on the target of a map back to the source by substituting the map into it. \par\noindent Applications: pullbacks transfer functions, measurements, differential forms, and geometric structures to new coordinates.

\term{Purely Imaginary}
A purely imaginary number has the form $bi$, with no real part. \par\noindent Applications: purely imaginary numbers describe rotations, waves, oscillations, and complex solutions of equations.

\term{Pushforward}
A pushforward carries a quantity along a map from the source to the target. For a smooth map, it carries tangent directions forward. \par\noindent Applications: pushforwards transfer vectors, measures, probabilities, and geometric data.

\term{Pushout}
A pushout is a way to glue two objects together along a shared part. For maps $B\leftarrow A\to C$, it joins $B$ and $C$ while identifying the two images of each element of $A$. \par\noindent Applications: pushouts build joined spaces, quotient objects, cell complexes, and constructions in algebraic topology.

\term{P vs NP}
The P versus NP problem asks whether every problem whose proposed solution can be checked in polynomial time can also be solved in polynomial time. Since $P\subseteq NP$, the question is whether this inclusion is strict. \par\noindent Applications: the problem affects optimization, scheduling, automated reasoning, cryptography, and the limits of efficient computation.
